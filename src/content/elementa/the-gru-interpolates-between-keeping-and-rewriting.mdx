## One state, two gates

The gated recurrent unit, or GRU, was introduced by Cho and colleagues in 2014
for an encoder–decoder translation model. It drops the separate cell state and
the output gate. In the form PyTorch documents,

<Eq id="I.10.7" gloss="PyTorch's convention: z near one keeps the old state, and the reset gate acts after the recurrent matrix.">

$$
\begin{aligned}
\vec r_t&=\sigma(\mat U_r\vec x_t+\mat W_r\vec h_{t-1}+\vec b_r),\\
\vec z_t&=\sigma(\mat U_z\vec x_t+\mat W_z\vec h_{t-1}+\vec b_z),\\
\vec n_t&=\tanh\big(\mat U_n\vec x_t+\vec b_n
 +\vec r_t\odot(\mat W_n\vec h_{t-1}+\vec b_{hn})\big),\\
\vec h_t&=(1-\vec z_t)\odot\vec n_t+\vec z_t\odot\vec h_{t-1}.
\end{aligned}
$$

</Eq>

The candidate $\vec n_t$ plays the part of the LSTM's $\tilde{\vec c}_t$. The
update gate $\vec z_t$ splits each coordinate between the old state and the
candidate. The reset gate $\vec r_t$ decides how much of the old state the
candidate may read: with $\vec r_t$ near zero, the candidate is computed almost
as if the sequence had just begun.

Hold the gates and candidate fixed. The direct derivative is
$\partial\vec h_t/\partial\vec h_{t-1}=\operatorname{diag}(\vec z_t)$, the GRU's
carry line. It is the LSTM's carry line with two constraints. The amount
written is tied to the amount kept: $1-\vec z_t$ plays the input gate and
$\vec z_t$ the forget gate. Greff and colleagues tested this coupling as one
of their LSTM variants. And there is no output gate, so the carried state is
the visible state.

That second constraint bounds the state. Each coordinate of $\vec h_t$ is a
convex combination of the old value and a candidate in $(-1,1)$, so
$|h_t|\leq\max(|h_{t-1}|,|n_t|)$. A state that starts in $[-1,1]$ stays there.
That is the GRU half of I.10.T3, proved in
[I.10.X07](/elementa/neural-networks/gating/problems/x07). The bound concerns
values; the full derivative has other paths, through $\mat W_r$, $\mat W_z$ and
$\mat W_n$, exactly as in (I.10.4).

## Read the equation, not the letter

Cho and colleagues wrote $h_t=z\,h_{t-1}+(1-z)\,\tilde h_t$, which is PyTorch's
convention. Chung and colleagues, comparing gated units later the same year,
wrote $h_t=(1-z)\,h_{t-1}+z\,\tilde h_t$. In one, $z$ near one keeps; in the
other, it overwrites.

The two describe the same family of functions. Since
$1-\sigma(a)=\sigma(-a)$, negating the update gate's weights and bias turns
one convention into the other exactly. Weights trained under one convention
and loaded under the other, without that conversion, do something else: with
$z=0.9$ for ten steps, one reading keeps $0.348678$ of the old state and the
other $10^{-10}$
([I.10.X06](/elementa/neural-networks/gating/problems/x06)).
A diagram labelled “update gate” does not say which. The equation does.

## Where the reset gate acts

The original paper applies the reset gate before the recurrent matrix:
$\tilde{\vec h}_t=\tanh\big(\mat U\vec x_t+\mat W(\vec r_t\odot\vec h_{t-1})\big)$.
PyTorch applies it after, as in (I.10.7). Keras offers both through
`reset_after`, whose default is the after form, described as cuDNN compatible.

The two candidates are $\mat W\operatorname{diag}(\vec r)\vec h$ and
$\operatorname{diag}(\vec r)\mat W\vec h$. They agree for every $\vec h$ exactly
when $\mat W$ commutes with $\operatorname{diag}(\vec r)$. Entry by entry,
that means $W_{jk}(r_k-r_j)=0$: every weight connecting two coordinates with
different reset values must be zero. A uniform reset satisfies the condition
for any $\mat W$, and a diagonal $\mat W$ satisfies it for any reset. Otherwise
the functions differ.
[I.10.B04](/elementa/neural-networks/gating/problems/b04) gives a two-unit case
where the same weights, state and gates produce mirror-image candidates.

The after form has a practical reason. The product $\mat W_n\vec h_{t-1}$ no
longer waits for $\vec r_t$, so all three recurrent products can be computed as
one $3H$ by $H$ matrix multiplication. It also has a cost in the count: the
recurrent bias $\vec b_{hn}$ sits inside the reset and cannot be merged with
$\vec b_n$, which adds $H$ parameters.

## What the parts cost

Count with one bias per gate, the convention of (I.9.1) and (I.10.1):

<Eq id="I.10.8" gloss="Each gated block repeats the cost of a plain recurrent layer.">

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{tanh RNN}&: H(D+H+1),\\
\text{GRU}&: 3H(D+H+1),\ \text{plus }H\text{ in the reset-after form},\\
\text{LSTM}&: 4H(D+H+1).
\end{aligned}
$$

</Eq>

For $D=3$ and $H=5$ these are $45$, $135$ or $140$, and $180$. PyTorch keeps two
bias vectors per block, giving $50$, $150$ and $200$. The multiply–accumulate
count per step scales the same way, $4H(D+H)$ for the LSTM against $H(D+H)$
for the plain cell.

Equal width is therefore not an equal comparison. At $D=3$ a GRU of width six
has $3\cdot6\cdot10=180$ parameters, exactly the LSTM's at width five.
Backpropagation through time also stores more per step: every gate value is
needed again in (I.10.6).
[I.10.B05](/elementa/neural-networks/gating/problems/b05) audits shapes, counts
and storage, and
[I.10.X10](/elementa/neural-networks/gating/problems/x10) scales the storage to
a realistic batch.

## Which one to use

The evidence does not crown either. Chung and colleagues found both gated
units better than a tanh RNN on their polyphonic music and speech-signal
tasks, and the GRU comparable to the LSTM. Jozefowicz, Zaremba and Sutskever
evaluated over ten thousand architectures. They found one that beat both on
some tasks but not all, and found that a forget bias of one closed the LSTM's
gap to the GRU. Greff and colleagues compared eight LSTM variants and found
none significantly better than the standard cell; the forget gate and the
output activation were its most critical parts.

The defensible procedure is a measured one. Compare at matched parameters or
matched compute, with the forget bias set deliberately, over several seeds,
on a validation split the comparison did not tune. Choose the GRU when its
smaller cost matters and it holds its own; choose neither by reputation.

## The same idea across depth

The GRU update is an interpolation between keeping a vector and replacing it.
Nothing in that idea needs time. Srivastava, Greff and Schmidhuber applied it
across layers:

$$
\vec y=\vec g(\vec x)\odot F(\vec x)+(1-\vec g(\vec x))\odot\vec x,
$$

where $F$ is a layer and $\vec g$ a learned gate. With the gate near zero, a
highway layer passes its input through, and its Jacobian is close to the
identity. A residual connection, $\vec y=\vec x+F(\vec x)$, drops the gate and
keeps the identity path unconditionally: its Jacobian is
$\mat I+\partial F/\partial\vec x$.

The question this chapter answered for time is the general one. When a deep
computation trains badly, look for a path from the loss back to the early
parameters along which the derivative is near the identity. Gating, highway
layers and residual connections are three answers. The transformer takes the
third.

## Reproduce the reset counterexample

~~~python
from math import tanh

W = [[0.0, 1.0], [1.0, 0.0]]
h, r, z = [0.5, -0.5], [0.75, 0.25], 0.5

def matvec(m, v):
    return [sum(a*b for a, b in zip(row, v)) for row in m]

before = [tanh(a) for a in matvec(W, [ri*hi for ri, hi in zip(r, h)])]
after = [tanh(ri*a) for ri, a in zip(r, matvec(W, h))]
for n in (before, after):
    new = [(1 - z)*nj + z*hj for nj, hj in zip(n, h)]
    print(" ".join(f"{v:.6f}" for v in n), "|",
          " ".join(f"{v:.6f}" for v in new))
~~~

The first line is the reset-before candidate and new state,
$(-0.124353,0.358357)$ and $(0.187823,-0.070821)$. The second is the
reset-after pair, $(-0.358357,0.124353)$ and $(0.070821,-0.187823)$.
