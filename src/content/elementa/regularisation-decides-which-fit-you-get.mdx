## The training loss cannot choose for you

Two predictors can agree on every training example and disagree on the next
one. An optimiser sees the agreement. It has no evidence about the disagreement
until we supply more data or a preference.

The figure is a schematic of that choice, not a theorem that the smoother
curve wins. If the true function oscillates, suppressing its oscillations costs
accuracy. Regularisation is useful precisely when its preference suits the
problem.

An explicit regulariser changes the quantity being minimised:

<Eq id="I.8.5" gloss="The fit and the preference are separate terms.">

$$
\mathcal{J}(\vec w)=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{data}}(\vec w)
  +\frac{\lambda}{2}\|\vec w\|_2^2,\qquad \lambda\geq0.
$$

</Eq>

Here $\vec w$ contains the weights we choose to penalise, not automatically
every parameter in the model. Biases and normalisation scales are separate
choices. A soft penalty does not remove functions from the hypothesis class;
it makes some of them more expensive.

## L2 and weight decay coincide in one particular update

Differentiating the penalty in (I.8.5) gives $\lambda\vec w$. Plain gradient
descent therefore takes the step

<Eq id="I.8.6" gloss="The same plain-gradient step can be read as a gradient plus shrinkage.">

$$
\vec w_{t+1}
=\vec w_t-\eta(\vec g_t+\lambda\vec w_t)
=(1-\eta\lambda)\vec w_t-\eta\vec g_t.
$$

</Eq>

For $0\leq\eta\lambda\leq1$, the first term shrinks the old weights without
reversing their sign. This is the algebra behind the familiar equivalence
between an L2 penalty and weight decay.

Keep the qualifier: **plain gradient descent**. With Adam, adding
$\lambda\vec w$ to the gradient changes the first and second moments. AdamW
forms those moments from the data gradient and applies the shrinkage separately:

$$
\vec w_{t+1}=(1-\eta_t\lambda)\vec w_t
  -\eta_t\frac{\hat{\vec m}_t}{\sqrt{\hat{\vec v}_t}+\varepsilon}.
$$

The operations do not commute. Even momentum without adaptive scaling can
break the equivalence when the penalty enters its accumulator but decoupled
decay does not. The comparison in (I.8.6) is not a licence to copy a coefficient
between arbitrary optimiser implementations.

Small weights also do not guarantee a smooth predictor independently of
architecture. In a multilayer network, sensitivities involve products of
weights and activation derivatives. A rescaling of one layer may be cancelled
by another. The penalty is a preference in a chosen parameterisation.

## A case where the preference can be solved exactly

For a linear model, take
$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{data}}=\|\mat X\vec w-\vec y\|_2^2/(2n)$.
Setting the derivative of (I.8.5) to zero gives

<Eq id="I.8.7" gloss="Positive lambda supplies curvature in directions the data do not identify.">

$$
\left(\frac{\mat X^{\mathsf T}\mat X}{n}+\lambda\mat I\right)
\vec w_{\mathrm{ridge}}
=\frac{\mat X^{\mathsf T}\vec y}{n}.
$$

</Eq>

For any nonzero $\vec u$, the quadratic form of the matrix on the left is
$\|\mat X\vec u\|_2^2/n+\lambda\|\vec u\|_2^2>0$ when $\lambda>0$.
It is invertible even when the columns of $\mat X$ are dependent.

In an eigendirection with curvature $a>0$ and right-hand component $b$,
least squares gives $b/a$ while ridge gives $b/(a+\lambda)$.
The ratio is $a/(a+\lambda)$. Weakly identified directions shrink most.
That is the whole mechanism, including its cost: a genuine component in a
weak direction is shrunk too.

Equation (I.8.7) uses an averaged data loss. Replacing it with a summed loss
while leaving $\lambda$ unchanged makes the penalty relatively weaker as the
dataset grows.

## Dropout changes the training problem by adding noise

Let $h$ be a fixed activation and let $M$ be an independent Bernoulli mask,
equal to one with keep probability $q\in(0,1]$. Inverted dropout uses

<Eq id="I.8.8" gloss="Surviving activations are enlarged to compensate for the missing ones.">

$$
\widetilde h=\frac{Mh}{q},\qquad
\mathbb E[\widetilde h\mid h]=h,\qquad
\operatorname{Var}(\widetilde h\mid h)=\frac{1-q}{q}h^2.
$$

</Eq>

There are only two outcomes: zero with probability $1-q$, and $h/q$ with
probability $q$. Multiplying each by its probability proves the mean in
(I.8.8); doing the same for the squares gives
$\mathbb E[\widetilde h^2\mid h]=h^2/q$, from which the variance follows.

Training minimises an expected loss over masks as well as examples.
This discourages a representation from depending too heavily on a particular
activation being present. At inference inverted dropout is the identity.
There is no second multiplication by $q$.

Preserving the mean is a smaller promise than reproducing the masked
networks' average prediction. If a later operation squares the activation,
the two results differ by the variance term above. The exact counterexample
is [I.8.B05](/elementa/neural-networks/normalisation/problems/b05).

## Stopping is also a preference

On one quadratic eigendirection,
$\mathcal L(w)=aw^2/2-bw+\text{constant}$, start at zero and apply gradient
descent. The recurrence $w_{t+1}=(1-\eta a)w_t+\eta b$ sums to

<Eq id="I.8.9" gloss="Finite training time acts as a direction-dependent filter.">

$$
w_t=\left[1-(1-\eta a)^t\right]\frac{b}{a},
\qquad a>0,\quad w_0=0.
$$

</Eq>

For $0<\eta a\leq1$, the bracket grows from zero towards one. Directions
with smaller curvature grow more slowly. Stopping early therefore suppresses
them, much as ridge does, but its filter in (I.8.9) is not ridge's
$a/(a+\lambda)$. A single $\lambda$ generally cannot match all directions.
For a non-convex network this calculation is an analogy with a specified
boundary, not an identity.

Choose the checkpoint on validation data. Keep the test set for the final
evaluation. Looking at test loss every epoch and selecting its minimum is
training a selection rule on the test set, even if no gradient passes through it.

## Augmentation spends domain knowledge

A label-preserving transformation states something stronger than a generic
penalty: these two inputs ought to receive the same target. The statement can
be wrong. Rotating a digit can change which digit it is; cropping an image can
remove the object whose class was retained.

Neither the regularisation strength nor the augmentation policy comes with
a guarantee. Compare them on held-out data, keep the evaluation protocol
fixed, and report the preference that was actually tested.
