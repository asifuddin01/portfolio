## Frequencies on a graph

On a grid, convolution is multiplication in the Fourier basis: each
frequency is scaled by the kernel's response. A graph has no sines and
cosines, but it has a matrix that plays their role. Let $\mat D$ be the
diagonal matrix of degrees and $\mat L=\mat D-\mat A$ the graph Laplacian.
Since $\vec x^{\mathsf T}\mat D\vec x=\sum_vd_vx_v^2$ counts each edge's two
endpoints once each, and $\vec x^{\mathsf T}\mat A\vec x$ counts each edge
twice,

<Eq id="I.14.4" gloss="The Laplacian's eigenvectors are a graph's frequencies, and a filter reweights them.">

$$
\begin{aligned}
\vec x^{\mathsf T}\mat L\vec x
 &=\sum_{(u,v)\in E}(x_u-x_v)^2,\\
\mat L&=\mat U\mat\Lambda\mat U^{\mathsf T},\\
g(\mat L)\,\vec x&=\mat U\,g(\mat\Lambda)\,\mat U^{\mathsf T}\vec x.
\end{aligned}
$$

</Eq>

The first line measures how much a signal varies across edges. The
Laplacian is symmetric, so it has an orthonormal eigenbasis
(<ApparatusRef ref="0.LA.04" />). An eigenvector with a small eigenvalue
varies little across edges: it is a low frequency. On a cycle the
eigenvectors are exactly the discrete sines and cosines. The third line is
a spectral filter: transform into the eigenbasis, scale each frequency by
$g$, and transform back.

Bruna and colleagues (2014) learned $g(\mat\Lambda)$ directly. That needs
the eigendecomposition, which costs $O(n^3)$; its filters are not local;
and a filter learned on one graph means nothing on another, whose
eigenvectors differ. Defferrard, Bresson and Vandergheynst (2016) made $g$
a polynomial of degree $K$. A polynomial in $\mat L$ needs no
eigenvectors, and by I.14.T2 it reaches exactly $K$ hops.

## The first-order filter, renormalised

Kipf and Welling (2017) kept the polynomial at degree one and tied its two
coefficients, giving $\theta(\mat I+\mat D^{-1/2}\mat A\mat D^{-1/2})$.
That matrix has eigenvalues in $[0,2]$, so stacking it can amplify a
signal without bound. Their repair, which they called the renormalisation
trick, adds a self-loop to every node first:

<Eq id="I.14.5" gloss="A graph convolution averages each node with its neighbours, weighted by degree.">

$$
\begin{aligned}
\hat{\mat A}&=\tilde{\mat D}^{-1/2}(\mat A+\mat I)\,\tilde{\mat D}^{-1/2},\\
\mat H'&=\sigma\big(\hat{\mat A}\mat H\mat W\big).
\end{aligned}
$$

</Eq>

Here $\tilde{\mat D}$ holds the degrees counted with the self-loop, so
$\hat A_{vu}=1/\sqrt{\tilde d_v\tilde d_u}$ for every neighbour $u$ of $v$
and for $u=v$. The eigenvalues of $\hat{\mat A}$ lie in $(-1,1]$ for a
connected graph, a fact proposition 3 uses. The layer is a fixed average
over each node's closed neighbourhood followed by one weight matrix
shared by every node: the convolution of I.11 with the kernel replaced by
a rule.

On the paw graph the degrees with self-loops are $3$, $3$, $4$ and $2$. Node
$1$'s row of $\hat{\mat A}$ is $(\tfrac13,\tfrac13,\tfrac1{\sqrt{12}},0)$, and
for $\vec x=(1,2,3,4)$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\hat{\mat A}\vec x=(&1.866025,\;1.866025,\\
 &3.030239,\;3.060660).
\end{aligned}
$$

## What the average cannot see

The coefficients of (I.14.5) depend on the degrees at the two ends of an
edge and on nothing else. A node therefore cannot tell one neighbour from
another, except through their states. The figure sets this against I.11.
On a line, a kernel of width three has three weights, $w_{-1}$, $w_0$ and
$w_{+1}$, and can weigh the left neighbour differently from the right.

On a cycle the difference is exact. There every degree with self-loop is
$3$, and $\hat{\mat A}$ averages each node with its two neighbours by
$\tfrac13$ each. Its eigenvalues are $(1+2\cos\theta)/3$ for the cycle's
frequencies $\theta$, a low-pass filter. A difference filter, which returns
$x_{i+1}-x_{i-1}$, is an antisymmetric matrix. Every polynomial in the
symmetric $\hat{\mat A}$ is symmetric, so no stack of linear graph
convolutions computes a directional difference
([I.14.X04](/elementa/neural-networks/gnn/problems/x04)). Equivariance was
bought by forgetting which neighbour is which.

The rule has a second consequence. Nodes $1$ and $2$ of the paw have the
same closed neighbourhood, $\{1,2,3\}$, and the same degree, so their rows
of $\hat{\mat A}$ are identical. One graph convolution gives both the
value $1.866025$, although their features are $1$ and $2$: the difference
between them is erased. The sum layer of (I.14.3) keeps them apart,
because its self-term $\mat H\mat W_0$ has its own weights.

Attention gives the coefficients more freedom. In the graph attention
network of Veličković and colleagues (2018) each edge's weight is
computed from the two nodes' states, so the average can favour some
neighbours. It is still a function of the states and not of an ordering,
so it still cannot invent a direction the features do not carry.

## What it costs

Each layer multiplies $\hat{\mat A}$ by an $n\times d$ matrix and then by a
$d\times d$ weight matrix:

<Eq id="I.14.9" gloss="A sparse graph is cheap only if its sparsity is used.">

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{edge list:}&\quad O(md+nd^2),\\
\text{dense }\hat{\mat A}:&\quad O(n^2d+nd^2).
\end{aligned}
$$

</Eq>

For a million nodes, ten million edges and width $128$, the dense product
alone is $1.28\times10^{14}$ multiply-accumulates and the matrix occupies
$4\times10^{12}$ bytes. With an edge list the whole layer is about
$1.9\times10^{10}$ operations
([I.14.B04](/elementa/neural-networks/gnn/problems/b04)). When even that is
too much, Hamilton, Ying and Leskovec (2017) sample a fixed number of
neighbours per node and layer, trading an exact average for an unbiased
estimate of it ([I.14.X09](/elementa/neural-networks/gnn/problems/x09)).

## Build the matrix

~~~python
from math import sqrt
A = [[0, 1, 1, 0], [1, 0, 1, 0], [1, 1, 0, 1], [0, 0, 1, 0]]   # the paw graph
d = [sum(row) + 1 for row in A]                              # degrees with self-loops
Ahat = [[(A[i][j] + (i == j))/sqrt(d[i]*d[j]) for j in range(4)] for i in range(4)]
x = [1.0, 2.0, 3.0, 4.0]
print(d)
print([round(v, 6) for v in Ahat[0]])
print([round(sum(a*b for a, b in zip(row, x)), 6) for row in Ahat])
~~~

It prints the degrees <code>[3, 3, 4, 2]</code>, node 1's row
<code>[0.333333, 0.333333, 0.288675, 0.0]</code>, and the convolved signal
<code>[1.866025, 1.866025, 3.030239, 3.06066]</code>, with the first two
entries equal.
