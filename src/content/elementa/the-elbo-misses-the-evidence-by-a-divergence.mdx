## Two derivations of one bound

Let $q(\vec z\mid\vec x)$ be any distribution over codes that is positive
exactly where the posterior is; for Gaussian priors and decoders that is
everywhere. Multiply and divide by it inside the evidence,
then apply Jensen's inequality to the concave logarithm
(<ApparatusRef ref="0.IT.05" />):

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\log p(\vec x)\\
&\;\;=\log\E_{q}\Big[\frac{p(\vec x,\vec z)}{q(\vec z\mid\vec x)}\Big]\\
&\;\;\geq\E_{q}\big[\log p(\vec x,\vec z)\\
&\;\;\qquad-\log q(\vec z\mid\vec x)\big].
\end{aligned}
$$

The right-hand side is the evidence lower bound, the ELBO. Jensen's
inequality is an equality only when the ratio inside is the same for every
$\vec z$, that is, when $q$ is proportional to $p(\vec x,\vec z)$, which
makes it the posterior.

The second derivation gives the size of the gap. Since $\log p(\vec x)$
does not depend on $\vec z$, it equals its own average under $q$. Write it
as $\log p(\vec x,\vec z)-\log p(\vec z\mid\vec x)$ inside that average, and
add and subtract $\log q$:

<Eq id="I.13.4" gloss="The bound misses the evidence by the divergence to the posterior.">

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\log p(\vec x)=\operatorname{ELBO}(q)\\
&\;\;+\KL\big(q(\vec z\mid\vec x)\,\|\,p(\vec z\mid\vec x)\big),\\[6pt]
&\operatorname{ELBO}(q)=\E_{q}\big[\log p(\vec x\mid\vec z)\big]\\
&\;\;-\KL\big(q(\vec z\mid\vec x)\,\|\,p(\vec z)\big).
\end{aligned}
$$

</Eq>

The second line splits $\log p(\vec x,\vec z)$ into
$\log p(\vec x\mid\vec z)+\log p(\vec z)$. Because a KL divergence is never
negative (<ApparatusRef ref="0.IT.03" />), the first line is the bound, and
it names the gap exactly. This is I.13.T1. With the model held fixed, the
evidence is a constant, so raising the ELBO over $q$ and lowering the KL
divergence from $q$ to the posterior are the same act.

## Reading the two terms

The first term of the ELBO is a reconstruction term: the decoder's
log-likelihood of $\vec x$, averaged over codes drawn from the encoder.
With a Gaussian decoder of variance $\sigma^2$ it is
$-\|\vec x-\hat{\vec x}\|^2/(2\sigma^2)$ plus a constant, the loss of I.12
with the code made random.

The second term is the price of the code: how far the encoder's
distribution sits from the prior. It is what I.12 lacked. It pulls every
input's distribution towards $\mathcal N(\vec 0,\mat I)$, so the
distributions of different inputs overlap and the decoder is trained on a
cloud of codes around each one, not on isolated points. Proposition 4
shows what happens when that price is set too high.

## The price of a Gaussian code

For $q=\mathcal N(\mu,s^2)$ and the prior $\mathcal N(0,1)$ in one
dimension, the KL divergence is the average under $q$ of
$\log q(z)-\log p(z)$. The log-densities are
$-\tfrac12\log2\pi s^2-(z-\mu)^2/2s^2$ and $-\tfrac12\log2\pi-z^2/2$. Under
$q$ the first quadratic averages to $\tfrac12$ and $z^2$ averages to
$\mu^2+s^2$. A diagonal Gaussian is a product, and its KL is the sum over
coordinates:

<Eq id="I.13.5" gloss="The price of a Gaussian code, coordinate by coordinate.">

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\KL\big(\mathcal N(\vec\mu,\operatorname{diag}\vec\sigma^2)\,\|\,
 \mathcal N(\vec 0,\mat I)\big)\\
&\;\;=\frac12\sum_{j=1}^k\big(\mu_j^2+\sigma_j^2-1-\log\sigma_j^2\big).
\end{aligned}
$$

</Eq>

Each coordinate costs nothing only at $\mu_j=0$, $\sigma_j=1$, which is the
prior. Its derivatives are $\mu_j$ with respect to $\mu_j$ and
$\tfrac12(\sigma_j^2-1)$ with respect to $\log\sigma_j^2$: the term pulls
every mean to zero and every variance to one. As $\sigma_j\to0$ the cost
grows like $-\log\sigma_j$ without limit, so a point code is infinitely
expensive ([I.13.X04](/elementa/neural-networks/vae/problems/x04)).

## One point, four bounds

Return to probabilistic PCA on the four points, with $\sigma^2=1$,
$\vec w=\sqrt3\,\vec u_1$ and the point $\vec x=(1,2)$. Proposition 1 found
$\log p(\vec x)=-3.531024$ and the posterior $\mathcal N(0.866025,\,0.25)$.
For any Gaussian $q=\mathcal N(m,v)$ both terms of the ELBO have closed
forms, so the figure can compare four encoders exactly:

| encoder's $q$ | ELBO | gap |
|---|---|---|
| $\mathcal N(0,1)$, the prior | $-5.837877$ | $2.306853$ |
| $\mathcal N(0,\tfrac14)$ | $-5.031024$ | $1.5$ |
| $\mathcal N(0.866,1)$ | $-4.337877$ | $0.806853$ |
| $\mathcal N(0.866,\tfrac14)$ | $-3.531024$ | $0$ |

Each gap is the KL divergence from that $q$ to the posterior, as
(I.13.4) says it must be. The prior pays the most. The right width with the
wrong mean, or the right mean with the wrong width, each closes part of the
gap. At the posterior the reconstruction term is $-2.837877$ and the price
of the code is $\log2=0.693147$, and they add to the evidence exactly.
[I.13.B01](/elementa/neural-networks/vae/problems/b01) computes every
entry.

## What maximising over the model does

A VAE maximises the ELBO over the decoder's parameters $\theta$ and the
encoder's $\phi$ together. Over $\phi$, the bound tightens. Over $\theta$,
it rises in two ways: by raising the evidence, which is the intended
effect, and by moving the true posterior towards distributions the encoder
can represent, which shrinks the gap without improving the model. The
trained model is therefore biased towards posteriors that look like
diagonal Gaussians. And the encoder is shared: with one network for every
input, the gap includes an amortisation part that a per-input
optimisation would remove (Cremer, Li and Duvenaud 2018).

None of this needs the evidence itself. Each training step uses one draw
of $\vec z$ per example to estimate the reconstruction term, the closed
form (I.13.5) for the price, and a gradient that proposition 3 explains.

## Reproduce the four bounds

~~~python
from math import log, pi, sqrt

x, w, var = (1.0, 2.0), (0.8*sqrt(3), 0.6*sqrt(3)), 1.0
wx, ww, xx = sum(a*b for a, b in zip(w, x)), sum(a*a for a in w), sum(a*a for a in x)

def elbo(m, v):      # q = N(m, v); both terms in closed form
    recon = -log(2*pi*var) - (xx - 2*m*wx + (m*m + v)*ww)/(2*var)
    kl = 0.5*(m*m + v - 1 - log(v))
    return recon - kl

for m, v in ((0, 1), (0, 0.25), (sqrt(3)/2, 1), (sqrt(3)/2, 0.25)):
    print(f"q = N({m:.3f}, {v}): ELBO {elbo(m, v):.6f}")
~~~

It prints $-5.837877$, $-5.031024$, $-4.337877$ and $-3.531024$. The
reconstruction term uses
$\E_q\|\vec x-\vec wz\|^2=\|\vec x\|^2-2m\,\vec w^{\mathsf T}\vec x+(m^2+v)\|\vec w\|^2$,
which holds for any $q$ with mean $m$ and variance $v$.
