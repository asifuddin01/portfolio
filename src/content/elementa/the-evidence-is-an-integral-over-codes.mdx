## From a code to a distribution over codes

Chapter I.12 ended with two functions and no distribution. Its encoder
gave every training input a code, and nothing said which other codes were
valid. A latent-variable model starts from the other end. It says how data
are generated: draw a code from a fixed prior, then draw an observation
from a distribution the decoder computes from that code.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\vec z&\sim p(\vec z)=\mathcal N(\vec 0,\mat I),\\
\vec x\mid\vec z&\sim p_\theta(\vec x\mid\vec z).
\end{aligned}
$$

The decoder now returns the parameters of a distribution: a mean
$g_\theta(\vec z)$ with variance $\sigma^2\mat I$ for real-valued inputs, or
one probability per coordinate for binary ones. By I.4, its negative log is
a reconstruction loss. Generating is two draws: a code from the prior, then
an observation from the decoder.

Two quantities follow, and both are hard.

<Eq id="I.13.1" gloss="The evidence is an integral over codes, and the posterior needs it.">

$$
\begin{aligned}
p_\theta(\vec x)&=\int p(\vec z)\,p_\theta(\vec x\mid\vec z)\,d\vec z,\\
p_\theta(\vec z\mid\vec x)&=\frac{p(\vec z)\,p_\theta(\vec x\mid\vec z)}
 {p_\theta(\vec x)}.
\end{aligned}
$$

</Eq>

The evidence $p_\theta(\vec x)$ is what maximum likelihood would maximise.
It averages the decoder's explanation of $\vec x$ over every code the prior
allows. The posterior, by Bayes' rule
(<ApparatusRef ref="0.PR.04" />), says which codes explain $\vec x$, and it
divides by the evidence. For a decoder that is a neural network neither has
a closed form.

## The one model where everything is exact

Make the decoder linear: $p(\vec x\mid\vec z)=\mathcal N(\mat W\vec z,\sigma^2\mat I)$.
A linear function of Gaussians is Gaussian, so the integral in (I.13.1) can
be done, and Bayes' rule can be completed by collecting the terms in
$\vec z$:

<Eq id="I.13.2" gloss="Probabilistic PCA: the one model in which the evidence and posterior are exact.">

$$
\begin{aligned}
\vec x&\sim\mathcal N\big(\vec 0,\;\mat W\mat W^{\mathsf T}+\sigma^2\mat I\big),\\
\vec z\mid\vec x&\sim\mathcal N\big(\mat M^{-1}\mat W^{\mathsf T}\vec x,\;
 \sigma^2\mat M^{-1}\big),\\
\mat M&=\mat W^{\mathsf T}\mat W+\sigma^2\mat I.
\end{aligned}
$$

</Eq>

This is probabilistic PCA. Tipping and Bishop (1999) found its maximum
likelihood in closed form. The noise variance is the average of the
discarded eigenvalues of the data covariance, and
$\mat W=\mat U_k(\mat\Lambda_k-\sigma^2\mat I)^{1/2}\mat R$ for any rotation
$\mat R$. The columns of $\mat W$ span the principal subspace of I.12.T1,
each scaled by how far its variance stands above the noise. A direction
whose eigenvalue does not exceed $\sigma^2$ gets no weight at all. Proposition
4 meets that threshold again.

Take the four points of Chapter I.12, $(1,2)$, $(2.2,0.4)$, $(-1,-2)$ and
$(-2.2,-0.4)$, with eigenvalues $4$ and $1$ along $\vec u_1=(0.8,0.6)$ and
$\vec u_2=(-0.6,0.8)$, and one latent dimension. Maximum likelihood gives
$\sigma^2=1$ and

$$
\begin{aligned}
\vec w&=\sqrt{4-1}\;\vec u_1\\
 &=(1.385641,\;1.039230).
\end{aligned}
$$

The model's covariance $\vec w\vec w^{\mathsf T}+\mat I$ has eigenvalues $4$
and $1$: it is the data covariance exactly. Every corner then has the same
log-evidence,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\log p(\vec x)&=-\log2\pi-\log2-1\\
 &=-3.531024.
\end{aligned}
$$

For $\vec x=(1,2)$, the scalar version of (I.13.2) gives the posterior.
Its precision is $1+\|\vec w\|^2/\sigma^2=4$ and its mean is
$\vec w^{\mathsf T}\vec x/(\|\vec w\|^2+\sigma^2)=2\sqrt3/4$:

$$
z\mid\vec x\sim\mathcal N(0.866025,\;0.25).
$$

Compare I.12. The principal-axis code of this point was $2$. The posterior
mean points the same way, shrunk by $\sqrt3/4$, and it comes with a width
of one half, set by the noise. The code is no longer a point.

## Why the integral cannot be sampled from the prior

The evidence is an expectation over the prior,
$p(\vec x)=\E_{p(\vec z)}[p(\vec x\mid\vec z)]$. Averaging
$p(\vec x\mid\vec z_s)$ over $n$ prior draws $\vec z_s$ estimates it without
bias. How many draws are needed? By Bayes' rule
$p(\vec x\mid\vec z)/p(\vec x)=p(\vec z\mid\vec x)/p(\vec z)$, so the
variance of one draw, relative to the square of what it estimates, is

<Eq id="I.13.3" gloss="Sampling the prior pays for every place the posterior is not.">

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\frac{\operatorname{Var}[p(\vec x\mid\vec z)]}{p(\vec x)^2}\\
&\;\;=\int\frac{p(\vec z\mid\vec x)^2}{p(\vec z)}\,d\vec z-1\\
&\;\;=\E_{p(\vec z\mid\vec x)}\!\Big[\frac{p(\vec z\mid\vec x)}{p(\vec z)}\Big]-1.
\end{aligned}
$$

</Eq>

The ratio inside is large wherever the posterior is concentrated and the
prior is not. For a one-dimensional Gaussian posterior with mean $\mu$ and
variance $s^2<2$ the integral is
$e^{\mu^2/(2-s^2)}/(s\sqrt{2-s^2})$. For the point $(1,2)$ the relative
variance is $1.320797$, so a relative standard error of ten per cent takes
about $132$ draws.

That is the easy case. With noise variance $0.01$ in place of $1$ the
posterior has standard deviation $0.05$ and the relative variance is
$22.316431$. For independent latent dimensions the integral in (I.13.3)
is a product, one factor of $1+r$ for each dimension with relative variance
$r$. In ten such dimensions the count exceeds $10^{15}$;
[I.13.B05](/elementa/neural-networks/vae/problems/b05) works it out. Almost
every prior draw lands where the posterior is not, and contributes almost
nothing.

The remedy is to draw codes from something close to the posterior. That
distribution must be found for every observation, and finding it by a
separate optimisation for each one is slow. Kingma and Welling (2014) and
Rezende, Mohamed and Wierstra (2014) train an encoder network to output
it instead. Sharing one network across all inputs is called amortised
inference. It is fast, and it costs something: Cremer, Li and Duvenaud
(2018) found that the bound lost to the shared encoder often exceeds the
loss from the Gaussian family itself.

## The variational autoencoder in one picture

The encoder of a variational autoencoder returns two vectors of width $k$,
a mean $\vec\mu$ and a width $\vec\sigma$, which define
$q_\phi(\vec z\mid\vec x)=\mathcal N(\vec\mu,\operatorname{diag}\vec\sigma^2)$.
A code is drawn from it, the decoder explains $\vec x$ from that code, and
the loss adds a second term that keeps $q_\phi$ close to the prior. The
figure shows the path. Proposition 2 derives the loss, and proposition 3
shows how a gradient passes through the draw.

A width must be positive. A linear output layer can return $-0.3$, which
is not a standard deviation. Encoders therefore predict $\log\sigma^2$ and
exponentiate, or pass the output through softplus, as
[I.3.X02](/elementa/neural-networks/activations/problems/x02) anticipated.

## Compute the evidence and the posterior

This script evaluates the integral in (I.13.1) on a grid for the point
$(1,2)$ and the model above:

~~~python
from math import exp, log, pi, sqrt

x, w, var = (1.0, 2.0), (0.8*sqrt(3), 0.6*sqrt(3)), 1.0   # probabilistic PCA

def joint(z):        # p(z) p(x | z) for z ~ N(0, 1), x | z ~ N(w z, var I)
    r2 = sum((xi - wi*z)**2 for xi, wi in zip(x, w))
    return exp(-z*z/2)/sqrt(2*pi)*exp(-r2/(2*var))/(2*pi*var)

h = 0.001
grid = [i*h for i in range(-10000, 10001)]
evidence = sum(joint(z) for z in grid)*h
mean = sum(z*joint(z) for z in grid)*h/evidence
spread = sum((z - mean)**2*joint(z) for z in grid)*h/evidence
print(round(log(evidence), 6), round(mean, 6), round(spread, 6))
~~~

It prints <code>-3.531024 0.866025 0.25</code>: the log-evidence, and the
posterior's mean and variance, as (I.13.2) predicts. A grid works in one
dimension. In twenty it would need $20{,}001^{20}$ points.
