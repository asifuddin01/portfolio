## Rate and distortion

Average the ELBO over the data and give its two terms names. The average
KL term is the rate $R$: the cost, in nats, of the code relative to the
prior. The average reconstruction term, negated, is the distortion. With a
Gaussian decoder of fixed variance $\sigma^2$ the distortion is the mean
squared error $D$, up to the factor $1/(2\sigma^2)$ and a constant, and the
ELBO is minus their sum.

Higgins and colleagues (2017) put a weight $\beta$ on the rate and found
that $\beta>1$ gave codes whose coordinates varied more independently. A
weight changes which point on the trade-off training reaches. Alemi and
colleagues (2018) drew that trade-off as a curve in the plane of rate and
distortion, and showed that models with the same ELBO can sit at very
different points of it. The ELBO alone does not say how much the code is
used.

## The linear β-VAE, solved exactly

Keep probabilistic PCA's decoder with one latent dimension and $\sigma^2$
fixed, and let the encoder be any Gaussian $\mathcal N(m,v)$ for each input.
Write $a=\vec w^{\mathsf T}\vec x$ and $r=\|\vec w\|^2$. The $\beta$-weighted
ELBO of one input is a quadratic in $m$ and a sum of $v$ and $\log v$, so
the best encoder can be found by setting two derivatives to zero:

$$
\begin{aligned}
m&=\frac{a}{r+\beta\sigma^2},\\
v&=\frac{\beta\sigma^2}{r+\beta\sigma^2}.
\end{aligned}
$$

At $\beta=1$ this is the exact posterior of (I.13.2). Substituting back and
averaging over the data, using $\E[a^2]=r\lambda_1$ for $\vec w$ along
$\vec u_1$, leaves a function of $r$ alone whose derivative is

$$
\frac{\beta}{2(r+\beta\sigma^2)}
 \Big(\frac{\lambda_1}{r+\beta\sigma^2}-1\Big).
$$

It is positive until $r+\beta\sigma^2=\lambda_1$ and negative after. If
$\beta\sigma^2\geq\lambda_1$ it is never positive, and the best $r$ is
zero. Evaluating the rate and the distortion at the optimum gives

<Eq id="I.13.8" gloss="A linear β-VAE trades rate for distortion and switches off past a threshold.">

$$
\begin{aligned}
\|\vec w\|^2&=\max(\lambda_1-\beta\sigma^2,\;0),\\
R&=\tfrac12\log\frac{\lambda_1}{\beta\sigma^2},\\
D&=\sum_{i>1}\lambda_i+\beta\sigma^2,
\end{aligned}
$$

</Eq>

the last two while $\beta\sigma^2<\lambda_1$. Past the threshold the rate
is zero and the distortion is the total variance. This is I.13.T3, and
[I.13.B03](/elementa/neural-networks/vae/problems/b03) derives every line.

On the four points, with $\lambda_1=4$, $\lambda_2=1$ and $\sigma^2=1$,
(I.13.8) gives a rate of $\tfrac12\log(4/\beta)$ and a distortion of
$1+\beta$. The figure draws the curve:

| $\beta$ | $\|\vec w\|^2$ | rate $R$ | $D$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| $0.25$ | $3.75$ | $1.386294$ | $1.25$ |
| $1$ | $3$ | $0.693147$ | $2$ |
| $2$ | $2$ | $0.346574$ | $3$ |
| $4$ | $0$ | $0$ | $5$ |

At $\beta=1$ the solution is probabilistic PCA, and the rate $\log2$ is the
price of the code found in proposition 2. As $\beta\to0$ the distortion
falls to $1$, which is the principal-axis autoencoder's loss in I.12, and
the rate grows without bound: the encoder's variance $\beta\sigma^2/\lambda_1$
goes to zero and the code becomes a point. The deterministic autoencoder of
I.12 is the corner of this curve where the code is free and infinitely
expensive.

## Posterior collapse

At $\beta=4$ the optimum is $\vec w=\vec 0$. The decoder ignores the code,
the encoder returns the prior $\mathcal N(0,1)$ for every input, the rate
is zero and the distortion is $5$, the whole variance of the data. That is
posterior collapse, and here it is not a failure of optimisation. It is the
maximum of the objective: the variance the code could explain, $4$, is not
worth its price, $\beta\sigma^2=4$.

With $\beta=1$ and $\sigma^2$ learned, the same threshold appears per
direction: probabilistic PCA gives zero weight to every direction whose
eigenvalue does not exceed the noise variance. Lucas, Tucker, Grosse and
Norouzi (2019) showed that linear VAEs collapse exactly where probabilistic
PCA does, and that the ELBO adds no bad maxima of its own. The threshold is
the one weight decay imposed in (I.12.6), with $\sigma^2$ in place of
$\gamma$.

Nonlinear decoders add a second route. A decoder powerful enough to model
$\vec x$ well without $\vec z$, such as one that predicts each word of a
sentence from the words before it, can reach a good reconstruction term
at zero rate. The KL term is then pure cost, and training removes the code.
Bowman and colleagues (2016) met this with sentence VAEs. They annealed the
KL weight up from zero during training, and weakened the decoder by hiding
some of its input words, so that the code had work to do.

## What the rate pays for

The rate is an average over the training examples, indexed by $n$. Write
$q(\vec z)$ for the aggregate posterior, the average of the encoder's
distributions. Then

<Eq id="I.13.9" gloss="The rate pays for information in the code and for mismatch with the prior.">

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\frac1N\sum_{n=1}^N\KL\big(q(\vec z\mid\vec x_n)\,\|\,p(\vec z)\big)\\
&\;\;=I_q(n;\vec z)+\KL\big(q(\vec z)\,\|\,p(\vec z)\big).
\end{aligned}
$$

</Eq>

To see it, write each $\log q(\vec z\mid\vec x_n)/p(\vec z)$ as
$\log q(\vec z\mid\vec x_n)/q(\vec z)$ plus $\log q(\vec z)/p(\vec z)$. The
first averages to the mutual information between the code and the index of
the example (<ApparatusRef ref="0.IT.04" />), the second to the KL from the
aggregate posterior to the prior. Hoffman and Johnson (2016) introduced
this split. It is I.13.T4.

Both parts mean something. Zero rate forces zero information: collapse is
exactly a code that says nothing about which example it came from. And the
second part is the hole problem of I.12, given a price. Where $q(\vec z)$
is thin and the prior is not, codes drawn for generation land in places
the decoder rarely saw in training.

On the four points at $\beta=1$, every encoder distribution has variance
$0.25$ and mean $\pm0.866025$, so $q(z)$ is a mixture of two Gaussians.
The rate $0.693147$ splits into $0.585939$ of information and $0.107208$ of
mismatch ([I.13.X06](/elementa/neural-networks/vae/problems/x06)). The
mixture has variance exactly $1$, like the prior, but not its shape: at
$z=0$ its density is $0.178032$ where the prior's is $0.398942$. The prior
draws codes near zero more than twice as often as the encoder produced
them.

## What the variational autoencoder fixed, and what it did not

Against I.12 it fixed three things. Codes have a distribution, so new ones
can be drawn. The space between training codes is trained, because each
input is decoded from a cloud of codes. And the objective is a lower bound
on the likelihood, which an autoencoder never offered.

Three things remain. The bound can be loose, and proposition 2 showed the
gap cannot be measured on its own. The holes are priced, not removed.
And a Gaussian decoder still returns a conditional mean, so ambiguous
inputs are reconstructed as blurred averages, for the reason given in
[I.12.X08](/elementa/neural-networks/autoencoders/problems/x08).

## The curve, in six lines

~~~python
from math import log
lam1, lam2, var = 4.0, 1.0, 1.0
for beta in (0.25, 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 8):
    r = max(lam1 - beta*var, 0.0)                  # ||w||^2 at the optimum
    rate = 0.5*log(lam1/(beta*var)) if r > 0 else 0.0
    distortion = lam2 + beta*var if r > 0 else lam1 + lam2
    print(f"beta {beta}: ||w||^2 {r:.2f}, rate {rate:.6f}, distortion {distortion:.2f}")
~~~

It prints the table above with $\beta=0.5$ and $\beta=8$ added: rate
$1.039721$ with distortion $1.5$, and a collapsed code with distortion $5$.
The check script compares these closed forms with a direct maximisation of
the averaged objective.
