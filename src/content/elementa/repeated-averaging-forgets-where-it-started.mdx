## Averaging has a fixed direction

Strip a graph convolution of its weights and nonlinearity and stack it $k$
times: the result is $\hat{\mat A}^k\vec x$. This is the part of a deep graph
network that the graph contributes, and it has a limit.

Let $\vec s$ hold the square roots of the degrees with self-loops,
$s_v=\sqrt{\tilde d_v}$. Each row of $\mat A+\mat I$ sums to that node's
degree, so

$$
\begin{aligned}
\hat{\mat A}\vec s
 &=\tilde{\mat D}^{-1/2}(\mat A+\mat I)\mathbf 1\\
 &=\tilde{\mat D}^{-1/2}\tilde{\vec d}=\vec s,
\end{aligned}
$$

and $\vec s$ is an eigenvector with eigenvalue $1$. The matrix $\hat{\mat A}$ is similar to
$\tilde{\mat D}^{-1}(\mat A+\mat I)$, a random walk that may stay put. For
a connected graph that walk can reach every node and is not periodic, so by
the Perron–Frobenius theorem the eigenvalue $1$ is simple and every other
eigenvalue lies strictly between $-1$ and $1$. Expanding $\vec x$ in the
orthonormal eigenvectors of the symmetric $\hat{\mat A}$, each component is
multiplied by its eigenvalue at every step:

<Eq id="I.14.6" gloss="Repeated averaging forgets everything but one direction.">

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\hat{\mat A}^k\vec x=\sum_i\lambda_i^k\,\vec u_i\vec u_i^{\mathsf T}\vec x\\
&\;\;\to\vec u_1\vec u_1^{\mathsf T}\vec x,\\
&\vec u_1=\vec s/\|\vec s\|,\\[4pt]
&\big\|\hat{\mat A}^k\vec x-\vec u_1\vec u_1^{\mathsf T}\vec x\big\|\\
&\;\;\leq|\lambda_2|^k\,\big\|\vec x-\vec u_1\vec u_1^{\mathsf T}\vec x\big\|.
\end{aligned}
$$

</Eq>

Here $|\lambda_2|$ is the largest absolute value among the other
eigenvalues. The limit gives every node the value $c\sqrt{\tilde d_v}$ for
one number $c$: once divided by $\sqrt{\tilde d_v}$, all nodes agree, and the
features that distinguished them are gone. That is over-smoothing, and
(I.14.6) is I.14.T3. The geometry is that of I.9: a product of the same
matrix, with the eigenvalues deciding what survives. There the problem was
a vanishing gradient; here it is a vanishing difference between nodes.

## On the paw graph

The paw's $\hat{\mat A}$ has eigenvalues $1$, $0.564333$, $0$ and
$-0.147667$, so $|\lambda_2|=0.564333$. Starting from $\vec x=(1,2,3,4)$, the
limit is

$$
\begin{aligned}
\vec u_1\vec u_1^{\mathsf T}\vec x
 =(&2.432522,\;2.432522,\\
 &2.808834,\;1.986146),
\end{aligned}
$$

which is $1.404417\sqrt{\tilde d_v}$ at every node. The distance to it is
$2.516217$ at the start and

| layers $k$ | distance to the limit |
|---|---|
| $1$ | $1.358469$ |
| $2$ | $0.766463$ |
| $4$ | $0.244093$ |
| $8$ | $0.024757$ |
| $16$ | $0.000255$ |

each within the bound $0.564333^k\times2.516217$. The figure plots the four
node values divided by $\sqrt{\tilde d_v}$: after ten rounds they differ by
less than one per cent.

The eigenvalue $0$ explains what proposition 2 found. Its eigenvector is
$(1,-1,0,0)/\sqrt2$, the difference between nodes $1$ and $2$, and one
application of $\hat{\mat A}$ removes that component entirely. That is why
the two nodes merged after one layer.
[I.14.B02](/elementa/neural-networks/gnn/problems/b02) derives every
number.

## What a trained network keeps

Weights and nonlinearities change the rate, not the tendency. Oono and
Suzuki (2020) showed that for graph convolutions with ReLU, when the
largest singular value of each weight matrix times $|\lambda_2|$ is below
one, the node states approach a subspace that carries no information
beyond degrees, exponentially fast in the depth. Li, Han and Wu (2018)
read the graph convolution as Laplacian smoothing. A few rounds help,
because a node borrows evidence from labelled neighbours; many rounds
hurt, because every node borrows the same evidence. Graph networks with
two or three layers are common for this reason. Residual connections,
which I.11 used to make deep convolutional networks trainable, keep part of
each node's own state and slow the collapse.

Depth fails in a second way that is not smoothing. A node $k$ hops from
many others must pass all their information through one fixed-width
vector. Alon and Yahav (2021) called this over-squashing: the number of
nodes within $k$ hops can grow exponentially while the width does not.

## Without self-loops

The self-loop is what keeps the eigenvalues above $-1$. Without it, a
bipartite graph's normalised adjacency $\mat D^{-1/2}\mat A\mat D^{-1/2}$ has
the eigenvalue $-1$, whose eigenvector alternates in sign between the two
sides. On a hexagon the signal $(1,-1,1,-1,1,-1)$ flips sign at every layer
and never converges. With self-loops the hexagon's eigenvalues are
$(1+2\cos\theta)/3$, the largest after $1$ is $\tfrac23$, and differences
fall below one per cent after $12$ layers
([I.14.X05](/elementa/neural-networks/gnn/problems/x05)).

## Watch it converge

~~~python
from math import sqrt
A = [[0, 1, 1, 0], [1, 0, 1, 0], [1, 1, 0, 1], [0, 0, 1, 0]]
d = [sum(r) + 1 for r in A]                                  # degrees with self-loops
Ahat = [[(A[i][j] + (i == j))/sqrt(d[i]*d[j]) for j in range(4)] for i in range(4)]
h = [1.0, 2.0, 3.0, 4.0]
u = [sqrt(di/sum(d)) for di in d]                            # the fixed direction
limit = [ui*sum(uj*hj for uj, hj in zip(u, h)) for ui in u]
for k in range(1, 17):
    h = [sum(a*b for a, b in zip(row, h)) for row in Ahat]
    if k in (1, 2, 4, 8, 16):
        print(k, round(sqrt(sum((a - b)**2 for a, b in zip(h, limit))), 6))
~~~

It prints the distances in the table: $1.358469$, $0.766463$, $0.244093$,
$0.024757$ and $0.000255$. From $k=8$ to $k=16$ the distance falls by a
factor of $97$, which is $0.564333^{-8}$ to within a third of one per cent:
the second eigenvalue, applied once per layer.
