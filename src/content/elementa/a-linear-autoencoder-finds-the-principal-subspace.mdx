## The question, stated so it can be answered

Proposition 1 left one thing open: a bottleneck forces a linear autoencoder
to discard a subspace, and the loss chooses which. By (I.12.2) the loss
depends on the two matrices only through their product
$\mat M=\mat D\mat E$, which has rank at most $k$:

$$
\mathcal L(\mat M)=\operatorname{tr}\big(
 (\mat I-\mat M)\,\mat S\,(\mat I-\mat M)^{\mathsf T}\big).
$$

Assume the data are centred, so that $\mat S$ is their covariance
([I.12.X03](/elementa/neural-networks/autoencoders/problems/x03) shows what
happens otherwise). Write its eigendecomposition
(<ApparatusRef ref="0.LA.04" />) as
$\mat S=\sum_i\lambda_i\vec u_i\vec u_i^{\mathsf T}$ with
$\lambda_1\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_d\geq0$ and orthonormal $\vec u_i$. Let
$\mat U_k$ hold the first $k$ eigenvectors as columns. Which $\mat M$ of
rank at most $k$ makes the loss smallest?

## Each half is a least-squares problem

Hold one matrix fixed and the other has a closed form.

Fix the encoder. The codes are then fixed, and the loss is a linear
regression of each input on its code. Setting
$\nabla_{\mat D}\mathcal L=\mat 0$ in (I.12.2) gives
$(\mat I-\mat D\mat E)\mat S\mat E^{\mathsf T}=\mat 0$, which is solved by the
first line below whenever the code covariance
$\mat E\mat S\mat E^{\mathsf T}$ is invertible.

Fix the decoder, with independent columns. For one input, the code that
minimises $\|\vec x-\mat D\vec z\|^2$ is the least-squares solution
$\vec z=(\mat D^{\mathsf T}\mat D)^{-1}\mat D^{\mathsf T}\vec x$. It is
linear in $\vec x$, so a linear encoder can produce the best code for every
input at once.

<Eq id="I.12.3" gloss="Each half, taken alone, is a least-squares problem with a closed form.">

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{fixed }\mat E:\quad
 \mat D&=\mat S\mat E^{\mathsf T}
 (\mat E\mat S\mat E^{\mathsf T})^{-1},\\
\text{fixed }\mat D:\quad
 \mat E&=(\mat D^{\mathsf T}\mat D)^{-1}\mat D^{\mathsf T}.
\end{aligned}
$$

</Eq>

The second line settles the form of the answer. With that encoder,

$$
\mat M=\mat D(\mat D^{\mathsf T}\mat D)^{-1}\mat D^{\mathsf T}=\mat P,
$$

the orthogonal projection onto the column space of $\mat D$
(<ApparatusRef ref="0.LA.05" />): $\mat P^2=\mat P$ and
$\mat P^{\mathsf T}=\mat P$. So for any decoder, no encoder does better
than the one that makes the autoencoder a projection onto the decoder's
columns. A decoder of lower rank spans a smaller subspace, and enlarging a
subspace never increases a distance to it. The search over pairs of
matrices has become a search over $k$-dimensional subspaces.

## The loss of a projection is the variance it drops

Let the columns of $\mat Q\in\R^{d\times k}$ be an orthonormal basis of the
subspace, so $\mat P=\mat Q\mat Q^{\mathsf T}$. Since $\mat I-\mat P$ is
also a symmetric projection, the cyclic property of the trace gives

<Eq id="I.12.4" gloss="A projection's loss is the variance it fails to keep.">

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal L(\mat Q\mat Q^{\mathsf T})
 &=\operatorname{tr}\big(\mat S(\mat I-\mat Q\mat Q^{\mathsf T})\big)\\
 &=\operatorname{tr}\mat S-\operatorname{tr}(\mat Q^{\mathsf T}\mat S\mat Q).
\end{aligned}
$$

</Eq>

Here $\operatorname{tr}\mat S$ is the total variance of the data and
$\operatorname{tr}(\mat Q^{\mathsf T}\mat S\mat Q)$ is the total variance of
the codes $\mat Q^{\mathsf T}\vec x$. By (I.12.4), minimising reconstruction
error and maximising retained variance are one problem.

Now expand in the eigenbasis:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{tr}(\mat Q^{\mathsf T}\mat S\mat Q)
 &=\sum_{i=1}^d\lambda_i c_i,\\
c_i&=\|\mat Q^{\mathsf T}\vec u_i\|^2.
\end{aligned}
$$

Each $c_i$ is the squared length of the projection of a unit vector, so
$0\leq c_i\leq1$. They sum to
$\operatorname{tr}(\mat Q^{\mathsf T}\mat Q)=k$, because the $\vec u_i$ form
an orthonormal basis. A weighted sum of the $\lambda_i$ with weights
between zero and one that total $k$ is largest when weight one sits on the
$k$ largest. Hence the retained variance is at most
$\lambda_1+\cdots+\lambda_k$. If $\lambda_k>\lambda_{k+1}$, equality forces
$c_i=1$ for $i\leq k$, which places every one of $\vec u_1,\ldots,\vec u_k$
inside the subspace.

<Eq id="I.12.5" gloss="The minimum fixes the subspace and leaves the basis free.">

$$
\begin{aligned}
\min_{\mat E,\mat D}\mathcal L&=\sum_{i>k}\lambda_i,\\
\text{attained at}\quad\mat D\mat E&=\mat U_k\mat U_k^{\mathsf T},\\
\text{that is,}\quad\mat D&=\mat U_k\mat G,\\
\mat E&=\mat G^{-1}\mat U_k^{\mathsf T}.
\end{aligned}
$$

</Eq>

That is I.12.T1.
[I.12.B02](/elementa/neural-networks/autoencoders/problems/b02) writes out
each step and evaluates it on the four points of the figure, where
$\lambda=(4,1)$ and $\vec u_1=(0.8,0.6)$: the best width-one autoencoder
projects onto that line and its loss is exactly $1$.

The result has an older name. With $\mat X$ the centred data matrix, its
right singular vectors are the $\vec u_i$ and
$\lambda_i=s_i^2/n$ for singular values $s_i$. The reconstruction
$\mat X\mat U_k\mat U_k^{\mathsf T}$ is the best approximation of $\mat X$
of rank $k$ in the Frobenius norm, which is the theorem of Eckart and Young
(1936). Bourlard and Kamp (1988) and Baldi and Hornik (1989) recognised it
in the autoencoder.

## The subspace, and not its axes

The last two lines of (I.12.5) hold for every invertible $\mat G$ of size
$k\times k$, since $\mat G$ cancels in the product. The loss cannot tell
these factorisations apart, so the minimum is a family with $k^2$ free
numbers.

Principal component analysis delivers more than this. Its directions are
orthonormal and ordered by variance, and its scores are uncorrelated. A
trained linear autoencoder delivers a basis of the right subspace and
nothing else. The decoder's columns need not be orthogonal or of unit
length, the code's coordinates may be correlated, and none comes first. On
the four points, $\mat E=(4,\,3)$ with $\mat D=(0.16,0.12)^{\mathsf T}$
computes the same reconstructions as $\mat E=(0.8,\,0.6)$ with
$\mat D=(0.8,0.6)^{\mathsf T}$, and every one of its codes is five times
larger
([I.12.X01](/elementa/neural-networks/autoencoders/problems/x01)).

Training does pick one member of the family, and the data do not decide
which. Under gradient descent with a vanishing step size,
$\mat E\mat E^{\mathsf T}-\mat D^{\mathsf T}\mat D$ does not change: by
(I.12.2), $\frac{d}{dt}(\mat E\mat E^{\mathsf T})$ and
$\frac{d}{dt}(\mat D^{\mathsf T}\mat D)$ are the same matrix. Du, Hu and Lee
(2018) prove invariances of this kind between adjacent layers of deep
homogeneous networks. The factorisation reached is therefore fixed by the
imbalance between the two matrices at initialisation.

To read principal components off a trained linear autoencoder,
orthonormalise the decoder's columns into $\mat Q$, form the $k\times k$
matrix $\mat Q^{\mathsf T}\mat S\mat Q$ and diagonalise it. Or change the
objective so that the freedom is not there.

## Weight decay removes most of the freedom

Add the penalty $\gamma(\|\mat E\|_F^2+\|\mat D\|_F^2)$ of I.8. The
stationarity conditions become
$(\mat I-\mat D\mat E)\mat S\mat E^{\mathsf T}=\gamma\mat D$ and
$\mat D^{\mathsf T}(\mat I-\mat D\mat E)\mat S=\gamma\mat E$. Multiply the
first on the left by $\mat D^{\mathsf T}$ and the second on the right by
$\mat E^{\mathsf T}$. The left-hand sides agree, so for $\gamma>0$,
$\mat D^{\mathsf T}\mat D=\mat E\mat E^{\mathsf T}$ at every stationary
point. Kunin, Bloom, Goeva and Seed (2019) prove more: every stationary
point has $\mat E=\mat D^{\mathsf T}$, and for $\gamma<\lambda_k$ the minima
are

<Eq id="I.12.6" gloss="Weight decay shrinks each direction and leaves only a rotation of the code.">

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mat D&=\mat U_k\operatorname{diag}\!\Big(\sqrt{1-\gamma/\lambda_i}\Big)
 \mat O^{\mathsf T},\\
\mat E&=\mat D^{\mathsf T},\\
\mat D\mat E&=\mat U_k\operatorname{diag}(1-\gamma/\lambda_i)\,
 \mat U_k^{\mathsf T},
\end{aligned}
$$

</Eq>

with $\mat O$ any orthogonal $k\times k$ matrix. The family has shrunk from
all invertible $\mat G$ to rotations of the code. When the kept eigenvalues
are distinct, so are the singular values $\sqrt{1-\gamma/\lambda_i}$ of the
decoder. Its left singular vectors are then
$\vec u_1,\ldots,\vec u_k$ in order, and the axes can be read from the
decoder alone.

The price is in the last line of (I.12.6). The end-to-end map is no
longer a projection: each kept direction is shrunk by $1-\gamma/\lambda_i$,
a weak one more than a strong one.
[I.12.X06](/elementa/neural-networks/autoencoders/problems/x06) verifies
that (I.12.6) is stationary and evaluates it.

## Every other stationary point is a saddle

Take any $k$ eigenvectors of $\mat S$, not necessarily the first, as the
columns of $\mat U_I$, and set $\mat D=\mat U_I$,
$\mat E=\mat U_I^{\mathsf T}$. Then
$(\mat I-\mat D\mat E)\mat S\mat U_I=\mat 0$ and
$\mat U_I^{\mathsf T}(\mat I-\mat D\mat E)=\mat 0$, so both gradients in
(I.12.2) vanish. The loss there is the sum of the eigenvalues left out.

Such a point is not a minimum unless it is the principal subspace. Suppose
$\vec u_j$ is included, $\vec u_m$ is not, and $\lambda_m>\lambda_j$.
Replace $\vec u_j$ by $\cos\theta\,\vec u_j+\sin\theta\,\vec u_m$. The
subspace stays $k$-dimensional and the loss changes by
$-(\lambda_m-\lambda_j)\sin^2\theta$, which is negative for every small
$\theta\neq0$. Scaling the decoder up instead raises the loss. A point with
a way down and a way up is a saddle. Baldi and Hornik (1989) show that for
full-rank $\mat S$ with distinct eigenvalues there are no other stationary
points of rank $k$ beyond these and their $\mat G$-families. This is
I.12.T2: the loss surface of a linear autoencoder has no local minimum that
is not global.

It promises the destination and not the schedule. Near a saddle the
gradient is small, and the escape is slower the closer the start. On the
four points, begin half a unit along $\vec u_2$ with a tilt of $10^{-6}$
towards $\vec u_1$, at step size $0.01$. The loss sits within $0.01$ of the
saddle's value $4$ from step $82$ to step $273$, and reaches $1.01$ only at
step $513$. With a tilt of $10^{-3}$ it has left by step $85$. Saxe,
McClelland and Ganguli (2014) solve these dynamics exactly for deep linear
networks: long plateaus, then rapid drops, with the stronger directions
learned first.

## A linear decoder caps the autoencoder at this bound

The projection step used nothing about the encoder except that it could
produce the best code. So make the encoder anything at all, deep and
nonlinear, and keep an affine decoder $g(\vec z)=\mat D\vec z+\vec c$.
Every reconstruction lies in the flat $\vec c+\operatorname{col}(\mat D)$,
of dimension at most $k$. For centred data, the mean squared distance to
that flat is the mean squared distance to the parallel subspace through
the origin plus the squared distance of $\vec c$ from that subspace, so it
is at least $\lambda_{k+1}+\cdots+\lambda_d$. A nonlinear encoder in front
of a linear decoder cannot beat principal components on the training loss.

To bend the reconstruction surface, the decoder must be nonlinear.
Hinton and Salakhutdinov (2006) trained a
$784$-$1000$-$500$-$250$-$30$ autoencoder on images of handwritten digits,
pretrained one layer at a time, with logistic units throughout except for a
linear code. On test images they report an average squared error of $3.00$
per image, against $13.87$ for principal components with the same thirty
numbers. Proposition 3 takes up what else
has to be controlled once the decoder has that freedom.

## Train one and watch the product

This script runs gradient descent on (I.12.2) for the four points, from a
deliberately unbalanced start:

~~~python
S = [[2.92, 1.44], [1.44, 2.08]]          # covariance of the four points
e, d = [1.0, 0.0], [0.2, 0.0]             # encoder row, decoder column

def loss_and_grads(e, d):
    r = [[(i == j) - d[i]*e[j] for j in range(2)] for i in range(2)]   # I - DE
    rs = [[r[i][0]*S[0][j] + r[i][1]*S[1][j] for j in range(2)] for i in range(2)]
    loss = sum(rs[i][j]*r[i][j] for i in range(2) for j in range(2))
    gd = [-2*(rs[i][0]*e[0] + rs[i][1]*e[1]) for i in range(2)]
    ge = [-2*(d[0]*rs[0][j] + d[1]*rs[1][j]) for j in range(2)]
    return loss, ge, gd

for step in range(4000):
    loss, ge, gd = loss_and_grads(e, d)
    e = [a - 0.01*g for a, g in zip(e, ge)]
    d = [a - 0.01*g for a, g in zip(d, gd)]
print(round(loss, 6), [round(d[i]*e[j], 4) for i in range(2) for j in range(2)])
print([round(v, 4) for v in e], [round(v, 4) for v in d])
~~~

It prints a loss of <code>1.0</code> and the product
<code>[0.64, 0.48, 0.48, 0.36]</code>, which is
$\vec u_1\vec u_1^{\mathsf T}$. The factors are
<code>[1.0048, 0.7536]</code> and <code>[0.6369, 0.4777]</code>: both
point along $(0.8,0.6)$, and neither is that vector. The imbalance
$\|\mat E\|^2-\|\mat D\|^2$ began at $0.96$ and ends at $0.9438$, moved
only by the finite step size. The product is determined by the data. The
factors remember where they started.
