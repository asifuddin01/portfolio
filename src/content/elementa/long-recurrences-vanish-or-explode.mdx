## Start at the loss, not at a product sign

Use the tanh cell of (I.9.1) and the summed loss of (I.9.2).
Write $\vec e_t=\partial\ell_t/\partial\vec z_t$ and
$\vec\delta_t=\partial\mathcal L/\partial\vec a_t$.
If position $t$ has no local loss, set $\vec e_t=0$.

Two arrows leave $\vec h_t$: one to its readout, one to the next recurrent
step. Reverse mode adds both returns. Since
$\tanh'(a)=1-\tanh^2(a)$,

<Eq id="I.9.3" gloss="The current loss and the future loss meet before the activation derivative.">

$$
\begin{aligned}
\vec q_t&=\mat V^{\mathsf T}\vec e_t+
          \mat W^{\mathsf T}\vec\delta_{t+1},\\
\vec\delta_t&=\vec q_t\odot(1-\vec h_t^2),\\
\vec\delta_{T+1}&=0.
\end{aligned}
$$

</Eq>

Squares and $\odot$ are coordinatewise. The backward sweep evaluates (I.9.3)
from $T$ down to $1$. A mean loss or a fixed padding mask is already included
in the definition of $\vec e_t$; do not average the gradients a second time.

At one cell, a perturbation of $\mat W$ produces
$d\vec a_t=d\mat W\,\vec h_{t-1}$. The local contribution is the outer product
$\vec\delta_t\vec h_{t-1}^{\mathsf T}$. Because all cells use the same matrix,

<Eq id="I.9.4" gloss="Each occurrence contributes to one shared parameter gradient.">

$$
\begin{aligned}
\nabla_{\mat W}\mathcal L
 &=\sum_t\vec\delta_t\vec h_{t-1}^{\mathsf T},\\
\nabla_{\mat U}\mathcal L
 &=\sum_t\vec\delta_t\vec x_t^{\mathsf T},\\
\nabla_{\vec b}\mathcal L&=\sum_t\vec\delta_t.
\end{aligned}
$$

</Eq>

The readout derivatives are
$\nabla_{\mat V}\mathcal L=\sum_t\vec e_t\vec h_t^{\mathsf T}$ and
$\nabla_{\vec c}\mathcal L=\sum_t\vec e_t$.
The input derivative is $\mat U^{\mathsf T}\vec\delta_t$.
For a learned initial state, its derivative is
$\mat W^{\mathsf T}\vec\delta_1$.
Regularisation would add another term to the parameter gradients.

Equations (I.9.3) and (I.9.4) prove I.9.T1. There is no extra learning rule
peculiar to time. There is a graph, reused parameters, and the chain rule.
[I.9.B02](/elementa/neural-networks/recurrence/problems/b02) works every scalar
contribution by hand.

## Now isolate a distant loss

Let $\mat A_t$ be the state-to-state Jacobian, holding inputs fixed:

<Eq id="I.9.5" gloss="The earliest Jacobian acts first, on the right of the forward product.">

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mat A_t&=\operatorname{diag}(1-\vec h_t^2)\mat W,\\
\frac{\partial\vec h_T}{\partial\vec h_s}
 &=\mat A_T\mat A_{T-1}\cdots\mat A_{s+1}.
\end{aligned}
$$

</Eq>

For a loss only at $T$, the column gradient at $s$ is the transpose of the
product in (I.9.5) times $\nabla_{\vec h_T}\ell_T$.
For losses at many positions, add the contribution of each later loss.
A locally supervised position can therefore receive a useful gradient even
when a much later loss has almost no influence on it.

The weights repeat. The Jacobians generally do not: their diagonal activation
factors depend on the actual inputs and states.

By applying $\|AB\|_2\leq\|A\|_2\|B\|_2$ repeatedly,

<Eq id="I.9.6" gloss="A uniform bound below one proves contraction; a bound above one does not prove explosion.">

$$
\begin{aligned}
\left\|\frac{\partial\vec h_T}{\partial\vec h_s}\right\|_2
 &\leq\prod_{k=s+1}^T\|\mat A_k\|_2,\\
\|\mat A_k\|_2\leq r\ \text{for all }k
 &\implies
 \left\|\frac{\partial\vec h_T}{\partial\vec h_s}\right\|_2
 \leq r^{T-s}.
\end{aligned}
$$

</Eq>

This proves I.9.T2. Since the tanh derivative is at most one,
$\|\mat W\|_2<1$ is a sufficient uniform contraction condition for this cell.
It is not necessary: saturation may contract a trajectory even with a larger
weight norm.

The figure shows a deliberately scalar special case with a constant multiplier.
Across thirty steps, $0.7^{30}\approx2.2539\times10^{-5}$ and
$1.3^{30}\approx2.6200\times10^3$.
The first is small, not numerically identical to zero. Whether it is useful
depends on the other gradients, numerical precision and optimiser.

A matrix needs more care than a scalar. Large singular values indicate
directions of amplification, but the next matrix may contract those directions.
Even eigenvalues strictly inside the unit circle do not exclude large
finite-time amplification for a non-normal matrix.
[I.9.B03](/elementa/neural-networks/recurrence/problems/b03) constructs one.
And alternating scalar factors $2$ and $1/2$ have a unit product every two
steps, despite neither factor being one.

## What clipping repairs

For a finite concatenated parameter gradient $\vec g$ and threshold $\tau>0$,

<Eq id="I.9.7" gloss="One common scale factor preserves the direction across all parameter tensors.">

$$
\vec g_{\mathrm{clip}}
=\vec g\min\left(1,\frac{\tau}{\|\vec g\|_2}\right).
$$

</Eq>

Define the zero-gradient case separately as zero. Equation (I.9.7) bounds the
norm by $\tau$. With plain gradient descent, the update norm is at most
$\eta\tau$. That does not directly bound an Adam update or a step containing
momentum, weight decay or other terms.

Accumulate the intended gradient first, then clip, then apply the optimiser.
Clipping each time step separately changes the sum. Clipping each tensor
separately changes the direction of the concatenated gradient.
[I.9.X07](/elementa/neural-networks/recurrence/problems/x07) makes the difference
numerical.

Clipping can control a finite oversized gradient. It cannot reconstruct
information that already vanished, and it cannot make a non-finite
computation reliable. A NaN needs a diagnosis, not a smaller threshold.

## Truncation changes credit assignment

Full backpropagation retains the whole unrolled dependency.
Truncated backpropagation detaches a carried state at a chunk boundary:
the next chunk receives the same value, but treats it as a constant for that
backward pass.

That omits cross-boundary derivatives. The forward model may still remember
earlier inputs, but a later loss cannot teach the detached earlier computations
what to retain across that boundary. The truncated gradient is generally not
the full-sequence gradient.

In [I.9.B05](/elementa/neural-networks/recurrence/problems/b05), the prediction
stays $2.5$ and the loss stays $1.125$, while a one-step truncation changes
$\partial\mathcal L/\partial w$ from $9$ to $7.5$.
For a finite-difference check of the truncated computation, freeze the boundary
state. Recomputing it under every parameter perturbation checks the full graph
instead.

A practical chunk loop starts with the correct stream's state, runs the chunk,
computes the valid-position loss, backpropagates, clips if required, and updates
the parameters. Before the next chunk, detach the state; when the stream ends,
reset it. If weights change between chunks, the carried value was computed
with earlier weights. That is part of this training procedure, not exact
full-sequence differentiation at one fixed parameter setting.

## A different cell is a different path

LSTMs and GRUs learn gates controlling what to retain and replace.
For the modern LSTM carry expression
$c_t=f_t\odot c_{t-1}+i_t\odot\tilde c_t$, holding the gate and candidate
values fixed leaves a direct derivative $\operatorname{diag}(f_t)$.
The total derivative can also include paths through those values.

This direct path explains the motivation, not a promise of perfect memory:
forget factors below one can still attenuate information.
[Chapter I.10](/elementa/neural-networks/gating) owns the gate equations and their
full backward pass. Here the requirement is to understand the failure they
were designed to address.
