## Replace the running state with a window

A recurrent cell reads the previous state. A sequence convolution reads a
window from the preceding layer. If that layer's inputs are already known,
all positions in the next layer can be evaluated together.

For a causal kernel of width $k$ and dilation $d$, the output at position $t$
reads $t,t-d,\ldots,t-(k-1)d$. No later input enters.
Left padding supplies the boundary convention. A centred window instead
reads both sides and is suitable only when that future context is allowed.

The diagram is a centred-window view of receptive-field growth, not a causal
language-model mask. The span calculation is the same; the alignment differs.

Let $R_\ell$ be the receptive-field span after layer $\ell$, with stride one
and no pooling. Each layer extends the span by $(k-1)d_\ell$.
Starting at $R_0=1$ and adding the increments gives

<Eq id="I.9.8" gloss="This is the span in original input positions, for a stride-one stack.">

$$
\begin{aligned}
R_L&=1+(k-1)\sum_{\ell=1}^L d_\ell,\\
d_\ell=2^{\ell-1}
 &\implies R_L=1+(k-1)(2^L-1).
\end{aligned}
$$

</Eq>

Equation (I.9.8) gives $R_L=1+L(k-1)$ with no dilation.
At width three and dilations $1,2,4$, the spans are $3,7,15$.
This schedule covers every offset inside the span. An arbitrary dilation
schedule need not: using only even dilations leaves odd offsets unseen.
[I.9.X10](/elementa/neural-networks/recurrence/problems/x10) distinguishes
span from actual coverage.

The full convolution operation belongs to
[Chapter I.11](/elementa/neural-networks/convolution). Here it is a comparison
of routes by which distant inputs can affect one another.

## Path length is not the same as total work

For a length-$T$ sequence and hidden width $H$, the following comparison fixes
the model and asks what happens as $T$ grows.

| Computation | Sequential stages with known inputs | How distant positions communicate |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Dense nonlinear RNN | $T$ cell steps | A chain with length proportional to separation |
| Width-$k$ dilated convolution | $L$ layers | Through a receptive field that can grow exponentially in $L$ |
| Dense self-attention | One attention layer, apart from internal reductions | A direct allowed query-key connection |

A recurrent hidden-to-hidden multiplication costs $O(TH^2)$ over the sequence.
A width-$k$, $H$-channel convolution costs $O(TkH^2)$ per layer, with $L$ such
layers in a stack. Dense attention's pairwise interaction costs $O(T^2H)$,
in addition to its projections. Parallel stages do not remove this work.

A straightforward dense attention implementation materialises a $T$ by $T$
score array. Implementations that tile the computation can avoid retaining
that whole array, so “attention always uses quadratic memory” is too broad.
The dense pairwise arithmetic remains quadratic in $T$ in this comparison.

These are architectural costs, not a speed benchmark. Dimensions, kernels,
batching and hardware determine the observed runtime.

## Training parallelism does not mean parallel generation

In teacher-forced next-token training, all preceding ground-truth tokens are
already available. A causal convolution or masked-attention layer can process
positions together without reading a future target.

During ordinary autoregressive generation, token $t+1$ depends on the token
sampled at $t$. The next input is not yet available. This dependency remains
for recurrent, convolutional and attention-based decoders.
Caching reduces repeated computation; it does not remove the sampling order.

A bidirectional recurrent encoder is another distinction. It can use both
past and future context for a fully observed sequence, but cannot be inserted
unchanged into a causal predictor. Check the leakage in
[I.9.X09](/elementa/neural-networks/recurrence/problems/x09).

## Why this leads to attention

A fixed-state encoder makes the decoder retrieve source information from one
vector. Bahdanau and colleagues let the decoder select among a collection of
encoder states instead. That addressed the access bottleneck without requiring
the encoder itself to stop being recurrent.

The Transformer then used attention without recurrent sequence transitions.
It changed both information access and the number of sequential training
stages. Those are concrete differences; neither is a theorem that attention
is the best model for every sequence.

For a streaming sensor with a small sufficient state, carrying that state can
be exactly the useful constraint. For a task requiring selective access to
many earlier positions, storing and retrieving separate representations may
be worth its cost. First name what must be remembered and when it must be
available. Then count the work.
