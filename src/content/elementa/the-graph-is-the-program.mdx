## Demonstration

Write a composition as a chain and the chain rule reads off it directly:

$$
L = f_k \circ \dots \circ f_1(x)
\quad \Longrightarrow \quad
\frac{\partial L}{\partial x}
= \frac{\partial f_1}{\partial x}\frac{\partial f_2}{\partial f_1}\cdots\frac{\partial L}{\partial f_k}
$$

which is (I.6.1). Nothing about that expression says which end to start from,
and that freedom is the whole subject: multiply left to right and you have
forward mode, right to left and you have reverse mode. The product is the same
number either way, and the cost is not.

A real network is not a chain, though. A value can feed several consumers, a
weight can be used at several positions, and a skip connection routes one value
past a block to be added on the other side. What all of these have in common is
that the picture is a *graph*: nodes are operations, edges carry values, the
parameters and inputs are sources, and a scalar loss is the sink.

Once it is a graph rather than a chain, (I.6.1) needs restating. The
generalisation is small and it is the only thing to remember:

$$
\bar{v} = \sum_{c \,\in\, \mathrm{consumers}(v)} \bar{c}\,\mat{J}_{v \to c},
\qquad \bar{L} = 1
$$

Each node's adjoint is the sum, over everything it feeds, of that consumer's
adjoint times the local Jacobian between them. The traversal starts at the sink
with $\bar{L} = 1$ and proceeds in reverse topological order, so a node is
visited only once every consumer has been.

## Corollary

The sum is not decoration. It is the multivariate chain rule, and it is what
makes a shared weight work: an embedding matrix used both to look up tokens and
to project logits appears twice in the graph, receives two contributions, and
its gradient is their sum. Replace that sum with an assignment and the gradient
is not noisy — it is the gradient of a different function.

The retention requirement follows from the same equation. $\mat{J}_{v \to c}$ is
evaluated at the value $v$ took on the forward pass, so that value must still
exist when the backward pass arrives. This is the entire difference in memory
between inference and training, and it is why a model that runs comfortably in
one may not fit in the other.
