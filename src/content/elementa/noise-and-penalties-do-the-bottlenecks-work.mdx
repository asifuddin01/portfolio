## Three ways to refuse the copy

With $k\geq d$ the identity is available, and by proposition 1 it is
optimal. Three classical changes to the objective each make it suboptimal
without narrowing the code.

- **Corrupt the input and keep the clean target.** This is the denoising
  autoencoder of Vincent, Larochelle, Bengio and Manzagol (2008).
- **Penalise how sharply the code responds to its input.** This is the
  contractive autoencoder of Rifai and colleagues (2011).
- **Penalise the size of the code.** This is sparse coding in the form
  Olshausen and Field (1996) gave it.

Each is a regulariser in the sense of I.8: among many maps that
reconstruct well, it decides which one is chosen. For a linear autoencoder
the choice has an exact description. Every one of these methods multiplies
each principal direction of the data by a gain between zero and one. The
figure shows the gains for the four points of this chapter, whose
eigenvalues are $4$ and $1$. A bottleneck cuts. The others shrink.

## Noise turns reconstruction into shrinkage

Corrupt each input as $\tilde{\vec x}=\vec x+\vec\varepsilon$, where
$\vec\varepsilon$ is independent of $\vec x$ with mean zero and covariance
$\sigma^2\mat I$. Train a linear map $\mat M$ to return the clean input.
Expanding the square, the cross term between $(\mat M-\mat I)\vec x$ and
$\mat M\vec\varepsilon$ has mean zero, and
$\E\|\mat M\vec\varepsilon\|^2=\sigma^2\operatorname{tr}(\mat M\mat M^{\mathsf T})$:

<Eq id="I.12.7" gloss="Input noise is a ridge penalty on the end-to-end map.">

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\E\big\|\mat M(\vec x+\vec\varepsilon)-\vec x\big\|^2\\
&\;\;=\operatorname{tr}\big((\mat M-\mat I)\mat S(\mat M-\mat I)^{\mathsf T}\big)\\
&\;\;\quad+\sigma^2\|\mat M\|_F^2,\\[6pt]
&\mat M^\star=\mat S(\mat S+\sigma^2\mat I)^{-1}\\
&\;\;=\sum_{i=1}^d\frac{\lambda_i}{\lambda_i+\sigma^2}\,
 \vec u_i\vec u_i^{\mathsf T}.
\end{aligned}
$$

</Eq>

The first identity is the clean reconstruction loss plus weight decay of
strength $\sigma^2$ on the end-to-end map. Only the mean and covariance of
the noise were used. The second sets the gradient
$2(\mat M-\mat I)\mat S+2\sigma^2\mat M$ to zero.

No bottleneck appears in (I.12.7), and the optimum is still not the
identity. A direction with $\lambda_i\gg\sigma^2$ passes almost unchanged.
A direction with $\lambda_i\ll\sigma^2$ is nearly removed, because there
the corrupted input is mostly noise. Principal components make a hard cut
at position $k$; noise makes a soft one at the scale $\sigma^2$. With a
bottleneck as well, the optimum keeps the top $k$ directions and shrinks
them by the same gains.
[I.12.B03](/elementa/neural-networks/autoencoders/problems/b03) works both
cases and the two limits: as $\sigma^2\to0$ the map returns to the
identity, and as $\sigma^2\to\infty$ it outputs the mean.

## The best denoiser points uphill in density

Drop linearity. Among all functions $r$, the expected squared error
$\E\|r(\tilde{\vec x})-\vec x\|^2$ is minimised by a conditional mean,
$r^\star(\tilde{\vec x})=\E[\vec x\mid\tilde{\vec x}]$, by the result of
I.4 for squared error.

For Gaussian noise that mean has a second form. Write $\phi$ for the
density of the noise, a Gaussian with mean zero and covariance
$\sigma^2\mat I$. The corrupted data have density
$p_\sigma(\tilde{\vec x})=\int p(\vec x)\,\phi(\tilde{\vec x}-\vec x)\,d\vec x$.
Differentiating $\phi(\tilde{\vec x}-\vec x)$ with respect to
$\tilde{\vec x}$ multiplies it by $(\vec x-\tilde{\vec x})/\sigma^2$, so

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\nabla p_\sigma(\tilde{\vec x})\\
&\;\;=\frac1{\sigma^2}\int p(\vec x)\,\phi(\tilde{\vec x}-\vec x)\,
 (\vec x-\tilde{\vec x})\,d\vec x\\
&\;\;=\frac{p_\sigma(\tilde{\vec x})}{\sigma^2}
 \big(\E[\vec x\mid\tilde{\vec x}]-\tilde{\vec x}\big).
\end{aligned}
$$

Dividing by $p_\sigma$ gives

<Eq id="I.12.8" gloss="The best denoiser moves a corrupted point up the noised density.">

$$
\E[\vec x\mid\tilde{\vec x}]
 =\tilde{\vec x}+\sigma^2\,\nabla\log p_\sigma(\tilde{\vec x}).
$$

</Eq>

This is I.12.T3. Statisticians know it as Tweedie's formula (Efron 2011).
Vincent (2011) and Alain and Bengio (2014) read it for autoencoders: the
displacement $r^\star(\tilde{\vec x})-\tilde{\vec x}$ of the best denoiser
is $\sigma^2$ times the score of the noised density. A denoising
autoencoder trained to its optimum has therefore estimated a vector field
that points from every corrupted input towards higher density. It has
learned something about the distribution without writing a density down.
Models that generate by denoising repeatedly start from this identity.

For Gaussian data, (I.12.8) is (I.12.7) again. The noised density is
$\mathcal N(\vec 0,\mat S+\sigma^2\mat I)$, its score is
$-(\mat S+\sigma^2\mat I)^{-1}\tilde{\vec x}$, and

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\tilde{\vec x}-\sigma^2(\mat S+\sigma^2\mat I)^{-1}\tilde{\vec x}\\
&\;\;=\mat S(\mat S+\sigma^2\mat I)^{-1}\tilde{\vec x}.
\end{aligned}
$$

The identity also explains a familiar defect. If the clean input is $+1$ or
$-1$ with equal probability, the best denoiser is
$\tanh(\tilde x/\sigma^2)$
([I.12.X08](/elementa/neural-networks/autoencoders/problems/x08)). At
$\tilde x=0$ it returns $0$, which is not a possible input. A conditional
mean of two sharp alternatives lies between them. Squared-error
reconstructions of ambiguous inputs are blurred for this reason, and no
amount of training removes it.

The corruption need not be additive. Vincent and colleagues (2008) set a
random subset of the input's coordinates to zero. The best reconstruction
is still a conditional mean, of the hidden coordinates given the visible
ones, but (I.12.8) no longer applies. Predicting the hidden part of an
input from the rest is the same objective with deletion as the corruption.

## A penalty on the Jacobian says it directly

The contractive autoencoder adds $\beta\|\mat J_f(\vec x)\|_F^2$ at every
training point, where $\mat J_f$ is the encoder's Jacobian. For a sigmoid
code $\vec h=\sigma(\mat W\vec x+\vec b)$ the Jacobian is
$\operatorname{diag}(\vec h\odot(1-\vec h))\,\mat W$, so the penalty is
$\sum_j\big(h_j(1-h_j)\big)^2\|\vec w_j\|^2$ with $\vec w_j$ the $j$-th row
of $\mat W$. It costs one pass over the weights.

Noise imposes nearly the same thing on the whole reconstruction map
$r=g\circ f$. Expand $r(\vec x+\vec\varepsilon)$ to first order as
$r(\vec x)+\mat J_r(\vec x)\vec\varepsilon$. As in (I.12.7),

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\E_{\vec\varepsilon}\big\|r(\vec x+\vec\varepsilon)-\vec x\big\|^2\\
&\;\;=\|r(\vec x)-\vec x\|^2
 +\sigma^2\|\mat J_r(\vec x)\|_F^2\\
&\;\;\quad+o(\sigma^2)
\end{aligned}
$$

near the optimum, where the second-order term of the expansion is
multiplied by the small residual $r(\vec x)-\vec x$ and drops out at this
order (Bishop 1995; Alain and Bengio 2014). For a linear map the relation
is exact and is the first identity of (I.12.7).

So the two methods differ in what they flatten. Denoising penalises the
Jacobian of the reconstruction; the contractive penalty acts on the code.
Both pull against the reconstruction term, which needs the map to vary
along the data in order to tell examples apart. The compromise is a map
that responds to movement along the data and ignores movement away from it.

## Sparsity needs a fixed decoder scale

A sparse autoencoder adds $\beta\sum_j|z_j|$ to the loss, usually with more
code units than inputs, so that each input is rebuilt from a few active
units out of many. Its effect is visible in one coordinate. For a target
$a$, minimise $(a-z)^2+\beta|z|$. For $z>0$ the derivative
$-2(a-z)+\beta$ vanishes at $z=a-\beta/2$, which is positive only if
$a>\beta/2$. The negative side is symmetric, and otherwise $z=0$ is best:

<Eq id="I.12.9" gloss="An L1 penalty zeroes small codes and shortens the rest.">

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\operatorname*{arg\,min}_z\;(a-z)^2+\beta|z|\\
&\;\;=\operatorname{sign}(a)\,\max\!\big(|a|-\tfrac\beta2,\,0\big).
\end{aligned}
$$

</Eq>

Two things follow from (I.12.9). Codes below the threshold are exactly
zero, which is the sparsity. Every surviving code is shortened by
$\beta/2$, which is a bias in its amplitude.

The penalty is only meaningful if the decoder cannot undo it. Divide the
encoder by a constant $c$ and multiply the decoder by $c$. Every
reconstruction is unchanged and the penalty falls by the factor $c$. As $c$
grows the objective approaches the unpenalised loss without reaching it,
and the penalty has bought nothing but large weights. The remedy is to hold
every decoder column at unit norm, or to weight each $|z_j|$ by the norm of
its column.
[I.12.X07](/elementa/neural-networks/autoencoders/problems/x07) computes
both sides. The same scaling defeats a contractive penalty when the
decoder is free. Rifai and colleagues tie the weights, noting that this
rules out a contracting encoder paired with an expanding decoder.

Makhzani and Frey (2013) replace the penalty by a hard rule: keep a fixed
number of the largest code coordinates and zero the rest. That gives
sparsity without the shortening of (I.12.9).

## Width is not capacity

All of these control the map and not only the code. One more quantity
needs control, and it is the oldest in the book.

A decoder with enough freedom can pass its reconstruction surface through
every training point, however narrow the code. In
[I.12.B04](/elementa/neural-networks/autoencoders/problems/b04) a
width-one code and a decoder with two ReLU kinks reconstruct all four
training points exactly: training loss $0$, against $1$ for the principal
axis. On a held-out point from the same rectangle the order reverses, with
a squared error of $4$ against $1$.

A bottleneck bounds the dimension of the surface. The size of the encoder
and decoder, and the amount of data, bound how it bends. Reconstruction
error is a training loss like any other, and by I.1 it must be read on
held-out data.

## Reproduce the gains

~~~python
lam = (4.0, 1.0)                      # eigenvalues of the four-point covariance
noise, decay = 1.0, 0.5
rows = {
    "bottleneck k=1": [1.0, 0.0],
    "noise": [l/(l + noise) for l in lam],
    "weight decay": [1 - decay/l for l in lam],
}
for name, gains in rows.items():
    error = sum((1 - g)**2*l for g, l in zip(gains, lam))
    print(f"{name}: gains {gains}, clean error {error:.4f}")
~~~

It prints gains $(1,0)$, $(0.8,0.5)$ and $(0.875,0.5)$, with clean
reconstruction errors $1.0000$, $0.4100$ and $0.3125$. The noise row is
(I.12.7) and the weight-decay row is (I.12.6). A map with gains $g_i$
leaves the error $\sum_i(1-g_i)^2\lambda_i$ on clean inputs. The two
shrinking methods reconstruct the training data better than the
bottleneck does, and neither has learned to copy.
