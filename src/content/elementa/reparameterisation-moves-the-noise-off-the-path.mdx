## A draw has no derivative

The reconstruction term of the ELBO is an expectation,
$\E_{q_\phi}[f(\vec z)]$ with $f(\vec z)=\log p_\theta(\vec x\mid\vec z)$.
Training needs its gradient with respect to the encoder's parameters
$\phi$, and $\phi$ enters through the distribution being averaged over, not
through $f$. Drawing $\vec z$ and backpropagating stops at the draw: a
sample from $\mathcal N(\mu,\sigma^2)$ is not a differentiable function of
$\mu$ and $\sigma$ as the program is written. The figure shows the break on
its left.

## Write the draw as a function

The same distribution can be produced differently. Draw $\vec\varepsilon$
from $\mathcal N(\vec 0,\mat I)$, which involves no parameter, and compute
$\vec z=\vec\mu+\vec\sigma\odot\vec\varepsilon$. The expectation over
$q_\phi$ becomes an expectation over $\vec\varepsilon$, whose distribution
does not move when $\phi$ does, so the gradient can go inside:

<Eq id="I.13.6" gloss="Moving the noise off the path lets the gradient through the draw.">

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\vec z=\vec\mu_\phi+\vec\sigma_\phi\odot\vec\varepsilon,\\[6pt]
&\nabla_\phi\,\E_{q_\phi}f(\vec z)\\
&\;\;=\E_{\vec\varepsilon}\big[\nabla_\phi
 f(\vec\mu_\phi+\vec\sigma_\phi\odot\vec\varepsilon)\big].
\end{aligned}
$$

</Eq>

In one dimension the derivative with respect to $\mu$ is $\E[f'(z)]$ and
with respect to $\sigma$ is $\E[f'(z)\,\varepsilon]$, since
$\partial z/\partial\mu=1$ and $\partial z/\partial\sigma=\varepsilon$. One
draw of $\varepsilon$ per example gives an unbiased estimate, and the
backward pass of I.6 computes it: $\varepsilon$ is one more input to the
graph. Kingma and Welling (2014) and Rezende, Mohamed and Wierstra (2014)
introduced this for variational autoencoders.

An encoder that predicts $\ell=\log\sigma^2$ adds one link to the chain,
$\sigma=e^{\ell/2}$, so $\partial z/\partial\ell=\tfrac12\sigma\varepsilon$.
[I.13.X02](/elementa/neural-networks/vae/problems/x02) takes one step by
hand.

## The estimator that needs no derivative

There is a second unbiased estimator. Differentiate the integral and use
$\nabla q=q\,\nabla\log q$:

<Eq id="I.13.7" gloss="The score function needs no derivative of f and pays in variance.">

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\nabla_\phi\,\E_{q_\phi}f(\vec z)\\
&\;\;=\int f(\vec z)\,\nabla_\phi q_\phi(\vec z)\,d\vec z\\
&\;\;=\E_{q_\phi}\big[f(\vec z)\,\nabla_\phi\log q_\phi(\vec z)\big].
\end{aligned}
$$

</Eq>

This is the score-function estimator, which Williams (1992) introduced as
REINFORCE. It never differentiates $f$, so $f$ may be a black box or
discontinuous, and $\vec z$ may be discrete. For a Gaussian,
$\partial\log q/\partial\mu=(z-\mu)/\sigma^2=\varepsilon/\sigma$. That is
I.13.T2: two unbiased estimates of one gradient.

## Which one is quieter?

Take $f(z)=z^2$ and $q=\mathcal N(1,0.25)$, so the true derivative with
respect to $\mu$ is $2\mu=2$. The pathwise estimate is $2(\mu+s\varepsilon)$,
with variance $4s^2=1$. The score-function estimate is
$(\mu+s\varepsilon)^2\varepsilon/s$, with variance
$(\mu^4+18\mu^2s^2+15s^4)/s^2-4\mu^2=21.75$.
[I.13.B04](/elementa/neural-networks/vae/problems/b04) derives both.

As the encoder grows confident the difference becomes extreme. At $s=0.01$
the pathwise variance is $0.0004$ and the score function's is $10{,}014$.
The score function divides by $s$; the pathwise estimate multiplies by it.
A trained VAE has narrow encoder distributions and a smooth decoder
log-likelihood, which is the regime where reparameterisation wins.

It does not always win. For $f(z)=\sin(10z)$ under $\mathcal N(0,1)$ the
pathwise estimate $10\cos(10z)$ has variance $50$, the score function
$0.5$, and the true gradient is $10e^{-50}$, zero to any printed precision
([I.13.X10](/elementa/neural-networks/vae/problems/x10)). The pathwise
variance grows with how fast $f$ changes, the score function's with how
sharp $q$ is (Mohamed, Rosca, Figurnov and Mnih 2020).

## Discrete codes

A Bernoulli code cannot be reparameterised this way. Writing
$z=\mathbf 1[u<\pi]$ for uniform $u$ produces the right distribution, but
its derivative with respect to $\pi$ is zero almost everywhere. A
backward pass through it returns a gradient of zero where the true one is
not ([I.13.X08](/elementa/neural-networks/vae/problems/x08)). Discrete
codes need the score function, or a continuous relaxation such as the
Gumbel-softmax of Jang, Gu and Poole (2017), which is reparameterisable
and biased.

## Measure the two variances

~~~python
from math import exp, pi, sqrt

mu, s = 1.0, 0.5
f, df = (lambda z: z*z), (lambda z: 2*z)
grid = [i/1000 for i in range(-8000, 8001)]
weight = [exp(-e*e/2)/sqrt(2*pi)/1000 for e in grid]   # N(0, 1) on a fine grid

def moments(estimate):
    m1 = sum(w*estimate(e) for w, e in zip(weight, grid))
    m2 = sum(w*estimate(e)**2 for w, e in zip(weight, grid))
    return round(m1, 6), round(m2 - m1*m1, 6)

print("pathwise", moments(lambda e: df(mu + s*e)))
print("score   ", moments(lambda e: f(mu + s*e)*e/s))
~~~

It prints <code>pathwise (2.0, 1.0)</code> and
<code>score (2.0, 21.75)</code>: the same mean, the correct gradient, and
variances that differ by a factor of $21.75$. Set <code>mu, s = 0.0, 1.0</code>
and replace <code>f</code> and <code>df</code> by $\sin(10z)$ and
$10\cos(10z)$: the variances become $50$ and $0.5$, and the order
reverses.
