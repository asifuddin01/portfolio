The three-rung ladder — association, intervention, counterfactual — is the whole
book, and everything else is illustration. That is not a criticism. Most
statistics education stops at rung one and never tells you there are two more.

What the book does well is refuse to be diplomatic. Pearl is arguing that a
generation of statisticians treated causal language as unrigorous and thereby
made it impossible to say true things. The historical chapters on Wright's path
analysis and the tobacco debates are there to show the cost of that refusal in
lives.

Where it frustrates: the do-calculus is presented as more automatic than it is.
In practice the hard part is never the algebra, it is defending the graph. Every
arrow is an assumption you cannot test from the data, and the book is much
lighter on how to earn those arrows than on what to do once you have them.

I read it while starting HCGT-PG, where the graph is over genes and the
intervention is a knockdown. The thing that stayed with me is rung two's demand:
if you cannot say what experiment your estimate predicts, you have not made a
causal claim. That sentence is doing a lot of work in my proposal.
