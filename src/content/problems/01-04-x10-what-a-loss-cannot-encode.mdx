The chapter's open exercise. Every loss in this chapter has the form
$\frac1n\sum_i \ell(\hat{y}_i, y_i)$ — a mean of a function of one prediction and
one target. Determine what objectives that form structurally cannot express, and
give a worked case for at least two of them.

<Hint>
Ask what happens to $\ell$ if you shuffle the examples, or if you change the
prediction for one example while holding the rest fixed.
</Hint>

<SolutionToggle>

**The structural constraint.** Two properties follow from the form alone, before
any particular $\ell$ is chosen.

*Permutation invariance.* The mean is unchanged by reordering, so no objective
that depends on the order or grouping of examples can be expressed.

*Separability.* $\partial L/\partial \hat{y}_i$ depends on example $i$ alone. No
objective in which the right prediction for one example depends on the
predictions made for others can be expressed.

Everything below is a consequence of one of these two.

### Case 1 — a ranking objective (separability fails)

**The objective.** Rank documents so relevant ones come above irrelevant ones.
What matters is the *relative order* of scores, not their values.

**Why it cannot be written per example.** Take two documents with true relevance
$1$ and $0$ and scores $\hat{y}_A = 0.6$, $\hat{y}_B = 0.4$. The ranking is
correct. Now consider $0.9$ and $0.8$: also correct. And $0.3$, $0.2$: also
correct. A per-example loss must assign each of these six predictions a value
based on that prediction alone, yet the quantity of interest — is $A$ above
$B$? — is identical in all three.

Worse, it can be destroyed by changing only $B$: with $\hat{y}_A = 0.6$ fixed,
$\hat{y}_B = 0.4$ is correct and $\hat{y}_B = 0.7$ is not. So the *correct value*
for $B$ depends on $A$'s prediction, which separability forbids.

**Worked numbers.** Under per-example squared loss with targets $(1, 0)$:

$$
(0.6, 0.4): \ \tfrac12\big[(0.4)^2 + (0.4)^2\big] = 0.1600
$$
$$
(0.9, 0.8): \ \tfrac12\big[(0.1)^2 + (0.8)^2\big] = 0.3250
$$

The loss prefers the first, though both rank correctly and the second is more
confident. And:

$$
(0.5, 0.5): \ \tfrac12\big[(0.5)^2 + (0.5)^2\big] = 0.2500
$$

which ranks *incorrectly* (a tie) yet scores better than $(0.9, 0.8)$, which
ranks correctly. **The loss and the objective disagree in sign.**

**What is done instead.** Pairwise losses of the form $\ell(\hat{y}_A - \hat{y}_B)$,
which are per-*pair* rather than per-example — a different form, with $O(n^2)$
terms and a gradient that couples examples.

### Case 2 — a constraint across examples (permutation invariance fails)

**The objective.** Predicted probabilities should be calibrated: among examples
predicted at $0.7$, about $70\%$ should be positive.

**Why it cannot be written per example.** Calibration is a statement about a
*set* of predictions. For any single prediction there is no fact of the matter —
$p = 0.7$ on one example with label $1$ is neither calibrated nor miscalibrated.
The quantity requires binning, and binning requires seeing the other examples.

**Worked numbers.** Ten examples, all predicted $p = 0.7$, of which $7$ are
positive. Perfectly calibrated. Cross-entropy:

$$
\overline{L} = \frac{7(-\log 0.7) + 3(-\log 0.3)}{10} = \frac{7(0.3567) + 3(1.2040)}{10} = \frac{2.4969 + 3.6120}{10} = 0.6109
$$

Now a model predicting $p = 1.0$ for the seven positives and $p = 0.0$ for the
three negatives — also perfectly calibrated, and with loss $0$. And one
predicting $0.7$ for all ten when only $3$ are positive — badly miscalibrated —
gives

$$
\frac{3(0.3567) + 7(1.2040)}{10} = 0.9498
$$

So the loss *does* respond to calibration here, but only through accuracy: it
cannot distinguish "well calibrated and uncertain" from "poorly calibrated",
because it never sees a group.

### Case 3 — fairness across a subgroup (both fail)

The objective "the error rate on group A must not exceed group B's by more than
$\varepsilon$" is a constraint on two *aggregates*. A per-example loss can
approximate it with group weights, as I.4.B05 does — but weights control expected
contribution, not a realised gap, and the constraint can be violated while every
weight is respected. This is the same gap between a surrogate and a target that
I.4.X06 exhibited for accuracy.

### The strongest true claim

> A per-example mean is a **separable, permutation-invariant** objective. Any
> quantity of interest that is genuinely a property of the *set* of predictions —
> an ordering, a rate within a group, a distributional match, a constraint
> between subgroups — is not in that class, and can only be approached by a
> surrogate whose disagreement with the target has to be measured rather than
> assumed.

**Why the form is used anyway.** Separability is what makes the gradient
computable in one pass and the loss decomposable over a minibatch. Give it up and
the gradient of one example requires the others, minibatching becomes an
approximation rather than an identity, and the whole training loop changes shape.
That is a real cost, and it is why the answer is nearly always a surrogate plus a
separately reported metric — which is exactly the discipline Chapter VIII.5 is
about.
