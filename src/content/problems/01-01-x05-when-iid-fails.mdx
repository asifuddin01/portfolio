The first assumption of this chapter is that examples are drawn independently
from one fixed distribution. Construct a dataset where that fails, show
concretely that the reported test accuracy is then not an estimate of anything
the modeller wants, and state the smallest repair.

<Hint>
Independence is a property of the *sampling*, not of the numbers. Ask what
happens when a single underlying object contributes several rows.
</Hint>

<SolutionToggle>

**The construction.** Take $100$ patients. From each, take $10$ photographs of
the same skin lesion, from slightly different angles: $1{,}000$ images, each
labelled benign or malignant according to that patient's biopsy.

Split the $1{,}000$ images uniformly at random, $800$ for training and $200$ for
testing.

**Why the assumption fails.** The ten images from one patient are not
independent draws. They share a lesion, a camera, a skin tone, a lighting
condition. Under a uniform split, a given patient's images land on *both* sides:
with $800/1000$ training, the chance that a particular patient has all ten
images in test is $(0.2)^{10} \approx 1.0 \times 10^{-7}$. In expectation,
essentially every patient in the test set also appears in training.

**What the model can do.** Nothing about the disease needs to be learned. A
model that recognises *the patient* — from a freckle, the skin tone, a
background corner of the image — can recall that patient's label from training
and apply it to the test images. It will score near-perfectly.

Concretely: suppose the model learns patient identity with $95\%$ accuracy and
nothing else. Test accuracy is then about $95\%$, against a base rate of, say,
$50\%$. The reported number looks like a strong result.

**What it is an estimate of.** It estimates performance on *new photographs of
patients already seen*. That is a real quantity, and it is almost never the one
anyone wants. The quantity wanted is performance on a new patient, and the
experiment contains no evidence about it whatsoever — a model with $95\%$
reported accuracy may be at chance on a new patient, and this design cannot
distinguish the two cases.

**The smallest repair.** Split by patient, not by image. Assign each of the
$100$ patients wholly to train or wholly to test. The test set then contains no
patient the model has seen, and the number estimates the quantity of interest.

The cost is that the effective sample size falls from $1{,}000$ to $100$, so the
confidence interval widens by roughly $\sqrt{10} \approx 3.2\times$
(<ApparatusRef ref="0.ST.02" />). That is not a loss — the narrow interval was
never real. The honest number is the wider one.

**The general form.** Wherever rows share a latent generator — patient, site,
author, session, scanner, document — independence fails, and the repair is
always to split at the level of the generator. Chapter VIII.3 makes this
quantitative; Proposition I.1.P05 is where it starts.
