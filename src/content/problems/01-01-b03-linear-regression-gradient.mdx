<Statement>
Derive the gradient of the squared loss for one-dimensional linear regression
through the origin, evaluate it at $w = 1$ on three data points, and confirm
that the parameter at which the gradient vanishes is the one the normal
equation gives.
</Statement>

<Given>
The model $\hat{y} = wx$, with a single scalar parameter $w$. The loss

$$\loss(w) = \frac{1}{2n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(w x_i - y_i\right)^2$$

and the three points

$$(x_1,y_1) = (1, 2), \quad (x_2,y_2) = (2, 4), \quad (x_3,y_3) = (3, 5)$$
</Given>

<Find>
A closed form for $\mathrm{d}\loss/\mathrm{d}w$; its value at $w = 1$; the
minimiser $w^\star$; and a demonstration that the two agree.
</Find>

<Strategy>
Differentiate under the sum, then set the result to zero. Both steps are legal
here because the sum is finite and each term is differentiable everywhere —
worth saying once, because for the absolute-error loss the second step is not
available.
</Strategy>

<Solution>

**Step 1 — differentiate one term.** Write $r_i = w x_i - y_i$ for the $i$-th
residual. By the chain rule (<ApparatusRef ref="0.MC.03" />), with
$\partial r_i / \partial w = x_i$:

$$\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}w}\,\tfrac12 r_i^2 = r_i \cdot \frac{\mathrm{d} r_i}{\mathrm{d}w} = x_i\,(w x_i - y_i)$$

The factor $\tfrac12$ in the loss exists precisely to cancel the $2$ that
differentiating a square produces. It is a convenience, not a modelling choice,
and it changes no minimiser.

**Step 2 — sum.** Differentiation is linear, so the derivative of the average
is the average of the derivatives:

$$\frac{\mathrm{d}\loss}{\mathrm{d}w} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i\,(w x_i - y_i) \tag{I.1.5}$$

Read what this says: each point pulls on $w$ in proportion to its own $x_i$ and
to how wrong the prediction currently is. A point with $x_i = 0$ exerts no pull
at all, whatever its target.

**Step 3 — evaluate at $w = 1$.** The three residuals are

$$
r_1 = (1)(1) - 2 = -1, \quad
r_2 = (1)(2) - 4 = -2, \quad
r_3 = (1)(3) - 5 = -2
$$

so

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}\loss}{\mathrm{d}w}\bigg|_{w=1}
= \frac{1}{3}\Big[(1)(-1) + (2)(-2) + (3)(-2)\Big]
= \frac{-1 - 4 - 6}{3}
= \frac{-11}{3}
= -3.6667
$$

Negative, so increasing $w$ decreases the loss. That matches the residuals:
every prediction is below its target.

**Step 4 — set the gradient to zero.** Because the loss is a quadratic in a
single variable with a positive leading coefficient, its unique stationary
point is its minimum.

$$
\frac{1}{n}\sum_i x_i(w x_i - y_i) = 0
\;\Longleftrightarrow\;
w\sum_i x_i^2 = \sum_i x_i y_i
\;\Longleftrightarrow\;
w^\star = \frac{\sum_i x_i y_i}{\sum_i x_i^2}
$$

This is the normal equation for the through-origin case. It is not a separate
result to be looked up: it is what step 2 becomes when set to zero.

**Step 5 — evaluate.**

$$
\sum_i x_i y_i = (1)(2) + (2)(4) + (3)(5) = 2 + 8 + 15 = 25
$$

$$
\sum_i x_i^2 = 1 + 4 + 9 = 14
$$

$$w^\star = \frac{25}{14} = 1.7857$$

</Solution>

<Answer>
$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}\loss}{\mathrm{d}w} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i x_i (w x_i - y_i),
\qquad
\frac{\mathrm{d}\loss}{\mathrm{d}w}\bigg|_{w=1} = -\frac{11}{3} = -3.6667,
\qquad
w^\star = \frac{25}{14} = 1.7857
$$

The gradient is a scalar in units of loss per unit $w$; $w^\star$ is
dimensionless here because $x$ and $y$ share units.
</Answer>

<CheckSanity>
**The gradient vanishes at the claimed optimum.** Substituting $w^\star$ into
(I.1.5) gives $0.0000$ to four decimal places — which is the definition of the
optimum, computed independently of the closed form that produced it.

**The loss falls.** $\loss(1) = 1.5000$ and $\loss(w^\star) = 0.0595$. If the
"optimum" had a higher loss than an arbitrary starting point, the sign of the
derivation would be wrong somewhere.

**The answer is bracketed by the per-point ratios.** Each point alone would
give $y_i/x_i$: that is $2$, $2$ and $1.667$. The least-squares fit,
$1.7857$, lies inside $[1.667, 2]$ — as any weighted compromise must. A value
outside that interval would mean an arithmetic slip.
</CheckSanity>

<Breaks>
The step from "gradient is zero" to "this is the minimum" uses convexity, and
convexity here comes from the model being *linear in the parameter* — not from
the loss being squared. Keep the squared loss but make the model $\hat{y} = wx$
with $w$ replaced by $\sigma(v)$ for a new parameter $v$, and the loss in $v$ is
no longer convex: stationary points can be maxima or saddles. Every network
after Chapter I.5 is in that second regime, and this is the last chapter in
which "set the derivative to zero" is a solution method rather than a hope.
</Breaks>
