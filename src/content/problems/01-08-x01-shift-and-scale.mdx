For $x=(-1,0,1)$ and $\varepsilon=1/3$, compute the standardised vector.
Then replace $x$ by $x+10$ and by $2x$. Which transformation leaves the result
unchanged? Derive the statement for a general positive multiplier $a$.

<Hint>
The variance is multiplied by $a^2$, but epsilon is not.
</Hint>

<SolutionToggle>

The original mean is zero, variance is $2/3$ and denominator is one, so the
standardised vector is $(-1,0,1)$. Adding ten changes the mean to ten and
leaves the centred values unchanged. The result is still $(-1,0,1)$.

For $2x=(-2,0,2)$, the variance is $8/3$ and the denominator is $\sqrt3$.
The result is $(-2/\sqrt3,0,2/\sqrt3)$, approximately
$(-1.1547,0,1.1547)$, which differs from the original.

For $a>0$,

$$
\frac{a(x_i-\mu)}{\sqrt{a^2v+\varepsilon}}
=\frac{x_i-\mu}{\sqrt{v+\varepsilon/a^2}}.
$$

Shift invariance is exact for fixed epsilon. Positive scale invariance is exact
only when epsilon is zero with positive variance, or in special degenerate
cases such as a constant group. It is an approximation when both relevant
epsilon-to-variance ratios are small. A negative multiplier also changes the
sign and cannot be folded into this positive-scale identity without that sign.

</SolutionToggle>
