<Statement>
Take a fixed number of ReLU units in the plane and count the linear regions two
arrangements of them can reach. Establish the growth rate in each case, evaluate
both at the same budget, and state the limit that the comparison is really
about.
</Statement>

<Given>
Input dimension $d = 2$. The two bounds of the chapter:

$$
R_1(n, d) \;\le\; \sum_{i=0}^{d}\binom{n}{i}
\tag{I.5.4}
$$

for one hidden layer of $n$ units, and

$$
R_L(n, d) \;\ge\; \left\lfloor \tfrac{n}{d} \right\rfloor^{\,d(L-1)} \sum_{j=0}^{d}\binom{n}{j}
\tag{I.5.5}
$$

for $L$ hidden layers of $n$ units each, valid when $n \ge d$.
</Given>

<Find>
The closed form of (I.5.4) at $d = 2$ and its growth rate in $n$; the region
counts for thirty units as $1 \times 30$ and as $3 \times 10$; the same for
sixty units at three depths; and the asymptotic statement the table is
evidence for.
</Find>

<Strategy>
Evaluate before generalising. The closed form at $d = 2$ is three terms, the
numbers are small enough to check by hand, and the growth rate is then visible
rather than asserted.
</Strategy>

<Solution>

**Step 1 — one hidden layer in the plane.**

$$\sum_{i=0}^{2}\binom{n}{i} = 1 + n + \frac{n(n-1)}{2}$$

so $R_1 = \Theta(n^2)$. Two units give 4 regions, three give 7, ten give 56, one
hundred give 5051. Doubling the width roughly quadruples the pieces: growth is
polynomial of degree $d$, and in the plane that degree is two.

**Step 2 — the same thirty units, two ways.** One layer of thirty:

$$1 + 30 + \frac{30 \cdot 29}{2} = 1 + 30 + 435 = 466$$

Three layers of ten, using (I.5.5) with $\lfloor 10/2 \rfloor = 5$:

$$5^{\,2 \cdot 2} \cdot \left(1 + 10 + 45\right) = 625 \cdot 56 = 35\,000$$

A factor of $75.1$, at the same unit count and very nearly the same parameter
count.

**Step 3 — sixty units, three ways.**

| arrangement | regions |
|---|---|
| $1$ layer $\times\ 60$ | at most $1\,831$ |
| $2$ layers $\times\ 30$ | at least $104\,850$ |
| $6$ layers $\times\ 10$ | at least $546\,875\,000$ |

The last is not a larger network. It is the same sixty units, stacked.

**Step 4 — the limit the table is about.** Read the two bounds as functions of
the budget. Hold depth fixed and grow width: $R_1 = \Theta(n^d)$, polynomial.
Hold width fixed and grow depth: the exponent $d(L-1)$ carries $L$, so
$R_L = \Omega\!\left(c^{\,L}\right)$ with $c = \lfloor n/d\rfloor^{d}$,
exponential.

$$\lim_{L \to \infty} \frac{R_L(n, d)}{R_1(nL, d)} = \infty$$

for any fixed $n > d$: spending a growing budget on depth outruns spending it on
width, eventually and then permanently. That is the precise sense of "depth
multiplies what width adds", and it is a statement about a limit rather than
about any particular architecture.

</Solution>

<Answer>
At $d = 2$, one hidden layer of $n$ units reaches at most $1 + n + n(n-1)/2$
regions, which is $\Theta(n^2)$.

Thirty units: $466$ as one layer, at least $35\,000$ as three layers of ten — a
factor of $75.1$. Sixty units: $1\,831$, $104\,850$, $546\,875\,000$ at depths
$1$, $2$ and $6$.

Width buys regions polynomially and depth buys them exponentially, so the ratio
diverges as the budget grows.
</Answer>

<CheckNumeric snippet="i-5-b07-region-counts.py" verified>
```python
def shallow(n, d):                       # Zaslavsky 1975, one hidden layer
    return sum(comb(n, i) for i in range(d + 1))

def deep(n, d, L):                       # Montufar et al. 2014, L hidden layers
    return (n // d) ** (d * (L - 1)) * shallow(n, d)
```
Prints the shallow table for $n = 1 \ldots 100$, the thirty-unit comparison with
its ratio, and the three sixty-unit arrangements.
</CheckNumeric>

<CheckSanity>
**Small cases are checkable by drawing.** One line cuts the plane into 2; two
crossing lines into 4; three lines in general position into 7. The formula gives
$2, 4, 7$. A fourth line meets the other three in three points and so adds four
regions, giving 11 — which is what $1 + 4 + 6$ says.

**The deep bound reduces to the shallow one at $L = 1$.** The exponent
$d(L-1)$ becomes zero, the factor becomes 1, and (I.5.5) collapses to (I.5.4).
A bound that did not do this would be inconsistent.

**The bounds point in the directions they should.** (I.5.4) is an upper bound
and (I.5.5) a lower one, so the comparison in Step 2 is between the *best* the
shallow network could do and the *worst* the deep construction achieves. The
factor of 75 is therefore a conservative statement of the gap, which is the only
direction in which such a comparison is worth anything.
</CheckSanity>

<Breaks>
Region counts measure how finely a network can chop its input space, and that is
not what anyone wants from a model. Three limits, in increasing order of how
often they are forgotten.

**The counts are extremal.** A trained network realises far fewer regions than
its architecture allows, and the ones it realises cluster near the data rather
than spreading evenly.

**Pieces are not accuracy.** A target needing few pieces can still be
approximated badly, and a network with many regions may spend nearly all of them
where no data lies.

**The bound assumes $n \ge d$.** A layer narrower than the input is not governed
by region counting at all but by rank: it has already discarded a subspace, and
no depth after it recovers what was lost. That failure is I.5.X04, and its
general form is the minimal-width theorem I.5.T4.
</Breaks>
