A dataset of 1000 examples is sorted by label — all 500 of class 0, then all 500
of class 1 — and read in batches of 100 without shuffling. Show that the
resulting gradient estimate is biased, that no learning rate corrects it, and
what the loss curve looks like.

<Hint>
What is the expectation of the gradient over the batches actually presented,
against the expectation over a random batch? Compare each individual batch, not
the average over an epoch.
</Hint>

<SolutionToggle>

**The unbiasedness that is assumed.** With $B$ examples drawn uniformly at
random, the estimator of (I.7.2) satisfies
$\mathbb{E}[\hat{g}_B] = \nabla\mathcal{L}$ — the batch gradient is the full
gradient plus zero-mean noise. Every convergence argument for stochastic descent
begins here.

**What sorting does.** Batches 1 to 5 contain only class 0; batches 6 to 10 only
class 1. Batch $k$'s gradient is

$$
\hat{g}_k = \nabla\mathcal{L}_{\text{class }0} \quad (k \le 5),
\qquad
\hat{g}_k = \nabla\mathcal{L}_{\text{class }1} \quad (k > 5)
$$

Each is the exact gradient of a *different* objective — the loss on one class —
and neither equals $\nabla\mathcal{L}$. The error is not zero-mean noise around
the right answer; it is the right answer to the wrong question, five times in a
row.

**Why no learning rate corrects it.** A scalar multiplies the direction and
cannot change it. Every one of the first five steps moves towards the minimiser
of the class-0 loss, which for a classifier is roughly "predict class 0 always".
Shrinking $\eta$ makes the journey there slower, not different.

**What the curve looks like.** Loss falls through batches 1–5 as the model
learns to predict class 0, then jumps sharply at batch 6 and falls again as it
unlearns that and predicts class 1, then jumps at the start of the next epoch. A
sawtooth with period one epoch, its teeth aligned to the label boundary. The
average over an epoch is unbiased — the two halves do cancel — but the parameters
never sit at that average, and with momentum the buffer arrives at each boundary
carrying ten steps of exactly the wrong direction.

**Why it is easy to hit.** Nothing errors. The data loads, the loss decreases
within each run of batches, and the sawtooth is easy to read as "needs a lower
learning rate" — which makes it worse, by lengthening the time spent on each
side. The fix is one flag, and the diagnosis is the hard part.

**The general statement.** Any correlation between batch membership and the
gradient breaks the assumption: sorting by label, by length, by acquisition date,
by hospital site. The last is the one that matters in the medical-imaging work of
Book VIII — batches that are accidentally single-site are not samples of the
population the model is meant to serve, and the bias survives every learning
rate, every optimiser and every schedule.
