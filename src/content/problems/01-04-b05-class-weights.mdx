<Statement>
A binary dataset holds $950$ negatives and $50$ positives. Derive the class
weights that make the two classes contribute equal gradient mass, compute them,
verify the loss scale is unchanged, and compare with the effective-number
weighting of Cui et al. (2019).
</Statement>

<Given>
$n_- = 950$, $n_+ = 50$, so $N = 1000$ and $K = 2$. The weighted empirical risk is

$$
\hat{R}(\theta) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} w_{c(i)}\;\ell\big(f(x_i;\theta), y_i\big)
$$

where $c(i)$ is example $i$'s class. Assume each example's loss has comparable
magnitude, so that a class's contribution is proportional to its count times its
weight.
</Given>

<Find>
Weights $w_-$ and $w_+$ equalising the two contributions; their ratio; a check
that the mean weight is $1$; and the same quantities under effective-number
weighting at $\beta = 0.999$.
</Find>

<Strategy>
Write the condition "the two classes contribute equally" as one equation, add
the normalisation "the average weight is one" as a second, and solve the pair.
Two conditions, two unknowns — the weights are then determined, not chosen.
</Strategy>

<Solution>

**Step 1 — the unweighted imbalance.** Without weights, class $c$ contributes
$n_c$ of the $N$ terms, so its share of the gradient is

$$
\frac{n_-}{N} = \frac{950}{1000} = 95\%, \qquad \frac{n_+}{N} = \frac{50}{1000} = 5\%
$$

The negatives outvote the positives nineteen to one. A model that predicts
"negative" always achieves $95\%$ accuracy and a low loss, and the gradient
pushing it away from that solution is one-nineteenth of the gradient holding it
there.

**Step 2 — the equalisation condition.** Class $c$'s weighted contribution is
$n_c w_c$. Requiring the two to be equal:

$$n_- w_- = n_+ w_+ \tag{i}$$

**Step 3 — the normalisation condition.** Requiring the mean weight over the
dataset to be $1$, so the weighted loss has the same scale as the unweighted one
and the learning rate need not be retuned:

$$\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} w_{c(i)} = \frac{n_- w_- + n_+ w_+}{N} = 1 \tag{ii}$$

**Step 4 — solve.** Substitute (i) into (ii). Writing $M$ for the common value
$n_-w_- = n_+w_+$:

$$\frac{M + M}{N} = 1 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad 2M = N \quad\Longrightarrow\quad M = \frac{N}{2}$$

Then from $n_c w_c = M$ for each class,

$$
\boxed{\ w_c = \frac{N}{K\,n_c}\ }
\tag{I.4.6}
$$

with $K = 2$ classes. Note the derivation generalises unchanged to $K$ classes:
$K$ equal contributions summing to $N$ gives $M = N/K$.

**Step 5 — evaluate.**

$$
w_- = \frac{1000}{2 \times 950} = \frac{1000}{1900} = 0.5263
$$

$$
w_+ = \frac{1000}{2 \times 50} = \frac{1000}{100} = 10.0000
$$

$$
\frac{w_+}{w_-} = \frac{10.0000}{0.5263} = 19.0000
$$

**The ratio equals the imbalance ratio**, $950/50 = 19$, which it must:
dividing (i) by $n_+w_-$ gives $w_+/w_- = n_-/n_+$ directly.

**Step 6 — check both conditions.**

*Equal contributions.* $n_-w_- = 950 \times 0.5263 = 500.0$ and
$n_+w_+ = 50 \times 10.0000 = 500.0$. Equal ✓

*Unit mean weight.* $(500.0 + 500.0)/1000 = 1.0000$ ✓ — so a loss of $0.5$
before weighting is a loss of about $0.5$ after, and the learning rate carries
over.

**Step 7 — effective-number weighting.** The inverse-frequency weight assumes
each example contributes independent information. Cui et al. argue that examples
of the same class overlap, so the $n$-th example of a class adds less than the
first. Modelling the *effective* number of examples as a geometric sum,

$$
E_n = \frac{1 - \beta^{\,n}}{1 - \beta},
\qquad\text{and weighting } w_c \propto \frac{1}{E_{n_c}} = \frac{1-\beta}{1-\beta^{\,n_c}}
$$

The parameter $\beta \in [0,1)$ says how fast the overlap sets in: $\beta = 0$
gives $E_n = 1$ for all $n$ (every example redundant beyond the first, so uniform
weights), and $\beta \to 1$ gives $E_n \to n$ (no overlap, recovering inverse
frequency).

At $\beta = 0.999$:

$$
\beta^{950} = 0.999^{950} = 0.3873
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\frac{1-\beta}{1-\beta^{950}} = \frac{0.001}{0.6127} = 1.630144\times10^{-3}
$$

$$
\beta^{50} = 0.999^{50} = 0.9512
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\frac{1-\beta}{1-\beta^{50}} = \frac{0.001}{0.0488} = 2.049417\times10^{-2}
$$

Normalising so the two average to $1$ across classes:

$$w_- = 0.1474, \qquad w_+ = 1.8526, \qquad \frac{w_+}{w_-} = 12.5720$$

**Step 8 — compare.**

| Scheme | $w_-$ | $w_+$ | ratio |
|---|---|---|---|
| none | $1.0000$ | $1.0000$ | $1.00$ |
| inverse frequency | $0.5263$ | $10.0000$ | $19.00$ |
| effective number, $\beta = 0.999$ | $0.1474$ | $1.8526$ | $12.57$ |

Effective-number weighting is **less aggressive**: $12.57$ against $19$. Its
argument is that the $950$ negatives are not $950$ independent facts, so
down-weighting them to one-nineteenth over-corrects. Whether that is right is an
empirical question about the data, and $\beta$ is the knob that encodes the
answer.

</Solution>

<Answer>
$$
w_c = \frac{N}{K n_c}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
w_- = 0.5263,\qquad w_+ = 10.0000,\qquad \frac{w_+}{w_-} = 19
$$

Each class then contributes $500.0$ of weighted mass, and the mean weight is
exactly $1.0000$, so the loss scale is preserved.

Effective-number weighting at $\beta = 0.999$ gives $w_- = 0.1474$,
$w_+ = 1.8526$, a ratio of $12.5720$ — a deliberately weaker correction.
</Answer>

<CheckSanity>
**A balanced dataset gives unit weights.** With $n_- = n_+ = 500$,
$w_c = 1000/(2 \times 500) = 1$ for both. A weighting scheme that changed
anything on balanced data would be wrong, and this one does not.

**The ratio is forced by the counts alone.** $w_+/w_- = n_-/n_+ = 19$ follows
from (i) without reference to $N$ or $K$. So the *ratio* is a property of the
imbalance and the *scale* is a property of the normalisation — two independent
choices that are easy to confuse.

**The two limits of $\beta$ behave.** At $\beta = 0$: $E_n = 1$ for every $n$, so
both weights are equal and the ratio is $1$ — no correction. As $\beta \to 1$:
$E_n \to n$ by L'Hôpital, so $w_c \propto 1/n_c$ and the ratio approaches $19$ —
inverse frequency. The computed $12.57$ lies between $1$ and $19$ as it must.

**Dimensional check.** $N/(Kn_c)$ is a count over a count, so weights are
dimensionless and the weighted loss carries the loss's own units. A weighting
scheme with units would be a rescaling in disguise.
</CheckSanity>

<Breaks>
Step 2's premise is that a class's gradient contribution is proportional to its
count. That holds when every example's loss has comparable magnitude — true at
initialisation, and false soon after.

Once the model has learned to classify the majority easily, those $950$ examples
have small losses and, by (I.4.4), small gradients $p - y$. Their *actual*
contribution collapses well below $95\%$, and the fixed weight $w_- = 0.5263$
keeps suppressing them anyway. **Static weights correct a static imbalance in a
dynamic quantity.**

That observation is precisely the argument for focal loss (Lin et al., 2017),
which multiplies each example's loss by $(1 - p_t)^{\gamma}$ — a factor computed
from the *current* prediction rather than from the class count. It down-weights
easy examples whichever class they belong to, and needs no counts at all.
</Breaks>
