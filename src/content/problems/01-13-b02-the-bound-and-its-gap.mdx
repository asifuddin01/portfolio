<Statement>
Derive the ELBO identity, derive the KL divergence of a Gaussian code from
the standard normal and its derivatives, and show that the encoder that
maximises the ELBO is the posterior.
</Statement>

<Given>
A model $p(\vec z)\,p(\vec x\mid\vec z)$ with evidence $p(\vec x)>0$, and a
distribution $q(\vec z\mid\vec x)$ that is positive exactly where the
posterior is. For the last part, the linear model of
[I.13.B01](/elementa/neural-networks/vae/problems/b01).
</Given>

<Find>
(a) $\log p(\vec x)-\operatorname{ELBO}(q)$ as a single divergence.
(b) $\KL(\mathcal N(\mu,s^2)\,\|\,\mathcal N(0,1))$ and its derivatives with
respect to $\mu$ and $\log s^2$.
(c) The $q=\mathcal N(m,v)$ that maximises the ELBO of the linear model, and
the ELBO there.
</Find>

<Strategy>
For (a), write $\log p(\vec x)$ as an average under $q$ of something that
does not depend on $\vec z$. For (b), average the log-ratio of densities.
For (c), differentiate the closed-form ELBO.
</Strategy>

<Solution>

**(a)** Bayes' rule gives
$p(\vec x)=p(\vec x,\vec z)/p(\vec z\mid\vec x)$ for every $\vec z$ with
positive posterior. The left side does not depend on $\vec z$, so it
equals its average under $q$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\log p(\vec x)\\
&\;=\E_q\Big[\log\frac{p(\vec x,\vec z)}{p(\vec z\mid\vec x)}\Big]\\
&\;=\E_q\Big[\log\frac{p(\vec x,\vec z)}{q(\vec z\mid\vec x)}\Big]\\
&\;\quad+\E_q\Big[\log\frac{q(\vec z\mid\vec x)}{p(\vec z\mid\vec x)}\Big].
\end{aligned}
$$

The first average is the ELBO and the second is
$\KL(q(\vec z\mid\vec x)\,\|\,p(\vec z\mid\vec x))$. Splitting
$p(\vec x,\vec z)=p(\vec x\mid\vec z)\,p(\vec z)$ in the first gives the
form in (I.13.4), with $-\KL(q\,\|\,p(\vec z))$ as its second term.
Since a KL divergence is non-negative and zero only for equal
distributions, the ELBO is a lower bound, tight exactly at the posterior.

**(b)** The log-densities are

$$
\begin{aligned}
\log q(z)&=-\tfrac12\log2\pi s^2\\
 &\quad-\frac{(z-\mu)^2}{2s^2},\\
\log p(z)&=-\tfrac12\log2\pi-\frac{z^2}{2}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Under $q$, $(z-\mu)^2$ averages to $s^2$ and $z^2$ to $\mu^2+s^2$.
Therefore

$$
\begin{aligned}
\KL&=-\tfrac12\log s^2-\tfrac12\\
 &\quad+\tfrac12(\mu^2+s^2)\\
 &=\tfrac12\big(\mu^2+s^2\\
 &\qquad-1-\log s^2\big).
\end{aligned}
$$

This is (I.13.5) for one coordinate. With $\ell=\log s^2$, so that
$s^2=e^\ell$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\partial\KL}{\partial\mu}&=\mu,\\[2pt]
\frac{\partial\KL}{\partial\ell}&=\tfrac12(s^2-1).
\end{aligned}
$$

Both vanish only at $\mu=0$, $s^2=1$. For example
$\KL(\mathcal N(1,0.25)\,\|\,\mathcal N(0,1))=\tfrac12(0.25+1.386294)=0.818147$.

**(c)** From B01, with $a=\vec w^{\mathsf T}\vec x$ and $r=\|\vec w\|^2$,
the ELBO of $\mathcal N(m,v)$ is, up to a constant,

$$
\begin{aligned}
&-\frac{-2am+(m^2+v)r}{2\sigma^2}\\
&\quad-\tfrac12\big(m^2+v-1-\log v\big).
\end{aligned}
$$

Setting the derivative in $m$ to zero gives $a/\sigma^2=m(r/\sigma^2+1)$,
so $m=a/(r+\sigma^2)$. In $v$ it gives $-r/2\sigma^2-\tfrac12+1/(2v)=0$,
so $v=\sigma^2/(r+\sigma^2)$. These are the posterior's mean and variance
in (I.13.2). For the point $(1,2)$ they are $0.866025$ and $0.25$, and by
(a) the ELBO there equals $\log p(\vec x)=-3.531024$.

</Solution>

<Answer>
(a) $\log p(\vec x)-\operatorname{ELBO}(q)=\KL(q(\vec z\mid\vec x)\,\|\,p(\vec z\mid\vec x))$.
(b) $\tfrac12(\mu^2+s^2-1-\log s^2)$, with derivatives $\mu$ and
$\tfrac12(s^2-1)$. (c) $m=a/(r+\sigma^2)$ and $v=\sigma^2/(r+\sigma^2)$,
the exact posterior, where the ELBO is $-3.531024$.
</Answer>

<CheckSanity>
The KL in (b) is zero at the prior and positive elsewhere: for fixed $s$
it grows with $\mu^2$, and $s^2-1-\log s^2\geq0$ with equality only at
$s^2=1$, by $\log t\leq t-1$.
</CheckSanity>

<Breaks>
If $q$ is positive somewhere the posterior is zero, the KL in (a) is
infinite and the ELBO is minus infinity. If $q$ is zero somewhere the
posterior is not, (a) still holds but $q$ cannot be the posterior, so the
gap never closes. A Gaussian prior and decoder make the posterior positive
everywhere, and neither case arises. If the decoder variance also depends
on $z$, (c) has no closed form.
</Breaks>

<Variation>
For a code with $k$ independent coordinates the KL is a sum of $k$ copies
of (b). At the posterior of the linear model the price is
$\tfrac12\log\big((r+\sigma^2)/\sigma^2\big)+\tfrac12(m^2+v-1)$. At the
maximum-likelihood $\vec w$ the second part averages to zero over the
data, and at every corner of the rectangle it is exactly zero, leaving
$\tfrac12\log4=\log2$.
</Variation>
