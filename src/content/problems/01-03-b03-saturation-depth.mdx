<Statement>
Find the maximum of $\sigma'$. Compute the product of ten sigmoid derivatives at
$|z| = 4$. Then find the depth at which the product underflows fp16, both at
$|z| = 4$ and in the best case.
</Statement>

<Given>
$\sigma'(z) = \sigma(z)(1-\sigma(z))$ from I.3.B02. The smallest positive fp16
value is the subnormal $2^{-24} = 5.96\times10^{-8}$
(<ApparatusRef ref="0.NU.01" />). A gradient reaching layer $1$ of an
$L$-layer stack is multiplied by one $\sigma'$ per layer.
</Given>

<Find>
$\max_z \sigma'(z)$; the value of $\sigma'(4)^{10}$; and the smallest $L$ with
$\sigma'^{\,L} < 2^{-24}$ at $|z| = 4$ and at $z = 0$.
</Find>

<Strategy>
Maximise the derivative by treating it as a quadratic in $\sigma$ rather than in
$z$ — the substitution turns calculus into inspection. Then take logarithms to
turn the repeated product into a linear count.
</Strategy>

<Solution>

**Step 1 — the maximum.** Let $s = \sigma(z) \in (0,1)$. Then
$\sigma' = s(1-s) = s - s^2$, a downward parabola in $s$ with vertex at
$s = 1/2$:

$$\max \sigma' = \tfrac12\left(1 - \tfrac12\right) = \tfrac14 = 0.2500$$

and $s = 1/2$ means $z = 0$. So **the best a sigmoid ever does for a gradient is
divide it by four**, and it does that only at one point.

**Step 2 — the derivative at $|z| = 4$.** $\sigma(4) = 1/(1+e^{-4}) = 0.982014$,
so

$$\sigma'(4) = (0.982014)(0.017986) = 0.0176627$$

Fourteen times smaller than the best case, from a pre-activation that is not
extreme — $z = 4$ is entirely ordinary in an unnormalised network.

**Step 3 — ten layers.**

$$\sigma'(4)^{10} = (0.0176627)^{10} = 2.955\times10^{-18}$$

For comparison the best case gives $0.25^{10} = 9.537\times10^{-7}$. Even at
every layer's single most favourable point, ten layers cost six orders of
magnitude.

**Step 4 — the fp16 depth.** Solve $g^{L} < 2^{-24}$ by taking logarithms:

$$L > \frac{-24\ln 2}{\ln g} = \frac{-16.6355}{\ln g}$$

*At $|z| = 4$*, $\ln(0.0176627) = -4.0362$, so $L > 4.12$, giving
$\boxed{L = 5}$.

*At $z = 0$*, $\ln(0.25) = -1.3863$, so $L > 11.999$, giving $\boxed{L = 12}$.

**What the two numbers say.** A sigmoid network in fp16 has a hard ceiling of
about twelve layers even under conditions that never occur — every unit sitting
exactly at its most favourable point on every example. In realistic conditions,
with pre-activations spread over a range including $|z| = 4$, the ceiling is
five. Not "training is slow past five layers": the gradient is *exactly zero*,
because the product is not representable and rounds to it.

This is why deep networks were not trained before roughly 2010, and it is why
three separate later chapters exist. ReLU (this chapter) removes the shrinking
factor entirely for active units. Normalisation (I.8) keeps $z$ near the
favourable region. Residual connections (II.6) provide a path with derivative
exactly $1$ that no activation stands on.

</Solution>

<Answer>
$$
\max_z \sigma'(z) = \tfrac14, \qquad
\sigma'(4)^{10} = 2.955\times10^{-18}
$$

fp16 underflow at depth $\mathbf{5}$ for $|z| = 4$, and at depth $\mathbf{12}$
in the impossible best case $z = 0$ throughout.
</Answer>

<CheckSanity>
**The maximum is attained where the function is symmetric.** $\sigma'$ inherits
$\sigma(-z) = 1-\sigma(z)$, so $\sigma'(-z) = \sigma'(z)$: the derivative is
even, and an even function's extremum on $\R$ sits at $0$ unless it is bimodal.
Consistent.

**The logarithmic estimate agrees with the loop.** The closed form gave
$L > 4.12$ and the loop counted $5$; $L > 11.999$ and the loop counted $12$. Two
methods, same answer.

**The magnitudes bracket correctly.** $2.955\times10^{-18} < 9.537\times10^{-7}$,
since $0.0177 < 0.25$ and both are raised to the same power. A product of
smaller numbers must be smaller.
</CheckSanity>

<Breaks>
The analysis assumes every layer's derivative is the same, which it is not: real
pre-activations vary per unit and per example, so the true product is a mix of
factors. That makes the situation *worse*, not better — the product is dominated
by its smallest factors, and a single saturated layer anywhere in the stack
zeroes the whole path regardless of how favourable the others are.

The analysis also assumes fp16 without loss scaling. Multiplying the loss by
$2^{15}$ before the backward pass shifts every gradient up by that factor and
buys about four more layers, which is exactly what mixed-precision training does
(II.8.B04). It buys layers; it does not remove the mechanism.
</Breaks>
