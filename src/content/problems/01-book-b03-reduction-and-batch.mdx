<Statement>
For one parameter whose per-example gradients are independent, compare
the mean and the variance of a gradient step under a mean reduction and a
sum reduction, then change the batch size.
</Statement>

<Given>
Per-example gradients $g_i$ with mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2=4$,
independent across examples. A batch of $B=32$. Learning rate $\eta=0.1$.
The step is $-\eta$ times the batch gradient, where the batch gradient is
either $\bar g=\tfrac1B\sum g_i$ or $\sum g_i$.
</Given>

<Find>
The variance of each batch gradient; the standard deviation of each step;
the learning rate that makes the sum's step match the mean's; the mean's
variance at $B=128$.
</Find>

<Strategy>
Variance scales with the square of a constant and adds over independent
terms (<ApparatusRef ref="0.PR.03" />).
</Strategy>

<Solution>

**Variances.** The sum of $B$ independent terms has variance
$B\sigma^2=128$. Dividing by $B$ divides the variance by $B^2$:
$\operatorname{Var}\bar g=\sigma^2/B=0.125$.

**Steps.** With $\eta=0.1$ the mean's step has standard deviation
$0.1\sqrt{0.125}=0.035355$ and the sum's $0.1\sqrt{128}=1.131371$, exactly
$B=32$ times larger. Its expected size, $\eta B\mu$, is also $32$ times
larger. The two runs are not the same algorithm with different noise; the
sum is the mean with a learning rate $32$ times higher.

**Matching.** The sum's step equals the mean's when
$\eta_{\text{sum}}=\eta/B=0.003125$. The reduction is part of the loss
(I.4.P04): changing it silently changes the learning rate by the batch
size.

**Larger batch.** At $B=128$ the mean's variance is $4/128=0.03125$, a
quarter of the value at $32$. Quadrupling the batch halves the standard
deviation of the step and leaves its expected value unchanged. The batch
size is a statement about gradient noise (I.7.P02); the direction it
estimates does not depend on it.

</Solution>

<Answer>
Variances $0.125$ for the mean and $128$ for the sum; step standard
deviations $0.035355$ and $1.131371$; matching learning rate $0.003125$;
at $B=128$ the mean's variance is $0.03125$.
</Answer>

<CheckSanity>
The ratio of the two standard deviations is $1.131371/0.035355=32=B$, as
the scaling argument requires.
</CheckSanity>

<Breaks>
Adaptive optimisers such as Adam divide the gradient by a running estimate
of its size, so multiplying the loss by a constant leaves their steps
nearly unchanged (I.7). The reduction then matters mainly through
$\epsilon$ and through any penalty added to the loss with its own
coefficient. Correlated examples, such as frames from one video, make the
variance shrink more slowly than $1/B$.
</Breaks>

<Variation>
A common heuristic raises the learning rate in proportion to the batch
size, keeping $\eta/B$ fixed so that the noise per unit of progress stays
roughly constant. It holds only while the step is small compared with the
curvature of the loss, which is why it fails at very large batches.
</Variation>
