<Statement>
Run one forward pass and one backward pass through a small network by hand,
producing every intermediate and every parameter gradient. Then check the
result against central differences, which is the only way to know a
hand-derived gradient is right rather than merely consistent with itself.
</Statement>

<Given>
A network with two inputs, two sigmoid hidden units and one linear output.
One example, so every activation is a row.

$$\vec{x} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad y = 1$$

$$
\mat{W}^{(1)} = \begin{pmatrix} 0.1 & 0.3 \\ 0.2 & 0.4 \end{pmatrix},
\qquad \vec{b}^{(1)} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0.1 \end{pmatrix}
$$

$$
\mat{W}^{(2)} = \begin{pmatrix} 0.5 \\ -0.6 \end{pmatrix},
\qquad b^{(2)} = 0.2
$$

The hidden nonlinearity is $\sigma(z) = 1/(1 + e^{-z})$, the head carries none,
and the loss is $L = \tfrac{1}{2}(\hat{y} - y)^2$.
</Given>

<Find>
Every intermediate of the forward pass; the adjoints $\delta^{(2)}$ and
$\delta^{(1)}$; the gradients with respect to all nine parameters; and the
largest disagreement between those gradients and a central-difference estimate.
</Find>

<Strategy>
Forward first, keeping everything — by Definition 3 the backward pass consumes
the stored values, so discarding them means starting again. Then apply (I.6.2)
from the sink: the loss adjoint is 1, and each node passes its own adjoint
times its local Jacobian to its producers. For this architecture that is
exactly the recurrence (I.6.3) and the outer product (I.6.4).
</Strategy>

<Solution>

**Step 1 — forward.** The pre-activations are $\vec{z}^{(1)} = \vec{x}\mat{W}^{(1)} + \vec{b}^{(1)}$:

$$z^{(1)}_1 = 1(0.1) + 2(0.2) + 0 = 0.5, \qquad z^{(1)}_2 = 1(0.3) + 2(0.4) + 0.1 = 1.2$$

$$\vec{a}^{(1)} = \sigma(\vec{z}^{(1)}) = \begin{pmatrix} 0.62245933 & 0.76852478 \end{pmatrix}$$

$$\hat{y} = z^{(2)} = 0.62245933(0.5) + 0.76852478(-0.6) + 0.2 = 0.05011480$$

$$L = \tfrac{1}{2}(0.05011480 - 1)^2 = 0.45114095$$

**Step 2 — the adjoint at the sink.** $\bar{L} = 1$ by definition, and the head
is linear, so

$$\delta^{(2)} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial z^{(2)}} = \hat{y} - y = -0.94988520$$

The sign says the prediction is below the target, which it is.

**Step 3 — the second layer's parameters.** By (I.6.4) the weight gradient is
the outer product of what entered the layer with what came back:

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial \mat{W}^{(2)}} = (\vec{a}^{(1)})^{\mathsf{T}} \delta^{(2)}
= \begin{pmatrix} -0.59126491 \\ -0.73001032 \end{pmatrix},
\qquad \frac{\partial L}{\partial b^{(2)}} = -0.94988520
$$

The bias gradient *is* the adjoint, because a bias enters its node with
coefficient one.

**Step 4 — travel back through the layer.** By (I.6.3), multiply by
$(\mat{W}^{(2)})^{\mathsf{T}}$ and gate by the slope of the nonlinearity:

$$\delta^{(2)}(\mat{W}^{(2)})^{\mathsf{T}} = \begin{pmatrix} -0.47494260 & 0.56993112 \end{pmatrix}$$

For the logistic function $\sigma' = \sigma(1 - \sigma)$, so it is read off the
stored activations rather than recomputed:

$$\sigma'(\vec{z}^{(1)}) = \begin{pmatrix} 0.23500371 & 0.17789444 \end{pmatrix}$$

$$
\delta^{(1)} = \begin{pmatrix} -0.47494260 & 0.56993112 \end{pmatrix} \odot
\begin{pmatrix} 0.23500371 & 0.17789444 \end{pmatrix}
= \begin{pmatrix} -0.11161327 & 0.10138758 \end{pmatrix}
$$

**Step 5 — the first layer's parameters.** The same outer product, one layer down:

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial \mat{W}^{(1)}} = \vec{x}^{\mathsf{T}} \delta^{(1)} =
\begin{pmatrix} -0.11161327 & 0.10138758 \\ -0.22322655 & 0.20277516 \end{pmatrix},
\qquad \frac{\partial L}{\partial \vec{b}^{(1)}} = \delta^{(1)}
$$

The second row is exactly twice the first, because the second input is twice the
first. A weight's gradient carries the size of the thing that fed it.

**Step 6 — the check.** Central differences with $h = 10^{-6}$, evaluating the
loss twice per parameter and taking $(L(\theta + h) - L(\theta - h))/2h$, agree
with all nine analytic gradients. The largest disagreement is
$5.79 \times 10^{-11}$.

That agreement is worth more than any of the individual numbers. The analytic
gradient came from the chain rule; the numeric one came from evaluating the
forward function eighteen times and never differentiating anything. Two
unrelated computations landing on the same nine numbers is evidence the
derivation is correct.

</Solution>

<Answer>

| quantity | value |
|---|---|
| $\vec{z}^{(1)}$ | $0.5,\ 1.2$ |
| $\vec{a}^{(1)}$ | $0.62245933,\ 0.76852478$ |
| $\hat{y}$ | $0.05011480$ |
| $L$ | $0.45114095$ |
| $\delta^{(2)}$ | $-0.94988520$ |
| $\delta^{(1)}$ | $-0.11161327,\ 0.10138758$ |
| $\partial L/\partial \mat{W}^{(2)}$ | $-0.59126491,\ -0.73001032$ |
| $\partial L/\partial \mat{W}^{(1)}$ | $(-0.11161327,\ 0.10138758;\ -0.22322655,\ 0.20277516)$ |
| largest gradient-check gap | $5.79 \times 10^{-11}$ |

</Answer>

<CheckSanity>
**The bias gradients equal the adjoints.** $\partial L/\partial b^{(2)} = \delta^{(2)}$
and $\partial L/\partial \vec{b}^{(1)} = \delta^{(1)}$, to the last digit. A bias
adds into its node with coefficient one, so its local Jacobian is the identity
and (I.6.2) leaves the adjoint unchanged. Any discrepancy here is an arithmetic
slip, not a modelling question.

**The rows of $\partial L/\partial \mat{W}^{(1)}$ are in the ratio of the
inputs.** $-0.22322655 / -0.11161327 = 2$ exactly, matching $x_2/x_1 = 2$. The
outer product in (I.6.4) says the gradient of a weight scales with the
activation entering it, so this ratio is forced.

**The sigmoid slopes are below $1/4$.** $0.23500371$ and $0.17789444$, against
the maximum $\sigma'(0) = 0.25$. Every layer of this kind multiplies the
travelling adjoint by something no larger than a quarter, which is the
observation I.6.B04 turns into a depth limit.
</CheckSanity>

<Breaks>
The finite-difference check that validates the gradient here does not scale, and
it is worth being precise about why. It costs two forward passes per parameter,
so checking a model with $N$ parameters costs $2N$ evaluations — for
$N = 567\,434$, the network audited in I.5.B03, that is over a million forward
passes to verify one gradient that reverse mode produced in a single traversal.
By (I.6.6) the analytic gradient cost about four forward passes. The ratio of a
million to four *is* the cheap gradient principle, seen from the other side.

The step size has its own trap. Too large and the truncation error of the
difference dominates; too small and floating-point cancellation does, because
$L(\theta + h)$ and $L(\theta - h)$ agree in their leading digits and
subtracting them discards exactly those. At $h = 10^{-12}$ in fp64 this same
check returns visible garbage. Gradient checking is a debugging tool for small
cases, not a verification method for a real model.
</Breaks>

<Variation>
Replace the linear head with a second sigmoid, so $\hat{y} = \sigma(z^{(2)})$,
and keep everything else. Which of the nine gradients change, and by what
factor? Predict the factor before recomputing — one number in Step 2 is
responsible for all of it.
</Variation>
