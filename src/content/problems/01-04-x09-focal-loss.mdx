Focal loss is $\mathrm{FL} = -(1-p_t)^{\gamma}\log p_t$, where $p_t$ is the
probability assigned to the true class. Tabulate the modulating factor, derive
$\mathrm{d}\,\mathrm{FL}/\mathrm{d}z$ for the binary case, evaluate at five
logits with $\gamma = 2$, and say how it differs from the class weighting of
I.4.B05.

<Hint>
Use the product rule on $-(1-p)^{\gamma}\log p$, then chain through
$\mathrm{d}p/\mathrm{d}z = p(1-p)$.
</Hint>

<SolutionToggle>

**Step 1 — the modulating factor.**

| $p_t$ | $\gamma = 0$ | $\gamma = 1$ | $\gamma = 2$ | $\gamma = 5$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $0.10$ | $1.00000$ | $0.90000$ | $0.81000$ | $0.59049$ |
| $0.50$ | $1.00000$ | $0.50000$ | $0.25000$ | $0.03125$ |
| $0.90$ | $1.00000$ | $0.10000$ | $0.01000$ | $0.00001$ |
| $0.99$ | $1.00000$ | $0.01000$ | $0.00010$ | $0.00000$ |

At $\gamma = 0$ the factor is $1$ everywhere and focal loss **is** cross-entropy.
As $\gamma$ grows, well-classified examples ($p_t$ near $1$) are suppressed
sharply while hard ones ($p_t$ near $0$) are barely touched: at $\gamma = 2$ the
suppression is $100\times$ at $p_t = 0.9$ and only $1.23\times$ at $p_t = 0.1$.

**Step 2 — derive the gradient.** Write $L = -(1-p)^{\gamma}\log p$ with $y=1$,
so $p_t = p$. By the product rule on the two $p$-dependent factors:

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial p}
= \underbrace{\gamma(1-p)^{\gamma-1}\log p}_{\text{from }(1-p)^\gamma}
\;\underbrace{-\;\frac{(1-p)^{\gamma}}{p}}_{\text{from }\log p}
$$

taking care with the sign: $\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}p}(1-p)^{\gamma} = -\gamma(1-p)^{\gamma-1}$,
and the leading minus of $L$ flips it back.

**Step 3 — chain through the sigmoid.** With $\mathrm{d}p/\mathrm{d}z = p(1-p)$:

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}L}{\mathrm{d}z}
= \left[\gamma(1-p)^{\gamma-1}\log p - \frac{(1-p)^{\gamma}}{p}\right] p(1-p)
$$

Distribute $p(1-p)$ into the bracket:

$$
= \gamma\,p\,(1-p)^{\gamma}\log p \;-\; (1-p)^{\gamma+1}
$$

and factor out $(1-p)^{\gamma}$:

$$
\boxed{\ \frac{\mathrm{d}L}{\mathrm{d}z} = (1-p)^{\gamma}\Big(\gamma\,p\log p + p - 1\Big)\ }
$$

**Check it reduces correctly.** At $\gamma = 0$: $(1)(0 + p - 1) = p - 1 = p - y$,
which is I.4.4 ✓.

**Step 4 — evaluate at $\gamma = 2$, $y = 1$.**

| $z$ | $p$ | CE | FL | $\mathrm{dCE}/\mathrm{d}z$ | $\mathrm{dFL}/\mathrm{d}z$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $-4.0$ | $0.01799$ | $4.01815$ | $3.874907$ | $-0.98201$ | $-1.086396$ |
| $-2.0$ | $0.11920$ | $2.12693$ | $1.650078$ | $-0.88080$ | $-1.076714$ |
| $0.0$ | $0.50000$ | $0.69315$ | $0.173287$ | $-0.50000$ | $-0.298287$ |
| $2.0$ | $0.88080$ | $0.12693$ | $0.001804$ | $-0.11920$ | $-0.004871$ |
| $4.0$ | $0.98201$ | $0.01815$ | $0.000006$ | $-0.01799$ | $-0.000017$ |

**Step 5 — read the last column.** At $z = 4$ — an example the model already
gets right — cross-entropy still sends $-0.01799$, while focal loss sends
$-0.000017$: **a thousandfold smaller**. At $z = -4$ — an example it gets wrong —
focal loss sends $-1.086$, *larger* in magnitude than cross-entropy's $-0.982$.

So focal loss does not merely rescale; it **reorders**. Under cross-entropy the
hard example's gradient is $55\times$ the easy one's; under focal loss it is
$64{,}000\times$.

**Step 6 — how this differs from class weighting.**

| | class weighting (I.4.B05) | focal loss |
|---|---|---|
| computed from | the class counts | the current prediction |
| fixed during training | yes | no |
| distinguishes easy from hard | no | yes |
| needs counts | yes | no |

Class weighting asks *which class is this?* and applies a constant. Focal loss
asks *how wrong is the model here, right now?* and applies a factor that changes
every step.

That difference is exactly the objection raised in I.4.B05's *Where this breaks*:
static weights correct a static imbalance in a quantity that is not static. Once
the model has learned the majority class, those examples' gradients have already
collapsed by (I.4.4), and the fixed weight keeps suppressing them anyway. Focal
loss suppresses them because they are easy, and stops suppressing anything that
becomes hard again.

**The cost.** $\gamma$ is a new hyperparameter with no principled setting — the
original paper's $\gamma = 2$ was chosen by sweep — and the loss no longer has
the log-likelihood interpretation of I.4.T1. It is a heuristic reshaping of a
principled loss, and worth using with that clearly in view.
