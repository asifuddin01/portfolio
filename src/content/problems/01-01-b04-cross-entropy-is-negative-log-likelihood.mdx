<Statement>
Show that binary cross-entropy and the negative log-likelihood of a Bernoulli
model are the same quantity, then evaluate both at $p = 0.8$ for each possible
label.
</Statement>

<Given>
A model that outputs a single number $p \in (0,1)$, interpreted as
$P(Y = 1 \mid x)$. The label $y \in \{0, 1\}$. Binary cross-entropy is defined
as

$$\mathrm{CE}(p, y) = -\big[\, y \log p + (1-y)\log(1-p) \,\big]$$

and the Bernoulli likelihood of the observed label is $P(Y = y) = p^{y}(1-p)^{1-y}$.
</Given>

<Find>
An algebraic demonstration that $\mathrm{CE}(p,y) = -\log P(Y = y)$, and the
value of each at $(p, y) = (0.8, 1)$ and $(0.8, 0)$.
</Find>

<Strategy>
Take the logarithm of the likelihood and let the exponents come down. The two
expressions are then identical term for term, so this is an identity rather
than an approximation — which matters, because it means choosing cross-entropy
*is* choosing a Bernoulli noise model, whether or not anyone said so.
</Strategy>

<Solution>

**Step 1 — write the likelihood.** The trick is the indicator exponent: since
$y$ is $0$ or $1$, exactly one of the two factors survives.

$$P(Y = y) = p^{y}(1-p)^{1-y}$$

Check both cases before going further. At $y=1$: $p^1(1-p)^0 = p$. At $y=0$:
$p^0(1-p)^1 = 1-p$. Correct in both.

**Step 2 — take logarithms.** Using $\log(ab) = \log a + \log b$ and
$\log(a^c) = c\log a$:

$$
\log P(Y=y) = \log\!\big(p^{y}\big) + \log\!\big((1-p)^{1-y}\big)
            = y\log p + (1-y)\log(1-p)
$$

**Step 3 — negate.**

$$-\log P(Y = y) = -\big[\,y\log p + (1-y)\log(1-p)\,\big] = \mathrm{CE}(p,y) \tag{I.1.6}$$

The two definitions coincide exactly, for every $p$ and every $y$. Nothing was
approximated and no assumption was added beyond the one already made when the
output was called a probability.

**Step 4 — evaluate at $y = 1$.** The $(1-y)$ term vanishes:

$$\mathrm{CE}(0.8, 1) = -\log 0.8 = -(-0.2231) = 0.2231$$

and independently, $-\log P(Y=1) = -\log 0.8 = 0.2231$. They agree.

**Step 5 — evaluate at $y = 0$.** Now the $y$ term vanishes:

$$\mathrm{CE}(0.8, 0) = -\log(1 - 0.8) = -\log 0.2 = 1.6094$$

and $-\log P(Y=0) = -\log 0.2 = 1.6094$.

The asymmetry is the point. The same prediction $p = 0.8$ costs $0.2231$ when
it is right and $1.6094$ when it is wrong — seven times as much. A model is
punished far more for confident error than it is rewarded for confident
correctness, and that asymmetry is what drives calibration.

</Solution>

<Answer>
$$\mathrm{CE}(p, y) = -\log P(Y = y) \quad\text{identically, for all } p \in (0,1),\ y \in \{0,1\}$$

$$
\mathrm{CE}(0.8, 1) = 0.2231 \text{ nats}, \qquad
\mathrm{CE}(0.8, 0) = 1.6094 \text{ nats}
$$

In bits, divide by $\log 2$: $0.3219$ and $2.3219$ bits respectively.
</Answer>

<CheckNumeric snippet="i-1-b04-bernoulli-cross-entropy.py" verified>
```python
def cross_entropy(p, y): return -(y * log(p) + (1 - y) * log(1 - p))
def likelihood(p, y):    return p if y == 1 else 1 - p
ce, nll = cross_entropy(p, y), -log(likelihood(p, y))
```

The two are computed from separate definitions and compared, rather than one
being derived from the other. It prints `equal=True` at every tested pair.
</CheckNumeric>

<CheckSanity>
**A perfect prediction costs nothing.** At $p \to 1$ with $y = 1$,
$-\log p \to 0$. The snippet's $p = 0.99$ row gives $0.0101$, which is close to
zero and on the right side of it.

**A maximally uncertain prediction costs $\log 2$.** At $p = 0.5$ the loss is
$0.6931$ nats $= 1$ bit, for either label. One bit is exactly the information in
a fair coin (<ApparatusRef ref="0.IT.01" />), so the units are behaving.

**The two branches sum correctly.** For any $p$, the expected cross-entropy
under the model's own distribution is $p(-\log p) + (1-p)(-\log(1-p))$, which is
the entropy of that Bernoulli. At $p = 0.8$: $0.8(0.2231) + 0.2(1.6094) = 0.5004$
nats, and the closed-form entropy of $\mathrm{Bern}(0.8)$ is $0.5004$. That is a
genuinely independent route to the same number.
</CheckSanity>

<Breaks>
The identity needs $p \in (0,1)$ strictly. At $p = 0$ with $y = 1$ the loss is
$-\log 0 = +\infty$: a model that assigns zero probability to something that
then happens is infinitely wrong, and no finite gradient step recovers from it.
This is not a corner case in practice — it is why the loss is computed from
logits with the log-sum-exp identity (<ApparatusRef ref="0.NU.02" />) rather
than from a probability that a float can round to exactly $0$ or $1$.
</Breaks>
