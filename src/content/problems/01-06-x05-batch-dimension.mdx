A layer has $\mat{W} \in \R^{256 \times 128}$ and sees a batch
$\mat{A} \in \R^{64 \times 256}$, producing adjoints
$\Delta \in \R^{64 \times 128}$. Give the shape of $\partial L/\partial \mat{W}$
and say where the 64 went. Then give the shape of the object a per-sample
gradient method needs instead, and its size in fp32.

<Hint>
A gradient has the shape of the thing it differentiates. $\mat{W}$ has no batch
dimension, so the answer is forced — the question is only which operation
removes it.
</Hint>

<SolutionToggle>

**The shape.** $\partial L/\partial \mat{W}$ is $256 \times 128$, the shape of
$\mat{W}$. The batch is contracted away by the product of (I.6.4):

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial \mat{W}} = \mat{A}^{\mathsf{T}}\Delta,
\qquad (256 \times 64)(64 \times 128) \to 256 \times 128
$$

The 64 is the inner dimension. Nobody writes a sum over the batch because the
matrix product already is one — it adds the 64 per-example outer products
together as it goes.

**What is lost.** The sum is not invertible. Once formed, $\mat{A}^{\mathsf{T}}\Delta$
cannot be taken apart into the 64 contributions that made it, so any method
needing individual examples must intervene before the contraction rather than
after it.

**The per-sample object.** That is the stack of outer products, one per example:

$$
\mat{G} \in \R^{64 \times 256 \times 128}, \qquad
\mat{G}_n = \mat{A}_{n,:}^{\mathsf{T}} \Delta_{n,:}
$$

$64 \times 256 \times 128 = 2\,097\,152$ values, $8\,388\,608$ bytes in fp32 —
$8.4$ MB for one layer's gradient at batch 64, against $131\,072$ bytes for the
reduced version. Sixty-four times the memory, which is the batch size, and that
is the whole cost of the method.

**Who needs it.** Differentially private training clips each example's gradient
to a fixed norm before averaging, which is meaningless once they are summed.
Influence functions and per-example importance weighting want the same object.
All of them pay the same $B\times$ memory, and the usual response is to
recompute per-sample gradients layer by layer rather than hold them all at once.
