<Statement>
Show that the pathwise and score-function estimators of one gradient are
both unbiased, compute their variances, and follow them as the encoder's
distribution narrows.
</Statement>

<Given>
$q=\mathcal N(\mu,s^2)$, $z=\mu+s\varepsilon$ with
$\varepsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,1)$, and $f(z)=z^2$. The target is
$\partial\,\E_q[f(z)]/\partial\mu$. The moments
$\E[\varepsilon^2]=1$, $\E[\varepsilon^4]=3$ and $\E[\varepsilon^6]=15$,
and odd moments vanish.
</Given>

<Find>
Both one-draw estimates, their means, their variances in general and at
$\mu=1$, $s=0.5$, and their behaviour as $s\to0$.
</Find>

<Strategy>
Write each estimate as a polynomial in $\varepsilon$ and take moments term
by term.
</Strategy>

<Solution>

**The target.** $\E_q[z^2]=\mu^2+s^2$, so the derivative is $2\mu$.

**Pathwise.** By (I.13.6) the estimate is
$g_{\mathrm p}=f'(z)=2(\mu+s\varepsilon)$. Its mean is $2\mu$ and its
variance is

$$
\operatorname{Var}g_{\mathrm p}=4s^2.
$$

**Score function.** By (I.13.7), with
$\partial\log q/\partial\mu=(z-\mu)/s^2=\varepsilon/s$,

$$
g_{\mathrm s}=\frac{(\mu+s\varepsilon)^2\,\varepsilon}{s}.
$$

Expanding, $(\mu+s\varepsilon)^2\varepsilon
=\mu^2\varepsilon+2\mu s\varepsilon^2+s^2\varepsilon^3$, whose mean is
$2\mu s$. So $\E g_{\mathrm s}=2\mu$: unbiased. For the second moment,

$$
\begin{aligned}
&(\mu+s\varepsilon)^4\varepsilon^2\\
&\;=\mu^4\varepsilon^2+4\mu^3s\varepsilon^3\\
&\;\quad+6\mu^2s^2\varepsilon^4+4\mu s^3\varepsilon^5\\
&\;\quad+s^4\varepsilon^6,
\end{aligned}
$$

which averages to $\mu^4+18\mu^2s^2+15s^4$. Dividing by $s^2$ and
subtracting the squared mean,

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\operatorname{Var}g_{\mathrm s}\\
&\;=\frac{\mu^4+18\mu^2s^2+15s^4}{s^2}\\
&\;\quad-4\mu^2.
\end{aligned}
$$

**The numbers.** At $\mu=1$, $s=0.5$ the pathwise variance is $1$ and the
score-function variance is $(1+4.5+0.9375)/0.25-4=21.75$.

**As $s\to0$.** The pathwise variance $4s^2$ goes to zero. The
score-function variance behaves like $\mu^4/s^2$ and grows without bound:

| $s$ | pathwise | score function |
|---|---|---|
| $0.5$ | $1$ | $21.75$ |
| $0.1$ | $0.04$ | $114.15$ |
| $0.01$ | $0.0004$ | $10{,}014$ |

</Solution>

<Answer>
Both have mean $2\mu$. Variances $4s^2$ and
$(\mu^4+18\mu^2s^2+15s^4)/s^2-4\mu^2$: at $\mu=1$, $s=0.5$, $1$ against
$21.75$. As $s\to0$ the first vanishes and the second grows like
$\mu^4/s^2$.
</Answer>

<CheckSanity>
At $\mu=0$ the score-function variance is $15s^2$, finite and small, while
the pathwise variance is $4s^2$. The score function is worst when $f$ is
large where $q$ sits, because it multiplies $f$ itself by
$\varepsilon/s$.
</CheckSanity>

<Breaks>
The comparison depends on $f$. For an integrand that oscillates quickly,
such as $\sin(10z)$ in
[I.13.X10](/elementa/neural-networks/vae/problems/x10), the order
reverses. A baseline subtracted from $f$ can reduce the score function's
variance a great deal without biasing it.
</Breaks>

<Variation>
Subtract the baseline $b=\E_q f=\mu^2+s^2$ from $f$ before multiplying by
$\varepsilon/s$. The estimate stays unbiased because
$\E[\varepsilon/s]=0$. The estimate becomes
$2\mu\varepsilon^2+s(\varepsilon^3-\varepsilon)$, with variance
$8\mu^2+10s^2$. At $\mu=1$, $s=0.5$ that is $10.5$: half of $21.75$, and
still ten times the pathwise $1$.
</Variation>
