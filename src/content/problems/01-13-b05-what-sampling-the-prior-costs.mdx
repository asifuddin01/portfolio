<Statement>
Count how many prior samples an unbiased estimate of the evidence needs,
in one dimension and in ten, and set that against the cost of one ELBO
step.
</Statement>

<Given>
The estimate is the average of $p(\vec x\mid z_s)$ over $n$ draws $z_s$
from the prior. By (I.13.3) one draw has relative variance
$r=\int p(z\mid\vec x)^2/p(z)\,dz-1$, and for a posterior
$\mathcal N(\mu,s^2)$ with $s^2<2$ the integral is
$e^{\mu^2/(2-s^2)}/(s\sqrt{2-s^2})$. Use the point $(1,2)$ under the model
of [I.13.B01](/elementa/neural-networks/vae/problems/b01), and again with
noise variance $0.01$, where $\|\vec w\|^2=3.99$. The target is a relative
standard error of ten per cent, so $n=r/0.1^2$. For the encoder, take a
hidden layer of $400$ units and a code of $k=20$ dimensions.
</Given>

<Find>
$r$ and $n$ for both noise levels in one dimension; $n$ in ten independent
latent dimensions; the cost of one ELBO step; the size of the encoder's
output layer.
</Find>

<Strategy>
Get the posterior from (I.13.2), apply the formula, and use the fact that
for independent dimensions the integral in (I.13.3) factorises.
</Strategy>

<Solution>

**Noise variance 1.** The posterior is $\mathcal N(0.866025,0.25)$, so
$\mu^2=0.75$ and $2-s^2=1.75$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
1+r&=\frac{e^{0.75/1.75}}{0.5\sqrt{1.75}}\\
 &=\frac{1.535063}{0.661438}\\
 &=2.320797.
\end{aligned}
$$

So $r=1.320797$ and $n=132$ draws.

**Noise variance 0.01.** The posterior variance is
$0.01/(3.99+0.01)=0.0025$, a standard deviation of $0.05$, and its mean is
$2\sqrt{3.99}/4=0.998749$. The same formula gives $r=22.316431$ and
$n=2{,}230$. Narrowing the posterior by a factor of ten multiplied the
cost by about seventeen.

**Ten dimensions.** For a factorised prior and posterior the integral is
a product, one factor $1+r$ per dimension:

$$
1+r_{10}=(1+r)^{10}.
$$

At noise variance $1$ that is $2.320797^{10}=4{,}530$, so
$n=4.53\times10^5$. At $0.01$ it is $23.316431^{10}=4.75\times10^{13}$,
so $n=4.75\times10^{15}$. The cost is exponential in the number of latent
dimensions.

**One ELBO step.** The bound needs one draw from the encoder's
distribution per example: one encoder pass, one draw of
$\vec\varepsilon$, one decoder pass, and the closed form (I.13.5) for the
KL. Its cost does not grow with $k$ except through the width of two
layers.

**The encoder's output layer.** It returns $\vec\mu$ and $\log\vec\sigma^2$,
$2k=40$ numbers, from $400$ hidden units: $400\cdot40+40=16{,}040$
parameters, twice what a deterministic code of the same width would need.

</Solution>

<Answer>
$r=1.320797$ and $n\approx132$ at noise variance $1$; $r=22.316431$ and
$n\approx2{,}230$ at $0.01$. In ten dimensions, $n\approx4.53\times10^5$
and $4.75\times10^{15}$. One ELBO step costs one encoder and one decoder
pass per example. The output layer has $16{,}040$ parameters.
</Answer>

<CheckSanity>
If the posterior were the prior, $\mu=0$ and $s=1$, the formula gives
$1+r=1/(1\cdot1)=1$ and $r=0$: every draw would return $p(\vec x)$ exactly.
The cost comes entirely from the posterior differing from the prior.
</CheckSanity>

<Breaks>
The closed form needs $s^2<2$; a posterior wider than twice the prior's
variance makes the integral infinite. Real posteriors are not factorised,
and the exponent of the dimension count then depends on their shape, but
the growth remains exponential in general.
</Breaks>

<Variation>
Draw from the posterior instead and weight each draw by
$p(z)/p(z\mid\vec x)$. Every draw then returns $p(\vec x)$ exactly and the
variance is zero. An encoder close to the posterior is a cheap
approximation to that ideal proposal.
</Variation>
