<Statement>
Construct a classification case in which squared loss produces a vanishing
gradient and cross-entropy does not. Derive both gradients with respect to the
logit, evaluate them at five logits, and identify precisely which factor is
responsible.
</Statement>

<Given>
A binary classifier emitting a logit $z$, with $p = \sigma(z)$ and target
$y = 1$. Two candidate losses:

$$
L_{\text{MSE}} = \tfrac12 (p - y)^{2},
\qquad
L_{\text{BCE}} = -\big[y\log p + (1-y)\log(1-p)\big]
$$

Recall $\sigma'(z) = \sigma(z)\big(1-\sigma(z)\big) = p(1-p)$ from I.3.B02.
</Given>

<Find>
$\mathrm{d}L/\mathrm{d}z$ for each loss in closed form; both evaluated at
$z \in \{-1, -3, -5, -10, -20\}$; and their ratio.
</Find>

<Strategy>
Differentiate each loss with respect to $p$, then apply the chain rule through
$\sigma$. The whole result turns on whether the $\sigma'(z)$ factor survives or
cancels, so keep it visible rather than simplifying early.
</Strategy>

<Solution>

**Step 1 — MSE's gradient.** Differentiating with respect to $p$ first:

$$\frac{\partial L_{\text{MSE}}}{\partial p} = \frac{\partial}{\partial p}\,\tfrac12 (p-y)^2 = (p - y)$$

Then the chain rule through the sigmoid (<ApparatusRef ref="0.MC.03" />):

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}L_{\text{MSE}}}{\mathrm{d}z}
= \frac{\partial L_{\text{MSE}}}{\partial p}\cdot\frac{\mathrm{d}p}{\mathrm{d}z}
= (p - y)\,\underbrace{p(1-p)}_{\sigma'(z)}
\tag{a}
$$

**The factor $\sigma'(z)$ is present, and it is the problem.** Chapter I.3
established that $\sigma' \le 1/4$ always, and that it collapses toward zero as
$|z|$ grows.

**Step 2 — BCE's gradient.** Differentiating with respect to $p$:

$$
\frac{\partial L_{\text{BCE}}}{\partial p}
= -\left[\frac{y}{p} - \frac{1-y}{1-p}\right]
= \frac{p - y}{p(1-p)}
$$

To see the last equality, put the bracket over a common denominator:

$$
\frac{y}{p} - \frac{1-y}{1-p}
= \frac{y(1-p) - p(1-y)}{p(1-p)}
= \frac{y - yp - p + py}{p(1-p)}
= \frac{y - p}{p(1-p)}
$$

and negating gives $(p-y)/[p(1-p)]$.

Now the chain rule:

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}L_{\text{BCE}}}{\mathrm{d}z}
= \frac{p-y}{p(1-p)}\cdot p(1-p)
= p - y
\tag{b}
$$

**The $\sigma'$ factor cancelled exactly.** The logarithm in the loss produced a
$1/[p(1-p)]$ that met the $p(1-p)$ from the sigmoid. This is the same
cancellation as I.4.B03's Step 7, in the binary case.

**Step 3 — compare the two closed forms.**

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}L_{\text{MSE}}}{\mathrm{d}z} = (p-y)\,\sigma'(z),
\qquad
\frac{\mathrm{d}L_{\text{BCE}}}{\mathrm{d}z} = (p-y)
$$

They differ by exactly one factor, $\sigma'(z) \in (0, 1/4]$. So

$$
\left|\frac{\mathrm{d}L_{\text{BCE}}/\mathrm{d}z}{\mathrm{d}L_{\text{MSE}}/\mathrm{d}z}\right| = \frac{1}{\sigma'(z)} = \frac{1}{p(1-p)}
$$

**Step 4 — evaluate.** With $y = 1$:

| $z$ | $p = \sigma(z)$ | MSE $\mathrm{d}L/\mathrm{d}z$ | BCE $\mathrm{d}L/\mathrm{d}z$ | ratio |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $-1$ | $2.689414\mathrm{e}{-1}$ | $-1.437348\mathrm{e}{-1}$ | $-0.731059$ | $5.1$ |
| $-3$ | $4.742587\mathrm{e}{-2}$ | $-4.303412\mathrm{e}{-2}$ | $-0.952574$ | $22.1$ |
| $-5$ | $6.692851\mathrm{e}{-3}$ | $-6.603562\mathrm{e}{-3}$ | $-0.993307$ | $150.4$ |
| $-10$ | $4.539787\mathrm{e}{-5}$ | $-4.539375\mathrm{e}{-5}$ | $-0.999955$ | $22{,}028.5$ |
| $-20$ | $2.061154\mathrm{e}{-9}$ | $-2.061154\mathrm{e}{-9}$ | $-1.000000$ | $485{,}165{,}197.4$ |

**Step 5 — read the two columns.** As $z$ becomes more negative the model becomes
*more* wrong: $p \to 0$ while $y = 1$.

Cross-entropy's gradient **grows** toward its maximum magnitude of $1$. At
$z = -20$ it is $-1.000000$: the loss is shouting.

MSE's gradient **shrinks** toward zero. At $z = -20$ it is $-2.06\times10^{-9}$.
In float32, where the smallest normal value is about $1.18\times10^{-38}$, this
is still representable — but multiplied through a few more layers of I.3's
saturation factors it is not, and in fp16 it underflowed long before.

**The model is as wrong as it is possible to be, and MSE reports almost
nothing.** That is the counterexample.

**Step 6 — which factor is responsible.** Not the squaring. Substituting
$y = 1$ into (a):

$$\frac{\mathrm{d}L_{\text{MSE}}}{\mathrm{d}z} = (p-1)\,p(1-p) = -p(1-p)^2$$

As $p \to 0$ this behaves as $-p$, and $p = \sigma(z) \to 0$ exponentially in
$z$. The culprit is the $\sigma'$ factor that (b) cancels and (a) keeps —
exactly the saturation of Chapter I.3, reaching the loss instead of a hidden
layer.

**Step 7 — the other end, which is the honest caveat.** At a point the model
already gets right:

| $z$ | MSE $|\mathrm{d}L/\mathrm{d}z|$ | BCE $|\mathrm{d}L/\mathrm{d}z|$ |
|---|---|---|
| $+5$ | $4.449445\mathrm{e}{-5}$ | $6.692851\mathrm{e}{-3}$ |
| $+10$ | $2.060873\mathrm{e}{-9}$ | $4.539787\mathrm{e}{-5}$ |

Both shrink, and MSE shrinks faster. So MSE is not *uniformly* worse — it is
quieter everywhere. The asymmetry that matters is that cross-entropy stays loud
where the model is wrong and goes quiet where it is right, while MSE goes quiet
in both directions.

</Solution>

<Answer>
$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}L_{\text{MSE}}}{\mathrm{d}z} = (p - y)\,p(1-p),
\qquad
\frac{\mathrm{d}L_{\text{BCE}}}{\mathrm{d}z} = p - y
$$

They differ by the factor $\sigma'(z) = p(1-p) \le 1/4$, which cross-entropy's
logarithm cancels and squared loss does not.

At $z = -10$, $y = 1$: MSE gives $-4.539\times10^{-5}$ against BCE's
$-0.999955$, a ratio of $22{,}029$. At $z = -20$ the ratio is
$4.85\times10^{8}$.
</Answer>

<CheckSanity>
**BCE's gradient is bounded by 1.** Every entry satisfies
$|p - y| \le 1$ since $p \in (0,1)$ and $y \in \{0,1\}$. The table's largest is
$-1.000000$, approached but not exceeded ✓

**The ratio equals $1/[p(1-p)]$ exactly.** At $z = -5$:
$1/(6.692851\times10^{-3} \times 0.993307) = 150.4$, matching the table's ratio
column — computed from the closed form rather than by dividing the two columns,
so it is an independent check.

**The two gradients agree where $\sigma'$ is largest.** At $z = 0$,
$\sigma' = 1/4$, so the ratio would be $4$ — the smallest it can ever be. The
table's smallest ratio, $5.1$ at $z = -1$, is consistent with approaching $4$ as
$z \to 0$.

**Signs are correct throughout.** With $y = 1$ and $p < 1$, both gradients are
negative, so descent raises $z$ — which is what should happen when the model
under-predicts the positive class.
</CheckSanity>

<Breaks>
The vanishing is a property of **the pair**, not of squared loss alone. Remove
the sigmoid — regress on an unbounded output with squared loss, as in I.1.B03 —
and the gradient is $(\hat{y} - y)$ with no saturating factor anywhere. Squared
loss is entirely well behaved for regression; it is squared loss *composed with a
saturating output* that fails.

The general lesson is worth stating in its own right: **check what the loss and
the output nonlinearity do together, not separately.** Cross-entropy pairs with
softmax and sigmoid because the logarithm inverts the exponential in them. Any
other pairing has to be checked, and the check is one line — differentiate and
see whether the output's derivative cancels.
</Breaks>
