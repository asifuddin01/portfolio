Audit two classifiers on the same task: widths $(784, 512, 256, 128, 10)$ from
I.5.B03, and widths $(784, 2048, 10)$. Give the parameter count of each, say
which is larger and by how much, and then answer the question that matters —
which layer would you shrink first, in each.

<Hint>
Use (I.5.2) and keep the layers separate. Predict the answer before computing;
the deep model has three times the layers.
</Hint>

<SolutionToggle>

**The deep model.** From I.5.B03: $401\,920 + 131\,328 + 32\,896 + 1\,290 = 567\,434$.

**The wide model.**

$$784 \cdot 2048 + 2048 = 1\,605\,632 + 2048 = 1\,607\,680$$

$$2048 \cdot 10 + 10 = 20\,490$$

$$N = 1\,628\,170$$

**The wide model is $2.87$ times larger** with a third of the layers. The
prediction most people make is the opposite one, and the reason it fails is that
depth appears in (I.5.2) as a *sum* while width appears as a *product*. Three
extra layers add three terms; one width of $2048$ instead of $512$ multiplies
the largest term by four.

**Which layer to shrink.** In both, the first. It holds $70.83\%$ of the deep
model and $98.7\%$ of the wide one, and in neither case is that because the
layer is doing more work — it is because it faces $784$ inputs.

**The lesson generalises.** For any MLP on wide input, the parameter count is
approximately $d_0 d_1$, and every other decision is noise against it. This is
why an image model does not begin with a dense layer, why a language model's
embedding table is discussed separately from its blocks, and why quoting depth
as a proxy for size is unreliable.
