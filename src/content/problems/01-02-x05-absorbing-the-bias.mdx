The proof in I.2.B03 assumed the bias had been absorbed by appending a constant
$1$ to every input. Prove the two formulations are equivalent, then show that
absorption changes $R$ — and quantify the effect on the mistake bound.

<Hint>
Appending a coordinate changes the norm of every input. Compute the new $R$.
</Hint>

<SolutionToggle>

**Step 1 — the construction.** Define augmented vectors

$$\tilde{\vec{x}} = \begin{bmatrix}\vec{x} \\ 1\end{bmatrix} \in \R^{d+1}, \qquad \tilde{\vec{w}} = \begin{bmatrix}\vec{w} \\ b\end{bmatrix} \in \R^{d+1}$$

**Step 2 — the scores agree.** By the definition of the inner product,

$$\langle \tilde{\vec{w}}, \tilde{\vec{x}}\rangle = \sum_{j=1}^{d} w_j x_j + b \cdot 1 = \langle \vec{w},\vec{x}\rangle + b$$

Identical for every $\vec{x}$, so the two models classify identically and have
the same decision boundary in $\R^d$. $\blacksquare$

**Step 3 — the updates agree.** The augmented update is
$\tilde{\vec{w}} \leftarrow \tilde{\vec{w}} + y\tilde{\vec{x}}$. Reading off its
components: the first $d$ give $\vec{w} \leftarrow \vec{w} + y\vec{x}$, and the
last gives $b \leftarrow b + y\cdot 1 = b + y$. Exactly Definition 7. So the
two runs are the same run, step for step.

**Step 4 — what it costs.** The norm grows:

$$\lVert\tilde{\vec{x}}\rVert = \sqrt{\lVert\vec{x}\rVert^2 + 1}$$

so the radius becomes $\tilde{R} = \sqrt{R^2 + 1}$, and the bound becomes

$$k \le \frac{R^2 + 1}{\tilde\gamma^{\,2}}$$

**Step 5 — quantify.** For the data of I.2.B02, $R = 2$ so $\tilde R = \sqrt5 = 2.2361$,
and $R^2 + 1 = 5$ against $R^2 = 4$: the numerator rises by $25\%$. For data
scaled so that $R = 10$, the rise is $101/100$, or $1\%$. **The penalty is
severe only when the data lies close to the origin**, because then the appended
$1$ is comparable to the data's own scale.

**The practical reading.** This is the same phenomenon as feature scaling. The
constant feature has magnitude $1$ regardless of the units the other features
are measured in, so if the inputs are in millimetres the bias feature is
negligible and if they are normalised to unit variance it is not. A bound that
changes when you change units is telling you the units matter — and here they do.

**Why the theorem is stated in the absorbed form anyway.** With $b$ separate,
Step 2 of I.2.B03 acquires a $b$-update term that must be bounded separately,
and the proof becomes two interleaved inductions instead of one. Absorption buys
a clean proof at the price of a slightly looser constant, which is the usual
trade and worth naming as such rather than hiding.
