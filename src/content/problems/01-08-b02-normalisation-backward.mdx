<Statement>
Derive the batch-normalisation backward pass including epsilon. Check it
against finite differences, and identify the term lost by treating the
variance as a constant.
</Statement>

<Given>
A group of $m$ scalars, $\mu=m^{-1}\sum_i x_i$,
$v=m^{-1}\sum_i(x_i-\mu)^2$, $r=\sqrt{v+\varepsilon}$,
$\hat x_i=(x_i-\mu)/r$, and $y_i=\gamma\hat x_i+\beta$.
The upstream derivative is $g_i=\partial\mathcal L/\partial y_i$.
For a numerical check take $x=(-1,0,1)$, $\varepsilon=1/3$,
$\gamma=2$, $\beta=0$, and $g=(1,-1,2)$.
</Given>

<Find>
The full Jacobian of the standardisation, input gradients, affine-parameter
gradients, and the reason the input gradients sum to zero. Decide whether the
gradient is also orthogonal to the centred input when epsilon is positive.
</Find>

<Strategy>
Differentiate the mean first, then the variance, then the inverse standard
deviation. Only then contract the Jacobian with the upstream derivative.
</Strategy>

<Solution>

Write $c_i=x_i-\mu$. Since $\sum_i c_i=0$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\partial c_i}{\partial x_j}&=\delta_{ij}-\frac1m,\\
\frac{\partial v}{\partial x_j}
&=\frac2m\sum_i c_i\left(\delta_{ij}-\frac1m\right)\\
&=\frac{2c_j}{m}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Consequently $\partial r/\partial x_j=c_j/(mr)$.
The product rule applied to $c_i r^{-1}$ gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
J_{ij}&=\frac{\delta_{ij}-1/m}{r}-\frac{c_i c_j}{mr^3}\\
&=\frac1r\left(\delta_{ij}-\frac1m-\frac{\hat x_i\hat x_j}{m}\right),
\end{aligned}
$$

which is (I.8.3). Omitting the derivative of the variance loses the last term.

Contract with $\gamma g_i$ and sum over $i$:

$$
\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial x_j}
=\frac\gamma r\left(g_j-\overline g-\hat x_j\overline{g\hat x}\right).
$$

This is (I.8.4). The parameter derivatives are

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\gamma}&=\sum_i g_i\hat x_i,\\
\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\beta}&=\sum_i g_i.
\end{aligned}
$$

For the supplied check, $\mu=0$, $v=2/3$, and $r=1$ exactly. Thus
$\hat x=(-1,0,1)$, $\overline g=2/3$, and
$\overline{g\hat x}=1/3$. Substitution gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
\nabla_x\mathcal L&=(4/3,-10/3,2),\\
\partial_\gamma\mathcal L&=1,\qquad \partial_\beta\mathcal L=2.
\end{aligned}
$$

To check an input derivative numerically, define
$F(x)=\sum_i g_i y_i(x)$ and recompute **all** statistics after each perturbation:

$$
d_j^{\mathrm{FD}}
=\frac{F(x+h e_j)-F(x-h e_j)}{2h}.
$$

The reproduction script uses $h=10^{-5}$ and asserts a maximum absolute
disagreement below $10^{-8}$. It also tests a non-symmetric input, so the check
does not rely only on $r=1$.

Summing the analytic gradients cancels both mean terms because
$\sum_j\hat x_j=0$. Their sum is zero. A common shift in every input cannot
change the normalised output.

The centred input is different. Direct multiplication gives

$$
J\vec c=\frac{\varepsilon}{r^3}\vec c.
$$

For nonzero centred data this vanishes only when epsilon is zero.
In the numerical example $\vec c^{\mathsf T}\nabla_x\mathcal L=2/3$,
not zero. Exact scale invariance would predict the wrong answer.

</Solution>

<Answer>
The Jacobian is (I.8.3), and the efficient input derivative is (I.8.4).
The checked gradient is $(1.333333,-3.333333,2.000000)$.
Its sum is zero, but its dot product with the centred input is $2/3$.
</Answer>

<CheckSanity>
A constant upstream gradient produces zero input gradient when gamma is shared
over the batch group: it asks to change a sum that normalisation has fixed.
The computation needs two reductions and one elementwise pass, not storage of
an $m$-by-$m$ matrix.
</CheckSanity>

<Breaks>
This is the training derivative. Frozen evaluation statistics yield only the
diagonal derivative $\gamma/r_{\mathrm{run}}$. For layer normalisation,
feature-specific gamma values must multiply the upstream gradient before
the group reductions.
</Breaks>

<Variation>
Derive the layer-normalisation input derivative with distinct $\gamma_i$.
Set $u_i=\gamma_i g_i$ and replace the expression by
$(u_j-\overline u-\hat x_j\overline{u\hat x})/r$.
Test why substituting the average gamma instead is generally wrong.
</Variation>
