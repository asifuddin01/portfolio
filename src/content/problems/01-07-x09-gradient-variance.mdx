Build a population of per-example gradients with known mean and variance, draw
four thousand minibatches at each of six sizes, and compare the measured
variance of the batch mean against $\sigma^2/B$. Check unbiasedness at the same
time, and say why the population was given a deliberately non-normal shape.

<Hint>
Two claims are being tested, and they need different evidence: that the mean of
the batch means sits at $\mu$, and that their spread falls as $1/B$.
</Hint>

<SolutionToggle>

**The population.** $N = 10\,000$ values with $\mu = -0.000960$ and
$\sigma^2 = 7.859247$. The shape is a sawtooth with occasional large spikes —
deliberately not a bell curve, so that agreement cannot be an artefact of the
per-example gradients happening to be normal. Real per-example gradients are not
normal either; they are heavy-tailed, with a few examples contributing far more
than the rest.

**The measurements.** 4000 batches at each size:

| $B$ | measured $\operatorname{Var}$ | $\sigma^2/B$ | ratio | mean of means |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | $7.845540$ | $7.859247$ | $0.998$ | $0.087900$ |
| 2 | $4.076844$ | $3.929624$ | $1.037$ | $0.017487$ |
| 8 | $0.998829$ | $0.982406$ | $1.017$ | $-0.012431$ |
| 32 | $0.248467$ | $0.245601$ | $1.012$ | $0.020018$ |
| 128 | $0.061120$ | $0.061400$ | $0.995$ | $0.007109$ |
| 512 | $0.014889$ | $0.015350$ | $0.970$ | $0.002993$ |

**Unbiasedness.** Every "mean of means" sits near $\mu = -0.000960$, and the
agreement tightens as $B$ grows — not because the estimator becomes less biased,
which it never was, but because the estimate of its mean becomes more precise.
Unbiasedness is a property at every $B$; only our ability to see it improves.

**The $1/B$ law.** Ratios between $0.970$ and $1.037$ across three orders of
magnitude in $B$. The variance falls by a factor of $512$ from $B{=}1$ to
$B{=}512$, matching the prediction to within four percent at the worst point.

**Where the residual error comes from.** The ratios wander by a few percent
because 4000 trials estimate a variance with a standard error of roughly
$\sqrt{2/(4000-1)} \approx 2.2\%$ — so the observed spread is what sampling
noise alone predicts. Measuring a variance is itself a noisy business, and
quoting three decimal places on the ratio would be quoting the noise.

**Why the non-normal shape matters.** The derivation in I.7.X10 uses only
linearity of expectation and independence — nothing about the distribution's
shape. A demonstration on Gaussian data would be consistent with a much weaker
claim that happened to need normality. Using a spiky population tests the
theorem that was actually proved.

**What this does not establish.** The law assumes independent draws. Sorted
batches (I.7.X05), duplicated examples, or any correlation within a batch break
the variance half while leaving the unbiasedness half intact — and no measurement
of this kind detects that, because the sampling here was constructed to be
independent. The experiment confirms the theorem under its hypotheses; it says
nothing about a pipeline that violates them.
