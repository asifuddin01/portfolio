Reverse mode is not simply better than forward mode. For
$f: \R^n \to \R^m$, price both, decide which wins for four different tasks, and
say what reverse mode pays for its advantage.

<Hint>
A forward sweep propagates a derivative with respect to *one input*; a reverse
sweep propagates the derivative *of one output*. Count how many of each you
need to fill a Jacobian.
</Hint>

<SolutionToggle>

**The rule.** Forward mode carries $\partial \cdot / \partial x_i$ for one
input $i$ through the whole graph, so it fills one column of the Jacobian per
sweep: $n$ sweeps. Reverse mode carries $\partial y_j / \partial \cdot$ for one
output, filling one row per sweep: $m$ sweeps. That is I.6.T2, and everything
else follows from it.

| task | $n$ | $m$ | forward | reverse | cheaper |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| training a network | $10^8$ | 1 | $10^8$ | 1 | reverse |
| a scalar of few inputs | 3 | 1 | 3 | 1 | reverse |
| sensitivity of an ODE | 4 | $10^6$ | 4 | $10^6$ | forward |
| Jacobian of a $1024^2$ layer | 1024 | 1024 | 1024 | 1024 | tie |
| one directional derivative | $10^8$ | $10^6$ | 1 | $10^6$ | forward |

**Training.** $n$ in the hundred millions, $m = 1$. Reverse mode is cheaper by a
factor of $10^8$ — the entire parameter count. This is the case the field is
built around, and it is why "autodiff" and "backpropagation" are used
interchangeably even though the first is much more general than the second.

**The last row is the interesting one.** Both $n$ and $m$ are enormous, yet
forward mode wins outright, because only one *direction* is wanted rather than
the whole Jacobian. A single forward sweep seeded with that direction returns
$\mat{J}\vec{v}$ — a Jacobian–vector product — at the price of one evaluation.
Reverse mode has no way to produce it in fewer than $m$ sweeps.

**What reverse mode pays.** Memory. Forward mode carries one extra value
alongside each node and never looks back, so nothing is stored:
$32\,768$ values live at any moment for the model of I.6.B05. Reverse mode must
keep the whole tape to have the local Jacobians on the way back:
$1\,572\,864$ values, $48\times$ more, exactly the depth of the network.

That is the trade in one line — reverse mode buys a factor of $n$ in arithmetic
with a factor of $L$ in memory, and the exchange is worth it precisely because
$n \gg L$.

**Where neither is cheap.** The middle row: a full $1024 \times 1024$ Jacobian
costs 1024 sweeps either way. There is no mode that produces a dense Jacobian
cheaply, and a method that needs one — some second-order and some
sensitivity-analysis methods — is expensive for a reason no implementation
detail will fix.
