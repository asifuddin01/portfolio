Prove that the minibatch gradient is an unbiased estimator of the full-batch
gradient, and derive its variance. State precisely which property of the
sampling each half of the result requires, so it is clear what breaks when the
sampling is wrong.

<Hint>
Linearity of expectation gives the first half and needs almost nothing.
Independence is what the second half needs, and it is a strictly stronger
assumption.
</Hint>

<SolutionToggle>

**Setup.** The full objective is the mean over $N$ examples,
$\mathcal{L}(\theta) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\ell_i(\theta)$, so
$\nabla\mathcal{L} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_i \nabla\ell_i$. A batch is $B$ indices
$i_1, \dots, i_B$ drawn uniformly at random with replacement, and

$$
\hat{g}_B = \frac{1}{B}\sum_{j=1}^{B}\nabla\ell_{i_j}
$$

**Unbiasedness.** For a single uniformly drawn index,

$$
\mathbb{E}[\nabla\ell_{i}] = \sum_{k=1}^{N}\frac{1}{N}\nabla\ell_k = \nabla\mathcal{L}
$$

By linearity of expectation — which requires no independence at all —

$$
\mathbb{E}[\hat{g}_B] = \frac{1}{B}\sum_{j=1}^{B}\mathbb{E}[\nabla\ell_{i_j}] = \nabla\mathcal{L}
$$

The only property used is that each index is *marginally* uniform. This is why
unbiasedness survives sampling without replacement, and why it fails under the
sorted batches of I.7.X05 — there the marginal distribution of an index within
batch 1 is uniform over class 0 only.

**Variance.** Write $\Sigma = \operatorname{Var}[\nabla\ell_i]$ for the
per-example covariance. Now independence is needed: for independent draws the
variance of a sum is the sum of the variances, so

$$
\operatorname{Var}[\hat{g}_B] = \frac{1}{B^2}\sum_{j=1}^{B}\Sigma = \frac{\Sigma}{B}
$$

which is (I.7.2). The standard error is $\sigma/\sqrt{B}$.

**What each half needs.** Unbiasedness needs marginal uniformity. Variance
needs independence between the draws. The gap between them is the whole reason
correlated batches are dangerous: a batch of near-duplicate examples can still
be unbiased while having a variance far above $\Sigma/B$, so the $1/B$
accounting overstates how much noise the batch removed. Deduplication is a
variance intervention, not only a data-hygiene one.

**Sampling without replacement.** Real training shuffles and takes disjoint
batches, which is sampling without replacement. Unbiasedness is unaffected. The
variance gains a finite-population correction $\frac{N-B}{N-1}$, which is
negligible while $B \ll N$ and becomes decisive as $B \to N$ — at $B = N$ the
variance is zero, because the batch *is* the dataset. The $1/B$ law is the
large-$N$ limit, and full-batch descent is its other end.

**The consequence for practice.** Halving the noise costs four times the
arithmetic, so the returns are square-root. That single fact is why batch size
is a trade rather than a setting to maximise, and why the critical batch size of
McCandlish and colleagues exists: past the point where the gradient is already
accurate, additional examples buy accuracy nobody needed.
