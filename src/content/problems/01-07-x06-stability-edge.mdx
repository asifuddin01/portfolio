Take the recurrence $e_{t+1} = (1 - \eta\lambda)e_t$ and examine the three
boundary cases: $\eta\lambda = 1$, $\eta\lambda = 2$, and $\eta\lambda \to 2^-$.
Say what the iteration does in each, and what the third implies about tuning a
learning rate by trying larger values until something breaks.

<Hint>
The multiplier is $1 - \eta\lambda$. Evaluate it, and remember it is a signed
quantity — the sign carries information the magnitude does not.
</Hint>

<SolutionToggle>

**$\eta\lambda = 1$: exact, in one step.** The multiplier is $0$, so
$e_1 = 0$ regardless of $e_0$. This is Newton's method for a quadratic: the step
$\eta = 1/\lambda$ lands on the optimum exactly. It is also the reason a
perfectly conditioned problem is trivial — with all eigenvalues equal, one
$\eta$ solves every direction at once.

**$\eta\lambda = 2$: perpetual oscillation.** The multiplier is $-1$, so
$e_t = (-1)^t e_0$. The error alternates in sign and *never changes magnitude*.
The iterate bounces between two points forever: the loss is constant, the
gradient is non-zero, and nothing is converging or diverging. Neither the loss
curve nor the gradient norm reveals progress, because there is none.

**$\eta\lambda \to 2^-$: convergence at an arbitrarily slow rate.** The
multiplier approaches $-1$ from inside, so the error still contracts — but by a
factor approaching $1$. Steps to reach a target grow without bound as the
threshold is approached, while the iteration remains formally convergent
throughout.

**What that implies about tuning.** The common procedure is to raise $\eta$ until
training becomes unstable and then back off. This limit says the procedure has a
trap: just below the threshold the run is *stable and useless*, contracting so
slowly that it is indistinguishable from a plateau over any finite budget. It
looks like a learning-rate problem of the opposite kind — too small — and the
natural response of raising $\eta$ further crosses into divergence.

The sign is the tell. Below $\eta\lambda = 1$ the multiplier is positive and the
error decays monotonically. Above it the multiplier is negative and the error
alternates, so the parameter oscillates around the optimum while approaching it.
A trajectory that zigzags is already past $1/\lambda$ in that direction and is
in the upper half of the stable band — which is visible in a parameter trace and
invisible in a loss curve.

**Why the loss hides it.** For a quadratic the loss is $\tfrac{1}{2}\lambda e^2$,
which is even in $e$. Both signs give the same loss, so the alternation that
distinguishes the two regimes is exactly what the loss discards. This is a small
instance of a general point: a scalar summary of a high-dimensional trajectory
loses the information needed to diagnose it, and monitoring only the loss is
monitoring the one quantity engineered to be flat.
