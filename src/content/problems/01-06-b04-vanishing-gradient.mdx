<Statement>
Backpropagation is exact. Show that exactness is not sufficiency: exhibit a
network on which the gradient reaching the first layer is correct to the last
bit and numerically zero, and establish the depth at which that happens. Then
show that the obvious remedy — larger weights — makes it worse.
</Statement>

<Given>
A chain of scalar sigmoid units, the simplest object with the property under
study:

$$
a^{(0)} = 0.5, \qquad z^{(\ell)} = w\,a^{(\ell-1)}, \qquad a^{(\ell)} = \sigma(z^{(\ell)})
$$

for $\ell = 1 \dots L$, with one shared weight $w$ and no biases. The adjoint
leaving the loss is normalised to $1$.
</Given>

<Find>
A bound on $|\partial L/\partial a^{(0)}|$ in terms of $L$ alone; the measured
ratio at $L = 10$ and $L = 40$ for $w = 1$; the smallest $L$ at which the ratio
falls below fp32's smallest normal number; and the effect of raising $w$.
</Find>

<Strategy>
Apply the recurrence (I.6.3) down the chain and collect factors. Each layer
contributes two: the weight, and the slope of the nonlinearity at the stored
pre-activation. The second factor has a ceiling that does not depend on
anything trainable, and a product of numbers below a ceiling is bounded by the
ceiling raised to the depth.
</Strategy>

<Solution>

**Step 1 — the bound.** For the logistic function $\sigma' = \sigma(1-\sigma)$,
maximised where $\sigma = 1/2$:

$$
\max_z \sigma'(z) = \tfrac{1}{2}\left(1 - \tfrac{1}{2}\right) = \tfrac{1}{4}
$$

The run confirms $\sigma'(0) = 0.250000$. Now walk the chain back. Each layer
multiplies the travelling adjoint by $w$ and by $\sigma'(z^{(\ell)})$, so

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial a^{(0)}} = \prod_{\ell=1}^{L} w\,\sigma'(z^{(\ell)}),
\qquad
\left|\frac{\partial L}{\partial a^{(0)}}\right| \le \left(\frac{|w|}{4}\right)^{L}
$$

With $w = 1$ this is $4^{-L}$, and nothing about the data, the loss or the
training procedure can raise it. It is a property of the architecture.

**Step 2 — the measurement.** Propagating the adjoint explicitly:

| depth | measured ratio | bound $4^{-L}$ |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | $2.350037 \times 10^{-1}$ | $2.5 \times 10^{-1}$ |
| 2 | $5.340838 \times 10^{-2}$ | $6.25 \times 10^{-2}$ |
| 5 | $6.079863 \times 10^{-4}$ | $9.765625 \times 10^{-4}$ |
| 10 | $3.483116 \times 10^{-7}$ | $9.536743 \times 10^{-7}$ |
| 20 | $1.143096 \times 10^{-13}$ | $9.094947 \times 10^{-13}$ |
| 40 | $1.231153 \times 10^{-26}$ | $8.271806 \times 10^{-25}$ |

The measured values sit below the bound at every depth, as they must, and the
gap widens because the units are not all sitting at their maximum slope.

**Step 3 — the depth where it stops being a small number.** fp32's smallest
normal value is $1.17549435 \times 10^{-38}$. The first depth at which the
ratio falls below it is

$$
L = 59, \qquad \text{ratio } 5.901063 \times 10^{-39}
$$

At that depth the gradient of the first layer is not small. It is
*unrepresentable*: it enters the subnormal range, where fp32 has almost no
precision left, and one more layer flushes it to exactly zero. The parameter
receives an update of zero and stays at its initialisation for the whole of
training, while the last layers train perfectly well. The loss falls, then
stalls, and nothing about the curve says which layers stopped.

**Step 4 — why more weight makes it worse.** The bound $(|w|/4)^L$ suggests
$|w| > 4$ should grow the adjoint rather than shrink it. Measured:

| $w$ | depth 10 | depth 40 |
|---|---|---|
| 1.0 | $3.483116 \times 10^{-7}$ | $1.231153 \times 10^{-26}$ |
| 2.0 | $2.954702 \times 10^{-6}$ | $1.227533 \times 10^{-23}$ |
| 4.0 | $5.486035 \times 10^{-11}$ | $1.536491 \times 10^{-44}$ |
| 4.5 | $1.449225 \times 10^{-12}$ | $3.182489 \times 10^{-51}$ |

Doubling $w$ to 2 helps a little. Raising it to 4 makes the gradient at depth 40
eighteen orders of magnitude *smaller* than at $w = 1$. The bound is not wrong;
it is not tight, and the reason is that the two factors are not independent. A
large $w$ drives $z$ away from zero, and a sigmoid away from zero is saturated,
so $\sigma'(z)$ collapses. The weight enters the product linearly and takes the
slope down exponentially with it.

This is what makes the failure a counterexample rather than a difficulty. There
is no setting of the free parameter that rescues it: small $w$ loses to the
factor $w$, large $w$ loses to saturation, and the best case is a shallow
optimum near $w = 2$ that still vanishes, only more slowly.

</Solution>

<Answer>

$|\partial L/\partial a^{(0)}| \le (|w|/4)^{L}$, so with $w = 1$ the adjoint is
bounded by $4^{-L}$ regardless of training.

Measured at $w = 1$: $3.483116 \times 10^{-7}$ at depth 10, and
$1.231153 \times 10^{-26}$ at depth 40. The ratio falls below fp32's smallest
normal at $L = 59$, where it is $5.901063 \times 10^{-39}$.

Raising $w$ does not help: at depth 40, $w = 4$ gives
$1.536491 \times 10^{-44}$, worse than $w = 1$ by eighteen orders of magnitude,
because a large weight saturates the sigmoid and its slope falls faster than the
weight rises.

</Answer>

<CheckSanity>
**Every measured ratio is below its bound.** $4^{-L}$ was derived from
$\sigma' \le 1/4$ with no reference to the values, so a measurement above it
would mean the propagation is wrong rather than that the bound is loose.

**Depth 1 nearly attains the bound.** $0.235004$ against $0.25$: the first unit
sees $z = 0.5$, close enough to zero that its slope is close to maximal. The
later units see larger $z$, saturate more, and the product falls away from the
bound — which is why the gap at depth 40 is two orders of magnitude.

**The arithmetic of the exponent checks out.** $4^{-59} = 8.6 \times 10^{-36}$,
and the measured $5.9 \times 10^{-39}$ is about 1500 times smaller — consistent
with a mean slope near $0.23$ rather than $0.25$, since
$(0.23/0.25)^{59} \approx 0.0075$.
</CheckSanity>

<Breaks>
The chain here is one unit wide, and width changes the constant without changing
the mechanism. In a real layer the adjoint is multiplied by
$(\mat{W}^{(\ell)})^{\mathsf{T}}$ rather than by a scalar, so the relevant
quantity is that matrix's largest singular value and the product is over
matrices rather than numbers. A width-512 layer can have a spectral norm well
above one while every individual weight is small, which is why the same
architecture can vanish in one initialisation and explode in another.

The demonstration also uses the logistic function, whose $1/4$ ceiling is what
makes the bound so sharp. ReLU has slope exactly $1$ wherever it is active, so
this particular argument does not apply to it at all — and the failure returns
in a different form, as dead units whose slope is exactly $0$ rather than merely
small. The general lesson is about products of Jacobians, and the specific
number $1/4$ belongs to one activation function.

Nothing here is an argument against depth. It is an argument that depth needs a
route the product cannot close, which is exactly (I.6.5): a residual block
contributes $\mat{I} + \mat{J}_F$, and the identity term keeps a path of gain
one open however many blocks are stacked.
</Breaks>

<Variation>
Replace $\sigma$ with $\tanh$, whose derivative is $1 - \tanh^2$ and whose
ceiling is $1$ rather than $1/4$. Predict the new bound, then find the depth at
which the ratio underflows fp32 and compare it with 59. Does a four-times
better ceiling buy four times the depth?
</Variation>
