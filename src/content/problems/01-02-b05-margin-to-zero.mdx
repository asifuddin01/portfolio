<Statement>
Analyse the mistake bound $(R/\gamma)^2$ as $\gamma \to 0^{+}$ at fixed $R$.
Tabulate it, state the limit, and say precisely what the theorem still
guarantees for near-separable data — and what it stops guaranteeing.
</Statement>

<Given>
Novikoff's bound $k \le (R/\gamma)^2$ from I.2.B03, with the radius fixed at
$R = 1$ and the geometric margin $\gamma$ shrinking.
</Given>

<Find>
The bound at $\gamma \in \{0.5,\ 0.1,\ 0.01,\ 10^{-3},\ 10^{-6}\}$; the limit as
$\gamma \to 0^{+}$; and the status of the guarantee at $\gamma = 0$ exactly.
</Find>

<Strategy>
Evaluate, then take the limit, then separate two cases that look continuous but
are not: $\gamma$ small and $\gamma$ zero.
</Strategy>

<Solution>

**Step 1 — tabulate.** With $R = 1$ the bound is simply $\gamma^{-2}$.

| $\gamma$ | $(R/\gamma)^2$ |
|---|---|
| $0.5$ | $4$ |
| $0.1$ | $100$ |
| $0.01$ | $10{,}000$ |
| $10^{-3}$ | $10^{6}$ |
| $10^{-6}$ | $10^{12}$ |

**Step 2 — the rate.** The bound is quadratic in $1/\gamma$, so halving the
margin quadruples the work:

$$\frac{(R/(\gamma/2))^2}{(R/\gamma)^2} = 4$$

This is worth stating as a rule of thumb, because it is the reason
near-separable problems are qualitatively different from comfortably separable
ones rather than merely slower.

**Step 3 — the limit.**

$$\lim_{\gamma \to 0^{+}} \left(\frac{R}{\gamma}\right)^{2} = +\infty$$

**Step 4 — the two cases, which are not the same.**

*Case $\gamma > 0$, however small.* The data **is** separable. The theorem
applies and the algorithm **does** terminate, in finitely many updates. The
guarantee is intact — it has merely become useless in practice, because a
finite number can exceed any budget anyone has.

*Case $\gamma = 0$ exactly.* There is no separating $\vec{u}$ with positive
margin. Step 1 of the proof in I.2.B03 has no lower bound to offer, the squeeze
never closes, and the conclusion is not weakened but **withdrawn**. The
algorithm does not terminate at all.

The distinction matters because the two cases are indistinguishable from
outside. A run that has made $10^6$ updates might be at $\gamma = 10^{-3}$ and
one update from finishing, or at $\gamma = 0$ and never finishing. The
perceptron has no test that separates them, and no amount of patience resolves
it.

</Solution>

<Answer>
$$\lim_{\gamma \to 0^{+}} \left(\frac{R}{\gamma}\right)^{2} = +\infty, \qquad \text{quadratically: halving } \gamma \text{ quadruples the bound.}$$

For every $\gamma > 0$ the guarantee holds and termination is certain. At
$\gamma = 0$ the guarantee does not degrade — it disappears, and so does
termination.
</Answer>

<CheckSanity>
**The tabulated values satisfy the quadratic rule.** From $\gamma = 0.1$ to
$\gamma = 0.01$ the margin fell by $10\times$ and the bound rose by $100\times$,
as $\gamma^{-2}$ requires.

**The bound is scale-invariant in the right way.** Doubling every coordinate
doubles both $R$ and $\gamma$, leaving $R/\gamma$ — and therefore the bound —
unchanged. A mistake count must not depend on the units the data is measured
in, and it does not.

**It matches the worked run.** I.2.B02 had $R = 2$, $\gamma = 0.4472$, bound
$20$, actual $2$. Consistent.
</CheckSanity>

<Breaks>
The whole analysis holds $R$ fixed while $\gamma$ moves. In real data they move
together: adding a distant outlier raises $R$ *and* usually lowers $\gamma$, so
the bound degrades on both factors at once and the quadratic rate above is an
underestimate of the damage. The single-variable limit is a clean statement
about the formula, not a forecast about a dataset.
</Breaks>

<Variation>
Fix $\gamma = 0.1$ and let $R \to \infty$ instead. Compare the rate of blow-up
with the $\gamma \to 0$ case, and say which of the two a practitioner can
actually control.
</Variation>
