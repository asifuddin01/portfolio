<Statement>
Give the shape of every tensor Adam holds for a concrete model, then establish
the property those shapes exist to provide: that the step taken in each
coordinate is unchanged when the loss is multiplied by a constant.
</Statement>

<Given>
The network of I.5.B03 — widths $(784, 512, 256, 128, 10)$, four affine layers,
$567\,434$ parameters. Adam as in (I.7.6), with $\beta_1 = 0.9$,
$\beta_2 = 0.999$, $\varepsilon = 10^{-8}$.
</Given>

<Find>
The shape of $m$ and $v$ for each parameter tensor and the totals; then, for a
loss rescaled to $c\mathcal{L}$, the effect on $g$, $m$, $v$ and the update.
</Find>

<Strategy>
Every moment estimate is an average of things with the shape of the gradient,
and a gradient has the shape of its parameter — so the shapes follow without
any new argument. The scale question is answered by tracking a factor of $c$
through (I.7.6) and seeing where it cancels.
</Strategy>

<Solution>

**Step 1 — the shapes.** $m$ and $v$ are elementwise running averages of $g$ and
$g^2$, so each has exactly the shape of the parameter it belongs to:

| tensor | shape | entries | $m$ | $v$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $\mat{W}^{(1)}$ | $784 \times 512$ | $401\,408$ | same | same |
| $\vec{b}^{(1)}$ | $1 \times 512$ | $512$ | same | same |
| $\mat{W}^{(2)}$ | $512 \times 256$ | $131\,072$ | same | same |
| $\vec{b}^{(2)}$ | $1 \times 256$ | $256$ | same | same |
| $\mat{W}^{(3)}$ | $256 \times 128$ | $32\,768$ | same | same |
| $\vec{b}^{(3)}$ | $1 \times 128$ | $128$ | same | same |
| $\mat{W}^{(4)}$ | $128 \times 10$ | $1\,280$ | same | same |
| $\vec{b}^{(4)}$ | $1 \times 10$ | $10$ | same | same |
| **total** | | $567\,434$ | $567\,434$ | $567\,434$ |

Two extra copies, $1\,134\,868$ values, $4.54$ MB in fp32 against the model's
own $2.27$ MB — the $2\times$ of I.7.B04, on a concrete model.

Nothing is per-layer or per-tensor: the state is *per scalar parameter*. That is
what "adaptive" means here, and it is the whole reason the state is this large.

**Step 2 — rescale the loss.** Replace $\mathcal{L}$ by $c\mathcal{L}$ for a
constant $c > 0$. Gradients are linear, so every $g_t$ becomes $cg_t$. Both
moments are linear in their inputs:

$$
m_t = \beta_1 m_{t-1} + (1-\beta_1)cg_t \Longrightarrow cm_t,
\qquad
v_t = \beta_2 v_{t-1} + (1-\beta_2)c^2g_t^2 \Longrightarrow c^2v_t
$$

The first moment picks up $c$ and the second picks up $c^2$, because one
averages the gradient and the other its square.

**Step 3 — the cancellation.** Bias correction divides both by constants, so the
factors survive it: $\hat{m}_t \to c\hat{m}_t$ and $\hat{v}_t \to c^2\hat{v}_t$.
The step is

$$
\frac{c\,\hat{m}_t}{\sqrt{c^2\hat{v}_t} + \varepsilon}
= \frac{c\,\hat{m}_t}{c\sqrt{\hat{v}_t} + \varepsilon}
$$

For $\varepsilon$ negligible against $c\sqrt{\hat{v}_t}$ the $c$ cancels and the
update is unchanged. **Adam's step is invariant to rescaling the loss**, and by
the same argument to rescaling any individual coordinate's gradient.

**Step 4 — what plain descent does instead.** Its update is $-\eta cg_t$, which
scales with $c$ directly. Doubling the loss doubles every step, so a rate tuned
for one loss scale is wrong for another — and since a sum over a batch and a
mean over a batch differ by a factor of $B$, that is not a hypothetical.

**Step 5 — where the invariance fails.** $\varepsilon$ is an additive constant
and does not scale, so the cancellation is approximate. When $c$ is small enough
that $c\sqrt{\hat{v}} \lesssim \varepsilon$, the denominator is dominated by
$\varepsilon$ and the step becomes $\approx c\hat{m}/\varepsilon$ — linear in
$c$ again, like plain descent. So the invariance holds over a wide range and
breaks at the bottom of it, which is one reason $\varepsilon$ is a number worth
knowing rather than a formality: it sets where the adaptive behaviour stops.

</Solution>

<Answer>

$m$ and $v$ each have the shape of the parameters, tensor for tensor:
$567\,434$ entries apiece, $1\,134\,868$ values of state, $4.54$ MB in fp32
against $2.27$ MB of weights.

Under $\mathcal{L} \to c\mathcal{L}$: $g \to cg$, $m \to cm$, $v \to c^2v$, and
the update $\hat{m}/(\sqrt{\hat{v}} + \varepsilon)$ is unchanged while
$c\sqrt{\hat{v}} \gg \varepsilon$. Plain descent's update scales with $c$.

</Answer>

<CheckSanity>
**The state has no shape of its own.** Every entry of $m$ and $v$ corresponds to
exactly one scalar parameter, so a memory estimate that treats optimiser state
as a per-layer overhead is wrong by orders of magnitude.

**The exponents on $c$ are 1 and 2.** They must be: $m$ averages $g$ and $v$
averages $g^2$. Getting $c$ and $c$ would mean the square had been dropped, and
the cancellation would then leave a stray factor rather than none.

**The step's magnitude is about $\eta$.** With $\hat{m} \approx \sqrt{\hat{v}}$
for a steady gradient, the ratio is near 1 and each coordinate moves by roughly
$\eta$ per step regardless of its gradient's size. That is the property that
makes a single $\eta$ serviceable across parameters of very different scales,
and it is also why Adam's $\eta$ is typically $10^{-3}$ where SGD's might be
$10^{-1}$ — they are measured in different things.
</CheckSanity>

<Breaks>
Scale invariance is a property of the *step*, not of training. Rescaling the loss
leaves Adam's trajectory unchanged but not anything coupled to the loss value:
gradient clipping by norm triggers at different points, a fixed convergence
threshold on the loss means something different, and in mixed precision the
gradient scaler exists precisely because the raw magnitude decides whether fp16
overflows or flushes to zero. "Adam does not care about the loss scale" is true
of one equation and false of the system around it.

The invariance is also per-coordinate, which is a double-edged property. A
parameter whose gradient is genuinely tiny because it barely matters is stepped
just as far as one whose gradient is large, since only the ratio survives. That
is the intended behaviour and it is also why Adam can keep moving parameters
that plain descent would leave alone — sometimes usefully, sometimes as an
argument for decoupled weight decay, which I.7.X04 takes up.

Finally, $\varepsilon$ sits *outside* the square root in (I.7.6) and some
implementations place it inside. The two differ, and the difference shows up
exactly in the small-gradient regime where the invariance is already breaking
down. It is not a detail worth arguing about often, but it is a real difference
between two things both called Adam.
</Breaks>

<Variation>
Work out what happens under a rescaling of the *parameters* rather than the loss
— replace $\theta$ by $a\theta$ with the model reparameterised to compensate. Is
Adam invariant to that too? The answer is different from Step 3's, and it is the
reason normalisation layers still matter when Adam is in use.
</Variation>
