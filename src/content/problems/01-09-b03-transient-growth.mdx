<Statement>
Disprove the claim that recurrent eigenvalues below one prevent every
finite-time amplification.
</Statement>

<Given>
Consider the linear state update $\vec h_t=\mat W\vec h_{t-1}$ with

$$
\mat W=\begin{bmatrix}1/2&4\\0&1/2\end{bmatrix}.
$$

Both eigenvalues are $1/2$. Use Euclidean vector norms and let
$\vec e_2=(0,1)^{\mathsf T}$.
</Given>

<Find>
A formula for $\mat W^n$, its effect on $\vec e_2$, and why this does not
contradict eventual decay or the norm bound (I.9.6).
</Find>

<Strategy>
Write $\mat W=(1/2)\mat I+\mat N$. Since $\mat N^2=0$, only two terms
survive in the binomial expansion.
</Strategy>

<Solution>

For integer $n\geq1$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mat W^n
 &=2^{-n}\mat I+n\,2^{-(n-1)}\mat N\\
 &=\begin{bmatrix}
 2^{-n}&4n\,2^{-(n-1)}\\
 0&2^{-n}
 \end{bmatrix}.
\end{aligned}
$$

It follows that

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mat W\vec e_2&=(4,1/2)^{\mathsf T},\\
\mat W^2\vec e_2&=(4,1/4)^{\mathsf T}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Their norms are approximately $4.031129$ and $4.007805$,
although the initial vector has norm one.
At ten steps the first coordinate is $0.078125$ and the second is
$0.0009765625$.

The matrix is non-normal: it does not commute with its transpose.
Its eigenvalue magnitudes describe the eventual exponential rate, not a
uniform bound on every finite power. The off-diagonal term introduces a
factor of $n$ before the exponential decay wins.
Both $2^{-n}$ and $n2^{-(n-1)}$ tend to zero, so $\mat W^n$ eventually
tends to the zero matrix.

Backward propagation uses $(\mat W^n)^{\mathsf T}$. Its spectral norm equals
that of $\mat W^n$, so a backward direction can be amplified too. For example,
$\mat W^{\mathsf T}(1,0)^{\mathsf T}=(1/2,4)^{\mathsf T}$.

There is no conflict with (I.9.6).
Indeed $\|\mat W\|_2\geq\|\mat W\vec e_2\|_2>4$.
The eigenvalue $1/2$ is not a valid replacement for the factor norm in that
inequality.

</Solution>

<Answer>
Eigenvalues inside the unit circle permit substantial transient
amplification. Here a unit perturbation grows by more than four in one step,
then ultimately decays.
</Answer>

<CheckSanity>
At $n=1$ the power formula returns the supplied matrix. At $n=2$ its
off-diagonal entry is four, matching direct multiplication.
</CheckSanity>

<Breaks>
For a nonlinear cell the temporal Jacobians can change at every step.
A formula for powers of one fixed matrix is then insufficient.
Even a large product norm only guarantees an amplified direction, not that
the actual loss gradient points along it.
</Breaks>

<Variation>
Set the off-diagonal entry to zero. The matrix becomes $(1/2)\mat I$,
and every vector contracts by $2^{-n}$ after $n$ steps. Identify the term
responsible for the transient in the original example.
</Variation>
