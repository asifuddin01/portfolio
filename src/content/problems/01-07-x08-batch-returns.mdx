Price what a larger batch buys. Give the noise, the arithmetic and the noise per
unit of arithmetic across $B$ from 1 to 4096, then identify the regime in which
raising $B$ stops being a good trade and say what the budget does and does not
decide.

<Hint>
By (I.7.2) noise falls as $1/\sqrt{B}$ while cost rises as $B$. Form the product
and read off which direction it moves.
</Hint>

<SolutionToggle>

**The trade.**

| $B$ | standard error | vs $B{=}1$ | arithmetic | error $\times$ cost |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | $1.000000$ | $1.0000$ | 1 | $1.00$ |
| 4 | $0.500000$ | $0.5000$ | 4 | $2.00$ |
| 16 | $0.250000$ | $0.2500$ | 16 | $4.00$ |
| 64 | $0.125000$ | $0.1250$ | 64 | $8.00$ |
| 256 | $0.062500$ | $0.0625$ | 256 | $16.00$ |
| 1024 | $0.031250$ | $0.0312$ | 1024 | $32.00$ |
| 4096 | $0.015625$ | $0.0156$ | 4096 | $64.00$ |

Quadrupling $B$ halves the noise and costs four times the arithmetic, at every
scale. The last column — noise per unit of compute — *doubles* each time, which
is the trade stated in the least flattering way that is still true: measured per
FLOP, a large batch is strictly worse at reducing noise.

**Why anyone uses large batches anyway.** The table prices arithmetic, and
arithmetic is not the binding constraint on modern hardware. A batch of 256 does
not take 256 times as long as a batch of 1 — it takes barely longer, because the
device was idle. The square-root law is about *statistical* efficiency; large
batches win on *hardware* efficiency, and the two pull in opposite directions.

**Where it stops.** Comparing noise against the signal it corrupts:

| $B$ | noise/signal | regime |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | $1.000000$ | noise-dominated: more $B$ helps a lot |
| 16 | $0.250000$ | mixed: linear scaling (I.7.7) roughly holds |
| 256 | $0.062500$ | mixed: linear scaling roughly holds |
| 4096 | $0.015625$ | signal-dominated: more $B$ buys little |
| 65536 | $0.003906$ | signal-dominated: more $B$ buys little |

Once the noise is small against the gradient, further averaging refines a number
that was already accurate enough to step with. That boundary is McCandlish and
colleagues' critical batch size, and the useful thing about it is that it is a
property of the *problem* at a point in training, not of the hardware — it
generally grows as training proceeds and the gradient shrinks.

**What the budget does not decide.** With a fixed budget of $10^6$ example-visits:

| $B$ | steps | noise per step |
|---|---|---|
| 32 | $31\,250$ | $0.17678$ |
| 256 | $3\,906$ | $0.06250$ |
| 2048 | $488$ | $0.02210$ |

Same compute, three different training runs: many noisy steps or few clean ones.
The budget constrains their product and says nothing about which is better —
that is decided by where the problem sits relative to its critical batch, and by
whether the noise is doing anything useful, which by I.7.P02 it sometimes is.
