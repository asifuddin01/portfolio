Three inputs are $(10,-1)$, $(10,0)$ and $(10,1)$. A width-one linear
autoencoder without biases is trained on them as they are. Find the
direction it keeps, the three codes and the loss. Then subtract the mean
first and repeat. Which result is the principal subspace?

<Hint>
Without centring, the matrix in (I.12.2) is the second-moment matrix
$\frac1n\mat X^{\mathsf T}\mat X$ and not the covariance. Apply I.12.T1 to
whichever matrix the loss contains.
</Hint>

<SolutionToggle>

**As they are.** The second-moment matrix is

$$
\frac13\mat X^{\mathsf T}\mat X
 =\begin{bmatrix}100&0\\0&2/3\end{bmatrix}.
$$

The algebra of I.12.T1 applies to this matrix. Its largest eigenvalue is
$100$, with eigenvector $(1,0)$, so the autoencoder keeps the first
coordinate. All three codes are $10$. Every input is reconstructed as
$(10,0)$ and the loss is the other eigenvalue, $2/3=0.666667$.

The code is the same number for every example. It carries no information
about which example it came from.

**Centred.** The mean is $(10,0)$, and the centred inputs are $(0,-1)$,
$(0,0)$ and $(0,1)$. The covariance is
$\begin{bmatrix}0&0\\0&2/3\end{bmatrix}$. Its largest eigenvalue is $2/3$,
with eigenvector $(0,1)$. The codes are $-1$, $0$ and $1$, and the loss is
the other eigenvalue, $0$.

The second result is the principal subspace: the direction along which the
data vary. The first is the direction in which the data are large. The two
matrices differ by $\vec\mu\vec\mu^{\mathsf T}$ for the mean $\vec\mu$, and
when the mean is long compared with the spread, that term dominates and
the leading direction is close to $\vec\mu$ itself.

An autoencoder with a bias in the decoder can learn the mean and escape
this. Without one, centre the data first.

</SolutionToggle>
