<Statement>
The perceptron rule looks like a hand-made heuristic. Show that it is exactly
one step of subgradient descent, with learning rate $1$, on a specific loss —
and identify that loss.
</Statement>

<Given>
The perceptron loss for one example, sometimes called the hinge-at-zero loss:

$$\ell(\vec{w}, b) = \max\big(0,\; -y(\langle\vec{w},\vec{x}\rangle + b)\big)$$

and the update rule of Definition 7: on a mistake,
$\vec{w} \leftarrow \vec{w} + y\vec{x}$ and $b \leftarrow b + y$.
</Given>

<Find>
The subgradient of $\ell$ with respect to $\vec{w}$ and $b$ in both regimes, and
the resulting descent step at $\eta = 1$.
</Find>

<Strategy>
Split on the sign of the margin, differentiate each branch, and compare the
resulting step with the rule. The function is not differentiable at the join,
which is why "subgradient" rather than "gradient" — and that is a feature worth
naming rather than glossing.
</Strategy>

<Solution>

**Step 1 — name the inner quantity.** Let $m = y(\langle\vec{w},\vec{x}\rangle + b)$
be the functional margin, so $\ell = \max(0, -m)$.

**Step 2 — the case $m > 0$ (correct).** Then $-m < 0$, the maximum is achieved
by the constant $0$, and $\ell$ is identically zero in a neighbourhood. A
locally constant function has zero derivative:

$$\nabla_{\vec{w}}\,\ell = \vec{0}, \qquad \partial \ell/\partial b = 0$$

The descent step changes nothing. **This matches the rule's "otherwise do
nothing" exactly.**

**Step 3 — the case $m < 0$ (wrong).** Then $\ell = -m = -y(\langle\vec{w},\vec{x}\rangle + b)$,
which is affine in the parameters and so differentiable. Using
$\nabla_{\vec{w}}\langle\vec{w},\vec{x}\rangle = \vec{x}$
(<ApparatusRef ref="0.MC.04" />):

$$
\nabla_{\vec{w}}\,\ell = -y\vec{x},
\qquad
\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial b} = -y
$$

**Step 4 — take a descent step.** Gradient descent moves against the gradient
(<ApparatusRef ref="0.OP.02" />), with $\eta = 1$:

$$
\vec{w} \leftarrow \vec{w} - \eta\,\nabla_{\vec{w}}\ell = \vec{w} - (1)(-y\vec{x}) = \vec{w} + y\vec{x}
$$

$$
b \leftarrow b - \eta\,\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial b} = b - (1)(-y) = b + y
$$

These are the update rule, term for term.

**Step 5 — the join at $m = 0$.** At exactly zero the function has a kink and no
derivative exists. The subdifferential is the whole interval between the two
one-sided slopes, $\{\,-\alpha\, y\vec{x} : \alpha \in [0,1]\,\}$. Any element is
a legal subgradient step; the perceptron picks $\alpha = 1$, which is why it
updates on $m \le 0$ rather than $m < 0$. That convention, which looked arbitrary
in I.2.B02, is a choice of subgradient.

</Solution>

<Answer>
The perceptron rule is subgradient descent on
$\ell = \max(0, -y(\langle\vec{w},\vec{x}\rangle + b))$ with $\eta = 1$, taking
the subgradient $\alpha = 1$ at the kink:

$$
\nabla_{\vec{w}}\,\ell =
\begin{cases}
\vec{0} & m > 0\\
-y\vec{x} & m \le 0
\end{cases}
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
\vec{w} \leftarrow \vec{w} + y\vec{x} \ \text{ on a mistake.}
$$
</Answer>

<CheckSanity>
**The loss is zero exactly when the rule is idle.** $\ell = 0 \iff m \ge 0 \iff$
no update. The two descriptions of "nothing to do here" agree, which they must
if one is to be a restatement of the other.

**The loss is convex.** It is the maximum of two affine functions of the
parameters, and a pointwise maximum of convex functions is convex
(<ApparatusRef ref="0.OP.01" />). So the perceptron *is* minimising a convex
objective — and yet it still fails to terminate on XOR, because the minimum of
that objective is not zero there. Convexity buys a well-posed problem, not a
useful answer.

**The learning rate is genuinely irrelevant here.** Any $\eta > 0$ gives
$\vec{w} \leftarrow \vec{w} + \eta y\vec{x}$, which rescales $\vec{w}$ but not
the sign of any score when starting from $\vec{0}$ — so the sequence of
predictions is identical. This is unique to the perceptron and stops being true
the moment Chapter I.4 introduces a loss with curvature.
</CheckSanity>

<Breaks>
The identification needs $\eta = 1$ and $\vec{w}_0 = \vec{0}$ for the *sequence*
to match; only the *form* of the step matches for general $\eta$. It also needs
the loss to be this one. The very similar hinge loss $\max(0, 1 - m)$ — which
demands a margin of at least $1$ rather than merely a correct sign — gives the
same-shaped update but a different idle region, and converges to a
maximum-margin solution the perceptron never finds. One symbol's difference in
the loss changes what the algorithm is for.
</Breaks>
