<Statement>
Derive squared error as the negative log-likelihood of a Gaussian observation
model with fixed variance, and cross-entropy as the negative log-likelihood of a
categorical one. Track every constant that appears, and state exactly which ones
may be discarded and why.
</Statement>

<Given>
**Gaussian model.** Observations are generated as
$y_i = f(x_i;\theta) + \varepsilon_i$ with
$\varepsilon_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)$ independent, $\sigma^2$ fixed and
not learned. The density is

$$
p(y \mid x, \theta) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}}\exp\!\left(-\frac{(y - f(x;\theta))^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right)
$$

**Categorical model.** A prediction is a distribution
$\vec{q} = (q_1, \dots, q_K)$ on the simplex, the observation is a class
$c \in \{1,\dots,K\}$, and

$$p(c \mid x, \theta) = \prod_{k=1}^{K} q_k^{\,y_k}, \qquad y_k = \mathbb{1}[k = c]$$
</Given>

<Find>
$-\log p(\mathcal{D} \mid \theta)$ for each model, reduced to a loss, with every
discarded term named.
</Find>

<Strategy>
Write the likelihood of the whole dataset, take a logarithm to turn the product
into a sum, negate, then separate the terms containing $\theta$ from those that
do not. Only the first group can affect the minimiser, and the second group is
where the constants go.
</Strategy>

<Solution>

### Part 1 — the Gaussian case

**Step 1 — the dataset likelihood.** Independence turns a joint density into a
product:

$$
p(\mathcal{D}\mid\theta) = \prod_{i=1}^{n} p(y_i \mid x_i, \theta)
= \prod_{i=1}^{n} \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}}\exp\!\left(-\frac{r_i^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right)
$$

writing $r_i = y_i - f(x_i;\theta)$ for the $i$-th residual.

**Step 2 — take the logarithm.** A product becomes a sum, and
$\log(ab) = \log a + \log b$ splits each factor:

$$
\log p(\mathcal{D}\mid\theta)
= \sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[\underbrace{\log \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}}}_{\text{no }\theta}
\;+\; \log \exp\!\left(-\frac{r_i^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right)\right]
$$

The exponential and the logarithm are inverses, so the second term simplifies
outright:

$$
= \sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[-\tfrac12\log\!\left(2\pi\sigma^{2}\right) - \frac{r_i^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right]
$$

**Step 3 — separate.** The first term does not depend on $i$, so summing it $n$
times gives a single constant:

$$
\log p(\mathcal{D}\mid\theta)
= -\frac{n}{2}\log\!\left(2\pi\sigma^{2}\right) \;-\; \frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n} r_i^{2}
$$

**Step 4 — negate.**

$$
-\log p(\mathcal{D}\mid\theta)
= \underbrace{\frac{n}{2}\log\!\left(2\pi\sigma^{2}\right)}_{A}
\;+\; \underbrace{\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}}_{B}\sum_{i=1}^{n} r_i^{2}
$$

**Step 5 — account for $A$ and $B$ precisely.**

*Term $A$ is additive and $\theta$-free.* Adding a constant to a function shifts
its graph vertically and moves no stationary point:
$\nabla_\theta (g(\theta) + A) = \nabla_\theta g(\theta)$. So $A$ may be dropped
without changing the minimiser or any gradient.

*Term $B$ is a positive multiplicative constant.* Since $\sigma^2 > 0$ we have
$B > 0$, and $\arg\min_\theta\, Bg(\theta) = \arg\min_\theta g(\theta)$ for any
$B > 0$. So $B$ may be dropped from the *objective*. It may **not** be dropped
from the gradient if the learning rate is fixed, because
$\nabla(Bg) = B\nabla g$ — dropping $B$ rescales every step by $1/B$, which is a
change of effective learning rate and nothing more.

**Step 6 — conclude.**

$$
\arg\min_{\theta} \left[-\log p(\mathcal{D}\mid\theta)\right]
= \arg\min_{\theta} \sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(y_i - f(x_i;\theta)\big)^{2}
$$

$\blacksquare$ Minimising squared error **is** maximum likelihood under a
fixed-variance Gaussian.

### Part 2 — the categorical case

**Step 7 — one example.** The indicator exponent means all but one factor is
raised to the power zero:

$$p(c\mid x,\theta) = \prod_{k=1}^{K} q_k^{\,y_k} = q_c$$

**Step 8 — take the logarithm and negate.**

$$
-\log p(c\mid x,\theta)
= -\log \prod_{k} q_k^{\,y_k}
= -\sum_{k=1}^{K} y_k \log q_k
$$

using $\log(a^b) = b\log a$ on each factor. The right-hand side is exactly the
cross-entropy $H(\vec{y}, \vec{q})$ of Definition 8.

**Step 9 — the dataset.** By independence again,

$$-\log p(\mathcal{D}\mid\theta) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} H(\vec{y}_i, \vec{q}_i)$$

$\blacksquare$

**Note the asymmetry with Part 1: there is no constant to discard.** The
categorical density has no normalising factor outside the probabilities
themselves, because $\sum_k q_k = 1$ is built into the parameterisation. Every
term of the cross-entropy depends on $\theta$.

### What the correspondence costs

Reading Steps 1–6 backwards is the uncomfortable direction. If minimising MSE is
maximum likelihood under a Gaussian, then **choosing MSE asserts a Gaussian**,
whether or not anyone intended to assert anything. Three properties come with
that assertion:

*Symmetry.* $p(\varepsilon) = p(-\varepsilon)$, so over-prediction and
under-prediction cost the same. False whenever the two have different
consequences.

*Constant variance.* One $\sigma^2$ for every $x$. False whenever the noise
scales with the signal, which is the ordinary situation for counts, prices and
concentrations.

*Unbounded support.* $\varepsilon$ may take any real value, so $y$ may too. False
for anything bounded — a probability, a proportion, a nonnegative count.

Chapter VII.2 will meet count data where all three fail at once, and will need a
negative binomial likelihood instead. The route there is exactly this derivation
run forwards with a different density.

</Solution>

<Answer>
$$
-\log p_{\text{Gauss}}(\mathcal{D}\mid\theta)
= \frac{n}{2}\log(2\pi\sigma^{2}) + \frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\sum_i r_i^{2}
$$

The first term is additive and $\theta$-free; the second's prefactor is a
positive constant. Discarding both leaves $\sum_i r_i^2$, so **MSE is Gaussian
maximum likelihood**.

$$
-\log p_{\text{cat}}(\mathcal{D}\mid\theta) = -\sum_i \sum_k y_{ik}\log q_{ik}
$$

which is cross-entropy exactly, with no constant to discard.
</Answer>

<CheckSanity>
**The Gaussian result reproduces the known optimum.** For a constant model
$f = \mu$, minimising $\sum(y_i - \mu)^2$ gives $\mu = \bar{y}$, the sample mean
— which is the maximum-likelihood estimate of a Gaussian mean. Two routes, one
answer.

**Dropping $B$ is exactly a learning-rate change.** With $\sigma^2 = 1$,
$B = \tfrac12$. Training on $\sum r_i^2$ at rate $\eta$ and on
$\tfrac12\sum r_i^2$ at rate $2\eta$ produce identical parameter sequences. This
is checkable in three lines of code, and it is why the factor of $\tfrac12$ in
front of squared losses is a convention rather than a claim.

**The categorical result reduces to the binary case.** At $K = 2$ with
$q_2 = 1 - q_1$ and $\vec{y}$ one-hot, Step 8 becomes
$-[y_1\log q_1 + (1-y_1)\log(1-q_1)]$, which is binary cross-entropy — the
identity established in I.1.B04, recovered here as a special case rather than
assumed.

**The units are consistent.** A log-likelihood is dimensionless (a log of a
probability), and so is cross-entropy. But $\sum r_i^2$ carries the square of
$y$'s units — the mismatch is absorbed by $1/(2\sigma^2)$, whose units are the
inverse square of $y$'s. Discarding $B$ therefore discards the dimensional
bookkeeping too, which is a small reason MSE values are hard to interpret across
problems.
</CheckSanity>

<Breaks>
Step 5 discards $A = \tfrac{n}{2}\log(2\pi\sigma^2)$ because it is $\theta$-free.
That holds **only while $\sigma^2$ is fixed.** Learn the variance — predict
$\sigma^2(x)$ as a second output head, as heteroscedastic regression does — and
$A$ becomes $\tfrac12\sum_i \log \sigma^2(x_i)$, which depends on $\theta$ and
cannot be dropped.

The resulting loss is
$\sum_i\left[\frac{r_i^2}{2\sigma_i^2} + \tfrac12\log\sigma_i^2\right]$, and its
behaviour is different in kind: the first term rewards predicting a large
variance, the second punishes it, and the balance is what makes the model report
calibrated uncertainty. Dropping $A$ there would let the model claim infinite
variance everywhere and drive the loss to $-\infty$. The constant was never
inert; it was inert *given an assumption*.
</Breaks>
