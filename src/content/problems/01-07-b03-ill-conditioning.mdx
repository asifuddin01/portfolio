<Statement>
Exhibit a quadratic on which *every* learning rate fails — not one badly chosen
rate, but the whole stable range — and identify what is responsible. Then show
that remembering the previous step fixes it, and by how much.
</Statement>

<Given>
$\mat{H} = \mathrm{diag}(100,\ 0.01)$, so $\kappa = 10^4$. Start at
$\vec{\theta}_0 = (1, 1)$; the target is an error norm below $10^{-3}$.
</Given>

<Find>
The stability limit; the step count for every $\eta$ across the stable range;
the count at the optimal $\eta^*$; and the same for heavy-ball momentum at its
own optimum, with the speed-up.
</Find>

<Strategy>
The two directions decouple, as in I.7.B02, so the difficulty can be stated
before anything is run: the steep direction demands $\eta < 0.02$, and at any
such $\eta$ the flat direction's multiplier is $1 - 0.01\eta \ge 0.9998$. One
rate cannot serve both. Sweeping confirms there is no exception rather than
merely no obvious one.
</Strategy>

<Solution>

**Step 1 — the bind, stated in advance.** Stability requires
$\eta < 2/\lambda_{\max} = 0.02$. At the largest permissible rate the flat
direction is multiplied by

$$
1 - \eta\lambda_{\min} > 1 - 0.02(0.01) = 0.9998
$$

so it loses at most two parts in ten thousand per step, however $\eta$ is
chosen. That is the counterexample in one line; the sweep is the demonstration.

**Step 2 — the sweep.**

| $\eta$ | steep multiplier | flat multiplier | steps to $10^{-3}$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| $0.020000$ | $1.000000$ | $0.999800$ | diverges |
| $0.010000$ | $0.000000$ | $0.999900$ | $69\,075$ |
| $0.019800$ | $0.980000$ | $0.999802$ | $34\,885$ |
| $0.019801$ | $0.980100$ | $0.999802$ | $34\,883$ |
| $0.019900$ | $0.990000$ | $0.999801$ | $34\,709$ |
| $0.020010$ | $1.001000$ | $0.999800$ | diverges |
| $0.050000$ | $4.000000$ | $0.999500$ | diverges |

Nothing in the stable range does better than about $34\,700$ steps for a
thousandfold reduction on a two-dimensional problem with an exactly known
Hessian.

**Step 3 — the most instructive row.** At $\eta = 0.01$ the steep multiplier is
*exactly zero*: $1 - 0.01(100) = 0$. That direction is solved perfectly, in one
step, and the run still takes $69\,075$ steps — twice as many as the rates that
solve it imperfectly. Perfect handling of the direction that constrains
stability buys nothing, because the cost is entirely in the other direction.
This is why "tune the learning rate" is not a complete answer to slow training.

**Step 4 — the optimum, and its transient.** $\eta^* = 0.0199980$ gives
$\rho = 0.99980002$ and $36\,272$ steps — slightly *worse* than $\eta = 0.0199$
at $34\,709$. As in I.7.B01, $\eta^*$ minimises the asymptotic rate while the run
is measured over a finite budget. The gap is small here because $\kappa$ is
large enough that the asymptote arrives early.

**Step 5 — momentum.** Heavy ball keeps a running average of gradients, so the
recurrence is second-order and its rate depends on $\sqrt{\kappa}$. At the
classical optimum

$$
\beta = \left(\frac{\sqrt{\kappa} - 1}{\sqrt{\kappa} + 1}\right)^2 = 0.96078816,
\qquad
\eta = \frac{4}{(\sqrt{\lambda_{\max}} + \sqrt{\lambda_{\min}})^2} = 0.03921184
$$

with $\rho = (\sqrt{\kappa} - 1)/(\sqrt{\kappa} + 1) = 0.98019802$ and **708
steps**, against $36\,272$: a speed-up of $51.2\times$.

**Step 6 — why it works.** Momentum does not make the steep direction stable at
a larger rate; note that $\eta = 0.0392$ is *above* the plain-descent threshold
of $0.02$. It works because the steep direction's gradient alternates in sign
when overshooting, so consecutive contributions to the moving average partly
cancel, while the flat direction's gradient keeps the same sign and accumulates.
The average suppresses oscillation and amplifies drift, which is exactly the
asymmetry the problem needs.

</Solution>

<Answer>

Stability requires $\eta < 0.02$, and every rate in that range needs at least
about $34\,700$ steps; $\eta^* = 0.0199980$ takes $36\,272$. The obstruction is
$\kappa = 10^4$ itself, not the choice of rate.

Heavy-ball momentum at $\beta = 0.96078816$, $\eta = 0.03921184$ reaches the same
target in **708 steps**, a $51.2\times$ speed-up.

</Answer>

<CheckSanity>
**The measured momentum rate matches the theory.**
$\rho = 0.98019802 = 99/101 = (\sqrt{\kappa} - 1)/(\sqrt{\kappa} + 1)$ for
$\sqrt{\kappa} = 100$, exactly. And it equals the *plain-descent* rate for a
problem of condition number 100 — momentum turns $\kappa = 10^4$ into an
effective $10^2$, which is what "the square root" means concretely.

**The speed-up is $51\times$, not the $100\times$ the rates suggest.** Both
methods are measured to a fixed target from a fixed start, so both pay a
transient the asymptotic rates ignore, and momentum's is proportionally larger
because it needs several steps before its average carries information. Quoting
$\sqrt{\kappa}$ as the speed-up would be quoting a limit as a measurement.

**Divergence appears immediately above $0.02$.** $\eta = 0.02001$ gives a steep
multiplier of $1.001$ and diverges; $\eta = 0.0199$ gives $0.99$ and converges.
Five parts in ten thousand separate them, which is the sharp edge of I.7.B01 in
a stiffer problem.
</CheckSanity>

<Breaks>
The momentum parameters here were computed from $\lambda_{\max}$ and
$\lambda_{\min}$, which nobody has for a real network. In practice $\beta$ is
set to $0.9$ and left there, which is optimal for $\kappa \approx 361$ and
merely serviceable elsewhere — the tuned value above is $0.96$, and using $0.9$
on this problem gives up much of the advantage. The theory says what is
available; it does not hand over the setting.

Momentum also does not help with the part of the difficulty that is noise.
Averaging gradients reduces variance, but the accelerated rate of I.7.T2 assumes
exact gradients, and with minibatch noise the improvement degrades towards the
noise floor. On a stochastic problem momentum is worth having and the factor is
smaller than $\sqrt{\kappa}$.

Finally, this is a fixed quadratic and a neural network is not. Its curvature
changes as training proceeds, so the tuned $(\eta, \beta)$ for step 1 is wrong
by step 10 000 — which is one honest reading of why schedules exist, and why
adaptive methods that estimate curvature per coordinate as they go, rather than
being told it once, are the ones that ended up in use.
</Breaks>

<Variation>
Set $\beta = 0.9$ — the default nobody tunes — and keep $\eta$ at its value
above. Predict whether the run gets faster or slower before measuring, then find
the $\kappa$ for which $\beta = 0.9$ *is* optimal. That number explains where the
default came from.
</Variation>
