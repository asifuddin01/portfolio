A scalar terminal gradient starts at one and is transported through thirty
identical Jacobian factors. Compute the result for factors $0.7$, $1$ and
$1.3$. Is the first numerically zero? Then consider thirty alternating
factors $2,1/2$.

<Hint>
The constant-factor example is $r^{30}$. Pair the alternating factors
before evaluating the second product.
</Hint>

<SolutionToggle>

The three magnitudes are

$$
\begin{aligned}
0.7^{30}&\approx0.00002253934,\\
1^{30}&=1,\\
1.3^{30}&\approx2619.995644.
\end{aligned}
$$

The first is representable in ordinary float32 and float64 arithmetic.
It may be small relative to other gradient contributions, but “small”
and “underflowed to zero” are different claims.

As the gap tends to infinity, a constant factor with magnitude below one
tends to zero; magnitude above one grows without bound; magnitude one
preserves magnitude. A negative factor can reverse signs.

The alternating product is $(2\cdot1/2)^{15}=1$.
It disproves the blanket statement that every long product of factors
different from one must vanish or explode. In real recurrent cells the
factors vary with the trajectory; this exercise isolates only the
constant-scalar mechanism.

</SolutionToggle>
