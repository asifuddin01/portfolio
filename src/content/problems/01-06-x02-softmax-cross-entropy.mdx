Softmax is not elementwise, so I.6.B02's Step 1 does not apply to it: its
Jacobian is dense. Derive $\partial L/\partial \vec{z}$ for softmax composed
with cross-entropy, show the dense product collapses, and then show what the
same softmax does when paired with squared error instead.

<Hint>
Write out $\partial p_j/\partial z_i = p_j(\delta_{ij} - p_i)$ and
$\partial L/\partial p_j = -y_j/p_j$, then form the sum over $j$. Most of it
cancels.
</Hint>

<SolutionToggle>

**The two pieces.** For $\vec{p} = \mathrm{softmax}(\vec{z})$ and
$L = -\sum_j y_j \log p_j$ with $\vec{y}$ one-hot:

$$
\frac{\partial p_j}{\partial z_i} = p_j(\delta_{ij} - p_i),
\qquad
\frac{\partial L}{\partial p_j} = -\frac{y_j}{p_j}
$$

The Jacobian is $d \times d$ and dense — for a 50 000-token vocabulary that is
$2.5 \times 10^9$ entries, which is why nobody forms it.

**The collapse.** By (I.6.2), summing over consumers:

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial z_i}
= \sum_j \left(-\frac{y_j}{p_j}\right) p_j(\delta_{ij} - p_i)
= -\sum_j y_j(\delta_{ij} - p_i)
= -y_i + p_i \sum_j y_j
$$

and $\sum_j y_j = 1$ for a one-hot target, so

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial \vec{z}} = \vec{p} - \vec{y}
$$

The $p_j$ in the Jacobian cancels the $p_j$ in the denominator of the loss
derivative. That cancellation is the only reason this pairing is cheap.

**Verified.** With logits $(2.0,\ 1.0,\ 0.1)$ and the true class $0$:

| | class 0 | class 1 | class 2 |
|---|---|---|---|
| $\vec{p}$ | $0.65900114$ | $0.24243297$ | $0.09856589$ |
| $\vec{p} - \vec{y}$ | $-0.34099886$ | $+0.24243297$ | $+0.09856589$ |
| via the Jacobian | $-0.34099886$ | $+0.24243297$ | $+0.09856589$ |

Largest disagreement $1.39 \times 10^{-17}$ — machine precision. Two routes,
one of which forms a dense matrix and one of which subtracts two vectors.

**The other pairing.** Replace cross-entropy with squared error and there is no
$1/p_j$ to cancel the $p_j$. The Jacobian survives:

| | class 0 | class 1 | class 2 |
|---|---|---|---|
| $\delta$ | $-0.12176312$ | $+0.09664897$ | $+0.02511415$ |
| ratio to $\vec{p} - \vec{y}$ | $0.35707778$ | $0.39866263$ | $0.25479555$ |

Smaller, and by a different factor per class — not a rescaling, a distortion.

**Why it matters most when the model is wrong.** Take logits $(8, 0, 0)$ with
the true class $2$: the model is confident and wrong, $p = (0.99932952,\
0.00033524,\ 0.00033524)$.

Cross-entropy gives $\delta_2 = -0.99966476$ — a full-strength signal, near the
largest it can be. Squared error routes through the softmax slope on the true
class, which is $3.35125324 \times 10^{-4}$: a gradient three thousand times
smaller, at the exact moment the model most needs correcting.

**The general point.** A loss and its output nonlinearity are chosen together,
not separately. Sigmoid with cross-entropy collapses the same way, for the same
reason, to $\hat{y} - y$ — which is why I.6.B01's linear head with squared error
also gave $\delta^{(2)} = \hat{y} - y$, and why frameworks expose a single fused
`cross_entropy` that takes logits rather than a softmax the caller composes
themselves. The fusion is not an optimisation; it is where the cancellation
lives.
