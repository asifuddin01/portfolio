A single batch produces an infinite gradient in one coordinate. Trace what Adam
does on that step and on every subsequent one, and explain why the loss goes to
NaN several steps *after* the batch responsible. Then say why skipping the bad
step is not sufficient.

<Hint>
Follow the value through (I.7.6), and remember that $m$ and $v$ persist between
steps while the gradient does not.
</Hint>

<SolutionToggle>

**The step itself.** With $g = \infty$ in one coordinate:

$$
m \leftarrow \beta_1 m + (1-\beta_1)\infty = \infty,
\qquad
v \leftarrow \beta_2 v + (1-\beta_2)\infty^2 = \infty
$$

The update is $\hat{m}/(\sqrt{\hat{v}} + \varepsilon) = \infty/\infty = $ NaN,
and that coordinate's parameter becomes NaN.

**Why it spreads.** A NaN parameter makes every activation downstream of it NaN,
so the next forward pass produces a NaN loss and the next backward pass NaN
gradients *everywhere*. On the following step every $m$ and $v$ in the model
becomes NaN, and every parameter follows. One coordinate at step $t$ is the whole
model by step $t + 2$.

**Why the timing misleads.** The observable is the loss, and the loss is a scalar
computed from the forward pass. At step $t$ the gradient is infinite but the
parameters are still finite, so the loss printed for step $t$ looks ordinary. The
NaN appears in the loss at step $t + 1$ or later, and the batch on screen when it
appears is not the batch responsible. People go looking for corrupt data in the
wrong file.

**Why skipping is not sufficient.** The natural fix — detect a non-finite
gradient and skip the update — leaves $m$ and $v$ untouched only if the skip
happens before they are written. If the moments have already been updated, they
are non-finite and persist: the next step divides by a NaN $\sqrt{\hat{v}}$ even
though its gradient is perfectly good. The optimiser state must be repaired or
reset, not merely the step abandoned. This is the sense in which the failure
outlives its cause, and it is what distinguishes this from an ordinary bad batch.

**What actually protects against it.** Gradient clipping bounds the magnitude
before it reaches the moments, which is the cheap general defence. In mixed
precision a loss scaler both provokes and manages the problem: it multiplies the
loss to keep fp16 gradients out of the underflow range, detects the resulting
overflows, skips the step, *and lowers the scale* — the last part being the
piece that makes it a control loop rather than a filter.

**The assumption this tests.** The chapter assumes the gradient is finite along
the path taken. That is not a mathematical nicety: it is the condition under
which the optimiser's state remains a summary of past gradients rather than an
absorbing state that no future gradient can leave.
