I spend most of my time training models on images that doctors look at.

My undergraduate thesis, *HierarchiRetina*, grades diabetic retinopathy severity
from retinal photographs. It doesn't go straight from picture to grade. It
screens first, then runs five separate segmentation models for five different
things — microaneurysms, haemorrhages, hard exudates, cotton wool spots,
vessels — then feeds those masks alongside the raw image into a grading model.
The point is interpretability: when the system says "severe," you can see which
lesions drove that.

Getting the data was its own project. I visited hospitals in person, emailed
researchers and institutions, and went through more than 37 published papers.
Every hospital request was declined. Most researcher emails were too. The FGADR
authors wrote back, which is why FGADR is in the pipeline.

Alongside the thesis I work with Niloy Farhan at BRAC on two problems. One is
open-world species recognition: what does a model do when it meets an organism
nobody trained it on, and where in the taxonomy does that organism belong? The
other is measuring kidney length automatically from thick-slice CT, where slices
sit 8mm apart and the kidney is tilted.

The newest one has pulled me somewhere different. Instead of asking which genes
correlate with Alzheimer's, it asks what would happen to a microglial cell if
you knocked a specific gene down, and then checks that answer against CRISPR
screens where somebody actually did knock it down.

Next is an MSc in Computer Science and Engineering at BRAC University, starting
Fall 2026. The proposal is already accepted. I want to keep doing research while
I build a career in AI and machine learning, not choose between them.

Outside computer science I read law and public policy, which turns out to be
less of a detour than it sounds.
