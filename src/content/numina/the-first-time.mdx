A Theban priest named Nesmin owned a long papyrus roll. Its
colophon dates it to 305 BCE. Most of what it holds is ritual: songs for
Isis and Nephthys, the bringing in of the god Sokar, and a book for
overthrowing Apep, the serpent who threatens the sun.[^nesmin]

In the middle of the spells against Apep, the creator speaks. Before he
came into being, he says, there was no sky and no earth. He raised himself
out of the inert water. And there he met the first difficulty of the
world: he found no place on which he could stand.[^budge]

Every Egyptian account of the beginning has to answer that difficulty.
Where does the first ground come from, and how does one become many?
Egypt answered more than once, in different texts written for different
purposes, as the last chapter led us to expect.

Egyptologists often name the answers after cities: Heliopolis, Memphis,
Hermopolis and Thebes. The names are useful, but they can mislead. The
evidence rarely tells us where or when an idea first took shape, and the
accounts were not rival creeds. As we shall see, a single hymn could hold
all of them at once.[^ucl-myth]

## Before there was anything

Start before the beginning. How do you describe what existed when nothing
yet existed?

One answer names what was missing. A group of eight beings, four male and
female pairs, stands for the state before creation. UCL's overview gives
the pairs as they appear in the earliest surviving reference to them, a
funerary text known as Coffin Text 76:[^ucl-eight]

- **Nun and Nunet**, from a word for a watery expanse: no solid ground.
- **Heh and Hehet**, from a word for unending time: no measure of time.
- **Kek and Keket**, from the word for darkness: no light.
- **Tenem and Tenemet**, from a word for wandering: no direction.

In later versions Amun and Amunet, from the word for *hidden*, take the
place of the last pair. The absence becomes an absence of sight.[^ucl-eight]

Each name is a quality of the known world, turned inside out: water
without a shore, time without a count, dark, and aimlessness. The Eight are
not monsters to be defeated. They are a way of describing nothing without
pretending that nothing is simple.

Egyptologists often call the Eight the Ogdoad and place them at Hermopolis.
The city's Egyptian name, Khemenu, means *Eight*. Its main god was Thoth,
whom the Greeks equated with Hermes, hence the Greek name.[^ucl-eight]
The Louvre's account of this tradition has Thoth coordinating the work of
the four couples, whose efforts produce the sun, appearing as a child inside
a shining lotus.[^louvre]

UCL raises two objections to the usual label, *Hermopolitan theology*. It is
not known where or when the references to the Eight developed. And they are
not one creation myth to be set against others, but an early stage in the
story of creation, which continues with the emergence of the sun god.[^ucl-eight]
Borges Pires makes the same point: the Ogdoad is often misunderstood as
automatically linked to Hermopolis.[^pires-ogdoad]

## Ground out of water

In the Nile valley the beginning had a model that came back every year.
The river flooded in late summer. In the autumn the fields emerged again,
coated in black mud. UCL suggests that this repeated experience supplied
the main images through which Egyptians expressed the mystery of
creation.[^ucl-atum]

Three of those images recur:

- A heron alights on the first dry ground. Its Egyptian name, *bnw*, may be
  the origin of the Greek phoenix.
- A lotus flower rises out of the water. From the New Kingdom onward this
  image is assigned to Nefertem, god of scented oils.
- The mound itself offers the first sacred ground. Temples are said to stand
  on the primeval mound of the first time of creation.[^ucl-atum]

That phrase, *the first time*, is *sp tpy* in Egyptian. It names the moment
when creation was set in motion. It is less ancient than it sounds.
Borges Pires, following Susanne Bickel, notes that the expression seems to
be absent from both the Pyramid Texts and the Coffin Texts.[^pires-sptpy]
It belongs to later religious writing, and we shall see what that writing
did with it.

## Atum, the complete one

The principal creator in Egyptian religion is the sun god. His name Ra means
*sun*, but he is also regularly called Atum, from a word meaning *complete*.
UCL reads the name as all matter concentrated in the creator before
creation emerged. Creation, on this view, is not making something from
nothing. It is the undivided All gradually separating into distinct
things.[^ucl-atum]

The Louvre summarises the account associated with Heliopolis, Atum's city.
Atum becomes aware of his existence within Nun, acquires movement, and
wishes to rest. A primeval mound emerges; he climbs onto it and organises
the universe from there. The Louvre notes that these texts survive in
funerary anthologies such as the Coffin Texts and the Book of the
Dead.[^louvre]

Then the creator has to become more than one. The earliest surviving texts
are frank about how. Pyramid Text 527, in James Allen's translation as
quoted by Borges Pires, says that Atum came into being in Heliopolis and
produced the twins Shu and Tefnut from his own body, by his own hand, with
no partner.[^pt527]

The twins are a pair of opposites. Shu's name is related to a word for
being dry; Tefnut's to a rare word that UCL connects with moisture. The
Coffin Texts also equate Shu with *life* and Tefnut with *Ma'at*, what is
right, the subject of a later chapter.[^ucl-atum]

From the twins come Geb, the earth, and Nut, the sky. Shu separates them,
and from Geb and Nut come Osiris, Seth, Isis and Nephthys.[^ucl-ennead]
Egyptian texts often refer to groups of gods as a Nine; Egyptologists use
the Greek form, Ennead. UCL notes that the rituals mentioning the Nine do
not list them by name, so any list of nine is a reconstruction.[^ucl-ennead]

Human beings receive almost no separate attention. UCL knows of no Egyptian
composition describing the creation of humankind. People are already there
early in the story, and in some accounts they come from the sun god's tears:
the Egyptian word for people sounds like the word for weeping.[^ucl-ennead]

The scarab on Iufaa's pyramidion carries the idea in a single sign. The
beetle was *kheperer* in Egyptian. The rising sun was *Kheperi*, and the
verb for *to become* or *come into being* was *kheper*. The same beetle
hieroglyph spelled all of these words.[^iufaa][^wah] The god of the morning
sun is, by his name, the god who comes into being.

## One papyrus, two beginnings

Return to Nesmin's papyrus. The creator's account appears in it twice.
A shorter version stands among the spells against Apep, and a longer one
follows a little further on. Budge, who first published the papyrus, could
not tell whether the scribe had two copies and inserted both, or copied the
short version and added to it as he went.[^budge-two]

The speaker comes into being in the form of Khepera, Budge's spelling of
Khepri. He works by his heart, which Budge also translates as *will*, and
lays the foundations of things by Ma'at. In the longer version he gives
himself being by speaking his own name.[^budge]

The two versions are not tidy about how the twins began. In one passage the
creator speaks of the time before he had emitted Shu and spat out Tefnut;
in another he unites with his own
hand and sends them forth from himself, and *from being one god I became
three*. Both are kept, and so is a third detail: men and women spring from
the tears of his Eye.[^budge]

Budge's translation is more than a century old, and modern translations
differ in their wording. Read it as a guide to the structure of the
account, not as the exact phrasing of the Egyptian.[^budge]

The setting matters as much as the words. Budge describes the book of
overthrowing Apep as spells recited so that nothing, animate or inanimate,
could prevent the sun from rising in the morning. The creation account has
no mention of Apep. Budge's explanation was that the whole account
was itself regarded as a powerful spell.[^budge-two] Whatever its original
purpose, in Nesmin's roll the story of how the world began sits inside the
work of making the next day begin.

## Ptah: the heart and the tongue

The account associated with Memphis credits a different god. It survives on
the Shabaka Stone, a stone slab in the British Museum, and the museum calls
it the only surviving copy of what Egyptologists know as the Memphite
Theology.[^shabaka]

The stone dates from the reign of Shabaka, a Kushite king of the
twenty-fifth dynasty, around 710 BCE. It was set up in the temple of Ptah
at Memphis and later reused as a millstone, which ground away much of its
text. As the previous chapter noted, the inscription claims to copy an
ancient, worm-eaten document. The museum's curators treat that claim as a
rhetorical device, and it is now generally accepted that the text in its
present form was composed in Shabaka's own time.[^shabaka]

Whether the *ideas* are older is still disputed. The museum's description
suggests a text perhaps first composed in the New Kingdom; its curators'
notes record scholarly dates for the content from the nineteenth dynasty to
the twenty-fifth.[^shabaka]

According to the curators, the Memphite account sought to give Ptah, the
patron god of the city, a prior claim over Atum of Heliopolis. It does so by
crediting Ptah with creating the other gods, and through them, indirectly,
the world. Atum is not erased. He is placed after Ptah.[^shabaka]

The museum's transcript of the text describes how. Ptah gave life to all
the gods *through the heart and through the tongue*. The senses report to
the heart, which brings understanding forth; the tongue repeats what the
heart has devised. Every divine word, every hieroglyph, every trade and art
came from that command. Then, the text says, Ptah was satisfied.[^shabaka-text]

UCL describes Ptah as creation by transformation: the shaping of existing
material, whether metal and stone in craft or speech in words.[^ucl-other]
Ptah also appears as a primordial creator in his own right. A hymn in the
Great Harris Papyrus, from the reign of Ramesses III, greets Ptah as
Tatenen, father of the gods, *the great god of the First Time*, who made the
sky as a creation of his heart.[^ptah-harris]

## Amun, who keeps them all

Amun's name means *the hidden one*. The Met's record notes that he came
into prominence at the beginning of the Middle Kingdom, and from the New
Kingdom onward was arguably the most important god in Egypt. As a creator
he is most often Amun-Re, joined with the sun god. His main sanctuary was
the temple complex at Karnak, at Thebes.[^amun]

Papyrus Leiden I 350 holds a collection of hymns to him, probably composed
in the second half of the reign of Ramesses II. Its sections are numbered
like chapters, and each number puns on the words that open or close its
section.[^leiden] Read in Borges Pires's translations, they show what Thebes
did with every other account in this chapter.

Chapter 100 calls the god the one who inaugurated existence at the First
Time.[^leiden] Chapter 80 makes the Eight his first manifestation: he hid
himself as Amun at the head of the gods, then came into being as Tatenen,
whom these hymns identify with Ptah, to bring the primordial gods to birth. Another
passage calls the Eight *another of his existences*, has those primordial
gods bring Ra to birth, and says he completed himself with Atum.
A damaged line appears to mention Shu and Tefnut spat out.[^leiden-80]

Then chapter 300 sums it up. All gods are three, Amun, Ra and Ptah, with no
equal; he hides his name as Amun, *he is Ra in the face, and his body is
Ptah*. Borges Pires reads this as one god with many aspects, not three
separate gods.[^leiden]

The Eight of Hermopolis, Atum of Heliopolis, Ptah of Memphis: none of them
is refuted. Each is taken up as a form of the one hidden god. The Theban
hymn does not choose between the earlier accounts. It keeps all of them.

## Every morning, the first time

There is no single Egyptian narrative that combines all these motifs. UCL
warns that our summaries of them may be a modern, non-Egyptian way of
answering the question of creation. The Egyptian response seems to have
been less to tell stories than to express relations between divine forces
in groups, which Jan Assmann has called *constellations*.[^ucl-myth]

Nor was the first time only in the past. In New Kingdom sun hymns, Borges Pires
notes, scholars disagree over whether the creation is described as a single
event or a continuing one. *Sp tpy* may occasionally designate the
repetition of creation in the daily appearance of the sun.[^pires-daily]

Look again at the face of Iufaa's pyramidion. The sun is lifted into its
boat on the legs of the beetle whose name means *coming into being*. Look
again at Nesmin's roll, where the account of the world's beginning sits
among spells to make sure the sun rises. On these monuments, the beginning
was not a lost origin to be recovered. It was something that had to keep
happening.

Creation divides the All into earth and sky and the first family of gods.
The next chapter follows that family into its first crime: Osiris, murdered
by his brother Set.
