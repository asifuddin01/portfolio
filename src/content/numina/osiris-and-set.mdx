A limestone stela a little over a metre tall stands in the Louvre. It was
made in the eighteenth dynasty, between 1550 and 1295 BCE, for a man named
Imenmes, chief of the flocks of Amun. In its curved top, offerings are
presented to him, his wife and one of his sons, and six more of the
couple's children kneel beneath. Below the family run twenty-eight lines
of text, and they begin with a hymn to Osiris.[^imenmes]

The hymn praises Osiris in city after city. It calls him the eldest of his
brothers and the heir of the earth god. Then, without a word of warning,
his sister Isis is searching for him. She flies over the land crying out
and does not come down until she has found him. She makes an heir from him
and nurses the child in a place nobody knows. The child grows strong, and
the gods give him his father's office.[^hymn]

Something is missing. The hymn never says how Osiris died. In Budge's
translation the killer is not named once.[^hymn]

The Louvre calls this stela rare testimony, and explains why. Allusions to
Osiris are countless, but few sources from the time of the pharaohs
recount his whole legend. That was left to Greek authors, much
later.[^imenmes]

So this chapter cannot simply tell the story. It has to show where each
part of it comes from, and to notice which parts the Egyptian texts
preferred not to write down.

## A god with a biography

Begin with the outline, as Mark Smith assembles it from Egyptian sources.
Osiris is the eldest child of Geb, the earth, and Nut, the sky. He marries
his sister Isis and succeeds his father as king of Egypt. His brother
murders him. Isis searches for the body and finds it, and it is made whole
again by mummification. With her spells she rouses him enough to conceive
a son, Horus. She raises the child in secret in the marshes of the Delta,
out of his uncle's reach. When Horus is grown he takes his father's case
to the gods. The killer is judged, Horus is acclaimed king, and Osiris
becomes ruler of the underworld and of everyone in it.[^smith-bio]

The brother's name is written Set or Seth in English. This book's chapter
list uses Set; most of the scholarship cited below uses Seth. Greek
writers called him Typhon.[^names]

Smith is careful about what this outline is. He calls it a composite, put
together from sources of different dates and from different parts of the
country. No Egyptian wrote it out in this form.[^smith-bio]

Even so, the outline is unusual. Smith observes that Osiris is one of the
few Egyptian gods of whom even the sketch of a biography can be written.
Other gods mattered for what they were: the sun, the sky, the flood.
Osiris mattered for what had happened to him.[^smith-bio]

The details vary even at this level. Smith gives one widespread tradition
in which Osiris was born at Thebes on the first of the five extra days at
the end of the year, and he notes that other accounts give a different
day, place and parentage.[^smith-bio] UCL's overview has the sky and earth
win those five days from Thoth, the god who reckons time, and the four
children are born on them.[^ucl-children]

Nor is it known when the story took shape. UCL points out that in the
earliest dynasties Horus and Set are already prominent as a pair of
opposites, but without Isis, Ra or Osiris. The family story we are about
to follow may be an amalgam of episodes that formed during the third
millennium BCE.[^ucl-when]

The Met's record notes that these three features, the wrapped body, the
sceptres and the white crown, appear on innumerable statuettes of the
first millennium BCE.[^osiris-met] The image hardly changes. The texts
are a different matter.

## Struck down in Nedit

The oldest evidence is in the Pyramid Texts, cut into royal tombs from the
twenty-fourth century BCE, as the first chapter described. They do not
tell the story. They use it.

One utterance, numbered 532 by modern editors, has Isis and Nephthys
arrive as two birds, one of them a kite. They find Osiris *after his
brother Set had felled him to the earth* in a place called Nedit. They
keep his body from decay.[^pt532]

That is nearly all the murder we are given: one clause, in the past tense,
inside a sentence about finding him. The spell does not say what weapon
was used, or why.

Another utterance, 477, remembers a trial. The gods confront Set in the
house of the prince at Heliopolis, because he struck Osiris to the ground.
Set first denies it. Then he says that Osiris defied him, and that Osiris
came too near to him. The spell turns those two excuses into the origins
of divine names, and moves on to raise Osiris to his feet between Isis
and Nephthys.[^pt477]

So the earliest surviving source already knows a version in which the
killer pleads his own case. Smith notes that in some sources Set justified
the act as self-defence.[^smith-defence]

A third pair of utterances, 366 and 367, has the two sisters heal Osiris.
Isis comes to him, and Horus is conceived, *the son who avenges his
father*. Then Horus is said to have collected his father's limbs and put
him together.[^pt366] Here the mending is the son's work, not the wife's.
The Pyramid Texts do not settle who did what.

Two cautions belong here. The translation used is Samuel Mercer's of
1952, which is in the public domain but has been superseded; it is a
guide to what each spell contains, not to exact wording.[^pt532] And these
are spells for a dead king. Where an editor prints *Osiris N.*, the tomb
had the king's own name. The murder is mentioned because the king, like
Osiris, has fallen and must be raised.

That may be why the death is so brief. A spell meant to raise a body has
little reason to dwell on how it fell. This is an inference from what the
texts were for, not something an Egyptian source says.

The place name lasted. About two thousand years after Unas, a healing
stela that we shall meet below still appeals to the sun god to remember
*the cry which came from the bank of Netit*, Budge's spelling of
Nedit.[^budge-stela]

## The search

Return to the hymn on the stela of Imenmes. What the Pyramid Texts give in
a clause, the hymn gives at length, and it is all about Isis.

She protects her brother and drives his enemies away. She is skilled of
tongue, and her spells do not fail. Then she goes *seeking for him
untiringly*. She circles the earth as a bird, wailing, and does not alight
until she finds him. She makes light with her feathers and air with her
wings. She raises the helpless limbs of the god *whose heart was at rest*,
takes his essence, and makes an heir.[^hymn]

Look at the phrase for his condition. His heart is at rest; his limbs are
helpless. In this translation the hymn describes a dead god without
using the word.

Laments in the voices of the two sisters were copied on papyrus as well.
The roll of Nesmin, where the last chapter began, opens with seventeen
columns of songs of Isis and Nephthys before it reaches the spells
against Apep.[^nesmin]

Notice what the search does not achieve. Osiris is not brought back to
the throne. In UCL's summary, Isis revives the body enough to conceive a
child, and then the transfigured Osiris withdraws to the underworld to
rule the dead.[^ucl-children] Smith puts the same point another way. A
relationship that began before either of them was born continued after
one of them had died.[^smith-bio]

The dead king stays dead. What is rescued is the succession.

## The child in the marshes

The hymn says that Isis suckled her child in solitude, *and none knew
where his place was*.[^hymn] Other sources name the place: the marshes of
Khemmis in the Delta.[^smith-bio]

The Met's record explains the scene. Isis is hiding the baby from his
uncle, who wants to kill him because he is the heir of Osiris. Images of
this kind became newly prominent in the early first millennium BCE, when
the myths of the Delta, especially those about goddesses and their sons,
were being explored and elaborated.[^isis-met]

The marsh was not safe. The fullest account of what happened there is on
another object, and it was written for a practical purpose.

According to the Met, the stela was commissioned by a priest named Esatum
and set up in the public part of a temple. Its lower half carries thirteen
spells against venomous bites and wounds. A victim could recite them, or drink water
that had been poured over the words and images.[^stela-met]

On the stela Isis speaks. While she and Horus were hiding in the marshes,
she says, the child fell ill. In despair she cried out to the boat of the
sun, *and the sun disk stopped opposite her*. Thoth came down from the
boat and cured the child with a list of spells, each ending with the same
phrase: *and the protection of the afflicted as well*.[^stela-met]

That closing phrase is the reason the story is there. The museum calls
the cure of Horus a mythic precedent. What Thoth did for the child, the
words can do for a patient.[^stela-met]

Budge published a translation of the stela in 1912, and his account
differs from the museum's on several points. He dated it to an earlier
king and named a different owner. He says the child was stung by a
scorpion that Set had sent, though he introduces this with *it seems*. He
also records that the stone was dug up in Alexandria in 1828. This chapter
follows the museum on the date and the owner.[^budge-stela]

Once again the story survives because it was useful. The stela tells how
Horus nearly died because people needed to be cured of scorpion stings.

## A lawsuit among the gods

Horus grows up, and the quarrel goes to court. The longest Egyptian
account of it is on a papyrus from Thebes, written under the Ramesside
kings and now in the Chester Beatty collection in Dublin. Scholars call
the composition *The Contendings of Horus and Seth*.[^ucl-children]

Marcelo Campagno summarises its plot. Horus and Set appear before the
Nine, the company of gods, presided over by the sun god. The court has to
decide whether the kingship of the murdered Osiris belongs to his young
son or to his brother, whose chief quality is his strength.[^campagno]

The murder itself happens before the tale begins. Campagno notes that the
first conflict, between Osiris and Set, is only implicit in the
text.[^campagno-myth] A New Kingdom storyteller, like the hymn and the
Pyramid Texts before him, starts after the death.

The case will not end. The court leans towards Horus, and Set rejects
each attempt to crown him. The hearings are broken up by contests of
strength and by tricks, in which Isis fights hard for her son. A ferryman
is beaten for carrying her to the Island in the Midst against the court's
direct order. After a contest of hippopotami, Horus turns on his own
mother and cuts off her head, and the court fails even to punish
him.[^campagno][^campagno-court]

In the end Osiris himself intervenes. From the underworld he demands that
his son succeed him. After an exchange of letters with the sun god, the
Nine recognise Horus. Set is taken prisoner, and Horus is made king of
Egypt.[^campagno] UCL adds that in some versions Set is given the
deserts as his own domain.[^ucl-children]

What kind of text is this? Scholars disagree. Campagno quotes one view
that it fits every test for a myth, and another that it is not myth in
literary form but a work of fiction. His own reading is that it is a
literary version of the myth, with non-canonical material and plays on
words added.[^campagno-myth]

He also makes a point about the court. The Nine cannot enforce a verdict.
They negotiate, give way and reopen the case, as a village council does
when neither party will compromise. Only in the last scene do they act
like a state, with a prisoner and a sentence. The papyrus belonged to a
family of scribes at Deir el-Medina, the workmen's village at Thebes, and
its composition has been linked to the accession of Ramesses V.[^campagno-court]

One wound from this quarrel became one of Egypt's most popular amulets.
In Egyptian mythology, the Met's record says, the eye of Horus was injured or
stolen by Set and restored by Thoth. The healed eye is the *wedjat*, the
one that is sound.[^wedjat] The Pyramid Texts already have Horus give his
eye to his father.[^pt366]

## Set is not the devil

It would be easy to read Set as simply evil. The Egyptian evidence does
not allow it.

UCL describes him as the god of disruptive power. He had a temple of his
own at Naqada in Upper Egypt. In one province he and Horus were worshipped
side by side, as if, UCL suggests, Horus was needed there to balance and
contain him.[^ucl-seth]

Kings claimed him. The oldest royal title shows the falcon of Horus
perched on a palace front, with the king's name inside. A ruler of the
second dynasty, Peribsen, put the animal of Set there instead.
Khasekhemwy, who reigned shortly after, put both.[^ucl-horusname]

The staff in the plate shows how thoroughly he was woven into ordinary
religion. The Met's record sets out the debate. Some scholars link the
*was* to the animal of Set and point to a gigantic example found in his
temple at Ombos. Others think the god's image derived from the staff. Yet
others give it a different origin altogether. What is not in doubt is its
meaning: the hieroglyph of this staff wrote the word *dominion*, and other
gods are shown carrying it.[^was]

There were even versions of the story in which the fault lay with Osiris.
Smith records the claim of self-defence, and another tradition in which
Set took revenge because Osiris had an affair with his wife, Nephthys.
The child of that affair was Anubis, who is sometimes called the eldest
son of Osiris.[^smith-defence]

None of this makes the killing less of a crime in the texts quoted
above. The Pyramid Texts meet Set's excuses with a threat from the gods.
But the murderer remains a god, with a temple, a province, a place in a
king's titles, and in some accounts a case of his own.

## Plutarch tells it straight through

The fullest continuous account that survives was written by a Greek.
Plutarch addressed his essay *Isis and Osiris* to a woman named Clea,
well over a thousand years after the stela of Imenmes was
carved.[^plutarch]

His version begins with the birth. The Sun curses the mother of the gods,
whom Plutarch calls Rhea, so that she cannot give birth in any month or
year. Hermes, the Greek name for Thoth, plays
draughts with the Moon and wins enough light to make five extra days.
Osiris is born on the first, and a voice announces *the Lord of
All*.[^plutarch]

Then the plot. Typhon gathers seventy-two conspirators. He secretly
measures his brother and has a beautiful chest made to fit. At a feast he
promises it to whoever lies in it exactly. Osiris lies down. The
conspirators slam the lid, seal it with lead and send it down the river to
the sea. Plutarch gives the date as the seventeenth of the month Athyr, in
the twenty-eighth year of the reign of Osiris, or, some said, of his
life.[^plutarch]

The chest drifts across the sea to Byblos, where a heather bush grows
around it into a tree. The local king cuts the tree
for a pillar. Isis traces it, becomes nurse to the queen's child, reveals
herself and takes the chest home. Typhon, hunting by moonlight, finds it.
He cuts the body into fourteen pieces and scatters them. Isis sails the
marshes in a papyrus boat and recovers all but one. The male member has
been eaten by three kinds of fish. She makes a replacement.[^plutarch]

Osiris then returns from the other world to train Horus for battle. Horus
wins. Isis lets the bound Typhon go, and Horus in fury tears the crown
from her head. Typhon challenges the legitimacy of Horus before the gods
and loses.[^plutarch]

Some of this matches the Egyptian sources closely. Smith cites Egyptian
evidence that Osiris died at twenty-eight, that he and Isis were lovers in
the womb, and that he fathered Anubis on Nephthys. All three details are
in Plutarch.[^smith-bio][^smith-defence] The quarrel between Horus and his
mother recalls the beheading in the Chester Beatty papyrus.[^campagno-court]

Other parts have no counterpart in the Egyptian sources used in this
chapter. None of them mentions Byblos, the chest or the seventy-two.

Greek writers did not agree with each other either. Diodorus of Sicily,
writing earlier than Plutarch, says Typhon cut the body into twenty-six
pieces and gave one to each accomplice. A note in the Loeb edition records
a manuscript that reads sixteen. In Diodorus, Isis moulds a figure of wax
and spices around each piece and tells each group of priests, under oath,
that they alone have the real burial. That, he says, is why every district
believes Osiris lies in its own soil.[^diodorus]

The rival tombs were real. At Abydos, by the Middle Kingdom, one of the
royal tombs of the first dynasty was regarded as the burial place of
Osiris, and a procession went out to it from the temple each
year.[^abydos] Diodorus reports that the strongest oath in southern Egypt
was sworn by Osiris *who lieth in Philae*, far up the river.[^diodorus]
One of the fish was honoured too: the Met holds a finely detailed metal
figure of the lepidotus, a fish sacred to the Egyptians and linked to the
story of the dismemberment.[^lepidotus]

Plutarch himself warned Clea how to take his narrative. She must not
think, he writes, *that any of these tales actually happened* in the way
they are told.[^plutarch] The continuous story that many modern
retellings follow is his, and it was written by an outsider with his own
purpose.

## What the story was for

Why did this story matter more than any other? Smith's answer starts
from how the Egyptians thought about a person.

A person, on this account, had a bodily self and a social self. Both
depended on being connected: limbs to a body, an individual to a
community. Death broke both connections at once. It was a kind of
dismemberment, of the body and of the person's place among
others.[^smith-two]

Osiris was the model for reversing both. Mummification restored his body.
The verdict against Set restored his standing among the gods. Smith,
following Jan Assmann, calls the second *moral mummification*, and adds
that the first could equally be called bodily justification. His death was
an injustice; to obtain justice was to regain life.[^smith-two]

This is why the dead were given his name. Funerary texts speak of *the
Osiris of* a person. Smith argues that this did not mean the dead person
became the god. It meant joining his following, as one of his worshippers
in the underworld. Women acquired an Osiris as readily as men.[^smith-two]

The story also explained the king. With the kingship of Horus, UCL writes,
creation reached its present stage: a Horus king on earth, the sun
ruling the sky, and Osiris ruling the dead below.[^ucl-children] The
oldest royal title, as we saw, names the king as Horus.[^ucl-horusname]

And it was tied to growth. Late in Egyptian history, figures of Osiris
were made and filled with grain. The Met's record notes that the grain
could sprout, and so the figures symbolised new life.[^corn]

Look again at the stela of Imenmes. A herdsman's family kneels under a
hymn about another family. The hymn passes over the killing in silence
and spends its words on the search, the child and the verdict. That is
the balance of the Egyptian sources as a whole. The crime is rarely
described, but nearly every source is concerned with what was done to
repair it.

The hymn ends when the gods give the sovereignty to the one *whose it is
by right*.[^hymn] The next chapter asks what the Egyptians meant by
right. Their word for it was Ma'at.
