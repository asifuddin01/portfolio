A small round tablet carries the name of a god six times. The name is Urash.
There is no battle here, no creation of the world, no journey into the land
of the dead. Someone was learning to write.

The tablet is about the width of a palm. It belongs to the Old Babylonian
period, roughly the twentieth to sixteenth centuries BCE. Its surface
preserves an ordinary act of practice: the same signs, tried again. We do
not know the pupil's name.[^exercise]

This is a good place to begin a book of Mesopotamian myths. Before a god
can speak to us across four thousand years, somebody has to learn the signs
that carry the god's name. Then somebody has to copy the story. The clay has
to survive. A reader has to find it, recognise the language, and work out
where a broken line belongs.

Between the ancient voice and our own reading, there are many hands.

## A page made from earth

Clay made writing a physical craft. A scribe shaped a tablet and pressed
signs into its surface while it was still workable. A cut reed could leave
a short wedge, broad at one end and narrow at the other. Groups of these
marks formed signs. The modern word *cuneiform* means wedge-shaped.[^origins]

The material mattered after the writing was finished, too. Many tablets
were dried rather than deliberately baked. Some were fired in antiquity;
others were heated in the destruction of a building. Museums also fired
tablets as a conservation treatment in more recent times. A hard tablet
does not, by itself, tell us which of these things happened.[^clay]

It is tempting to say that fire saved the books. That is too easy. A tablet
can outlast a palace and still lose half its words. Even when the clay
survives, its history may be hard to recover. Recent work on tablet
minerals helps researchers investigate how an object was made and whether
it was heated. The writing and the material can answer different
questions.[^clay]

A missing corner is a loss with an exact shape. It may have held a name,
a verb, or the reason a god changed their mind. On the printed page, the
loss becomes a few dots. On the object, it is a piece of the world that
has gone.

## Before the stories, the accounts

Some of the earliest surviving writing from southern Mesopotamia comes
from the late fourth millennium BCE. Uruk, in what is now southern Iraq,
was a major centre. Much of this early material records goods and amounts.
People were keeping track of grain, animals, labour, and deliveries. The
earliest tablets give us offices at work before they give us long
conversations between gods.[^origins]

Look at the tablet above. It is an account, not a page of myth. The museum
identifies a record of barley distribution alongside impressions made by
a cylinder seal. These early signs are called *proto-cuneiform*. Some are
pictures or drawn shapes; others record numbers.[^account]

Over time, signs could also stand for sounds. This opened ways to write
names, grammar, and things that could not be shown by a simple picture.
By the middle of the third millennium BCE, writing served many kinds of
work, including literature.[^origins]

None of this dates the first spoken story. An early written account tells
us that someone kept an account. It does not tell us what that person heard
at home, sang at a festival, or believed about the river. The age of writing
sets one limit on our evidence. It does not set a starting date for the
human imagination.

## One script, different languages

Cuneiform is a way of writing. Sumerian and Akkadian are languages. Keeping
that distinction clear prevents a great deal of confusion.

Sumerian has no established relationship to another known language.
Akkadian belongs to the Semitic language family. The script developed for
Sumerian was adapted to write Akkadian, and later scribes could study both.
Long after Sumerian ceased to be a spoken mother tongue, it remained a
language of learned writing.[^languages]

Think of a modern alphabet used for several languages. Recognising the
letters does not mean that you can read every sentence written with them.
The comparison has limits, but it makes the first distinction clear.

There is a further difficulty. A cuneiform sign can have more than one
reading. It may stand for a word in one place and a sound in another. The
reader needs the language, the neighbouring signs, and knowledge of how
scribes used them. Ancient word lists were part of that training. They
were also part of the knowledge passed between generations.[^lexical]

For us, a change of language is a reason to pay attention. A Sumerian poem
and an Akkadian work may share a god or an episode without being the same
composition. We should read each text before joining them into a single
story.

## The people who kept copying

The round tablet from the opening brings us back to the scale of a hand.
On it, a sign marking a divine name comes before the sign read here as
Urash. The pupil was learning a writing system in which the same sign
could do different jobs.[^exercise]

The Sumerian school is often called the *eduba*, or tablet house. The
name can make us picture a grand public building. Excavation gives a more
intimate example. At Nippur, a small house known to archaeologists as House F
held a large body of school material. Eleanor Robson's study connects the
rooms and their tablets to the work of teaching. Some old exercises ended
up in building material. Clay could also be soaked and used again.[^school]

Learning involved repetition. On one common kind of school tablet, a
teacher's model and a student's exercise share a surface. The other side
can carry earlier material recalled for practice. Such objects let
researchers study the order in which exercises were learned, rather than
guessing a curriculum from the hardest texts alone.[^lexical]

Literature belonged to this work. Many surviving manuscripts come from
scribal education. Andrew George has stressed how strongly this shapes
the evidence for Babylonian literary texts. A poem could have other uses,
yet survive for us because students copied it.[^literature]

That changes the way we look at a familiar myth. Its survival does not
give us a poll of ancient belief. It gives us, among other things, a record
of what teachers chose and pupils practised. A widely copied poem may have
been useful for learning. A story that nobody set as an exercise may have
left much less behind.

The school preserved a great deal. It also helped decide what we would
ever get the chance to read.

## Three dates for one story

The phrase "a four-thousand-year-old story" can hide several different
claims. We need to separate them.

The first date belongs to the physical object: when this copy was made.
The second concerns the composition: when a work took the form we are
trying to study. The third belongs to the events inside the story. A poem
about the beginning of the world was still written at a particular time
by people living long after that imagined beginning.

*Gilgamesh* makes the problem plain. There are separate Sumerian poems
about the hero, then Akkadian texts with their own literary history.
Old Babylonian copies already show a long narrative. Later manuscripts
preserve the form usually called the Standard Babylonian epic. These
are related bodies of evidence, not pages from one unchanged original.[^gilgamesh]

| Surviving evidence | Approximate date | What the date tells us |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Early accounts from Uruk | Late fourth millennium BCE | Writing was in use. It does not date the first spoken myths. |
| Many school copies of Sumerian Gilgamesh poems | Eighteenth century BCE | These poems were being copied then. Some evidence is older. |
| Old Babylonian Gilgamesh manuscripts | Early second millennium BCE | An Akkadian narrative existed long before the copies at Nineveh. |
| The Flood Tablet from Nineveh | Seventh century BCE | This particular copy is Assyrian, not the first telling of the flood. |

The dates in this table are broad guides to surviving evidence.[^gilgamesh], [^flood-object]
They do not give us a chain of named authors. A late copy can preserve old
words. It can also preserve revisions. Age alone does not tell us how much
changed between copies.

This is why a careful edition names the manuscript as well as the work.
The distinction can feel fussy until two versions disagree. Then it is
the thing that allows us to understand the disagreement.

## The king's library

In the seventh century BCE, the Assyrian king Ashurbanipal assembled a
large collection of learned texts at Nineveh, beside modern Mosul in
northern Iraq. What we call his library was a working collection, shaped
by the king and his scribes. Recent research on its tablets shows different
stages of collecting and editing. Some texts were copied closely; others
were put into new forms.[^library]

Sometimes a tablet carries a note after the main text. Scholars call this
a *colophon*. These notes can tell us about ownership and the care taken
over copying. They also show how seriously knowledge could be guarded.
One of Ashurbanipal's formulas says that the tablet was checked by
scholars and kept for royal use. It calls on Nabu, the divine scribe, to
erase the name of anyone who erases the king's name and substitutes
their own.[^colophons]

The threat makes possession part of the text. The tablet carries words
about gods, and then calls on a god to protect the object itself. A copy
could be a source of knowledge and a claim to authority at once.

Nineveh was destroyed in 612 BCE. The familiar picture of an intact
library simply buried beneath a burning roof is incomplete. The British
Museum's recent project reports evidence that the collection was sifted,
deliberately smashed, and scattered around the palaces. The fragments
recovered by excavators therefore preserve the aftermath of handling and
destruction, as well as the earlier work of collection.[^library]

We should resist giving that destruction a happy ending because some
tablets survived. We do not have a list of every work that vanished. The
books we can read are evidence of survival; they cannot measure the full
size of the loss.

## How a broken text becomes a book

A modern edition often looks more complete than any one ancient tablet.
That appearance takes work.

An editor first needs to identify what is actually visible. A photograph
helps, but the writing is made of impressions in a surface. Examining the
object and checking a hand copy can change a reading. The signs are then
represented in modern characters, a step called *transliteration*. Working
out their language and meaning leads towards a translation.[^reading]

There are two different ways another fragment can help. It might fit a
broken edge of the same tablet. Or it might belong to a separate copy that
preserves the damaged passage. A physical join repairs part of an object.
A parallel copy supplies another witness to the wording. Those are
different kinds of evidence.[^reading]

When an editor draws on several witnesses to present a continuous text,
the result is a *composite text*. Oxford's Electronic Text Corpus of
Sumerian Literature makes such texts available alongside translations.
Much of the literature it presents has been recovered from many broken
tablets.[^corpus]

The marks around the words matter. Square brackets in many editions show
restored material where the tablet is damaged. An unreadable sign may be
marked with an *x*. Other signs mark uncertain readings, omissions, or
additions. Conventions differ, so the edition's own guide should always
come first.[^conventions]

There is a practical difference between a word supplied from a clear
parallel copy and a word suggested because it would fit the sentence.
Both may sit inside an editor's brackets. The notes explain the grounds
for the choice.

An honest blank can tell us more than a confident sentence. It lets us
know exactly where the evidence stops.

## A goddess and an extra eight lines

We can watch this work in a real myth. Near the beginning of the Sumerian
*Inana's Descent*, the goddess leaves her sanctuaries before travelling to
the underworld. In the Oxford edition, a note tells us that one manuscript
adds eight lines to the list of places she leaves. Later, another note
records a different version of her instructions to her minister.[^inana]

The extra lines are not a modern attempt to make the poem longer. They
are evidence that its ancient copies differed. A translation that quietly
blends them into one smooth account makes that fact harder to see.

Even the list of temples changes how we read the departure. Inana leaves
places where she has standing and receives worship. The journey takes her
away from a world that knows her rank. Read this way, the geography helps
prepare us for the gates below, where that rank will fail to protect her.
That is a reading of the poem, not proof of why an unnamed scribe added
a particular line.

The distinction leaves the story open to thought without making every
thought into an ancient fact.

## The flood reaches a new audience

The most famous tablet from Nineveh belongs to the flood episode in
*Gilgamesh*. The British Museum lists it as K.3375, a seventh-century BCE
copy.[^flood-object] The collection number matters: it points to a specific
object, not to every surviving version of the episode.

In the Standard Babylonian epic's eleventh tablet, Gilgamesh asks
Uta-napishti how he gained life beyond the ordinary human span.
Uta-napishti answers with the story of a flood. The gods decide on
destruction. Ea gives a warning, a boat is built, and living beings are
taken aboard. The account reaches Gilgamesh through a survivor speaking
about his own past.[^flood-text]

This setting matters. The flood belongs inside a poem about a man who
wants to escape death. Read on its own, the episode can seem like a loose
tale of disaster. Read in the poem, it answers a desperate question about
whether another person can cross the boundary that everyone else must
face. A parallel with a later story is worth studying, but it should not
make this literary setting disappear.

In 1872, George Smith announced his reading of the flood account. Its
resemblance to the biblical flood drew enormous attention. That moment
depended on earlier excavation at Nineveh and the work of people such as
Hormuzd Rassam, an archaeologist from Mosul who had helped bring the
Assyrian sites to light.[^discovery]

Deciphering a passage in London and finding the object in Iraq were
different parts of the recovery. Neither happened through the efforts of
a single person working alone.

The object above is a useful warning about appearances. It looks ancient,
but the Met identifies it as a nineteenth-century plaster cast of a modern
reconstruction. Paul Haupt built a clay model using several fragments as
evidence. Copies made the text available to students who could not handle
the originals.[^reconstruction]

It belongs in the history of learning to read the tablets. Its complete
shape should not persuade us that one equally complete ancient object
was lifted from the ground. A museum caption, like an editor's note, can
change what we think we are seeing.

## Reading with the gaps in view

Start with the story, but keep its source within reach. Notice whether
you are reading a translation of a named text or a retelling that combines
several versions. Check which language it comes from. Look at how the
translator marks damaged passages. When a surprising claim turns on one
word, follow the note.

We now have ways to do some of this without entering a museum study room.
The Cuneiform Digital Library Initiative provides object records and
digital access to tablets. Oxford's corpus offers Sumerian texts and
translations. The electronic Babylonian Library brings together editions
and a searchable collection of fragments. These are working scholarly
resources, so they should be read with their documentation and dates in
view.[^digital]

The objects also have a modern history. A tablet without a recorded find
place may still be readable, but we lose evidence about the room, building,
and other objects around it. A photograph cannot restore that
context.[^clay] Nor does an object's presence in a collection settle the
history of how it arrived there. In 2021, the United States formally handed
the Gilgamesh Dream Tablet back to Iraq after its illicit entry into the
American market. It is a different object from the Nineveh Flood Tablet.
Their shared hero does not make their histories interchangeable.[^return]

The geography remains real. These stories come from places where people
still live, and the tablets belong to the history of those places as well
as to world literature.

Return, finally, to the small exercise with Urash's name. We cannot turn
its pupil into a character with a face, a voice, or a childhood we have
invented. We can see the repeated signs. Someone learned them well enough
to press them into clay. The movement ended thousands of years ago, but
the marks remain available to another reader.

The next chapter begins before heaven and earth have names. Our way into
that imagined world begins here, with a hand and a piece of clay.
