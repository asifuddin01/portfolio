Eight arms spread from the shoulders of a woman carved in grey stone. The
slab comes from Bihar in eastern India and was made in the second half of
the ninth century. One hand swings a broad sword above her head. Others
hold a discus, a shield, a bow and a noose, and draw an arrow from a
quiver on her back. With the last pair she seizes a small man by the hair
and drives a trident into him. He is climbing out of the neck of a
buffalo whose head lies cut off at her feet.[^durga-met]

The Met's record explains what the weapons are. They were lent to her by
the assembly of the male gods, who could not, singly, defeat this
demon.[^durga-met] The sword and the trident and the discus are theirs.
The victory is hers.

That is the whole problem of the Goddess in one image. Is she the sum of
what the gods gave her, or are they parts of her? The text that tells
this story gives both answers, and this page follows it before turning
to where she came from and how many names she has.

## The poem that tells it

The story is told in a poem called the *Devi Mahatmya*, the greatness of
the Goddess. It survives as thirteen cantos inside a much longer work,
the Markandeya Purana.[^pargiter-cantos]

F. Eden Pargiter, who translated the Purana in 1904, judged that the
poem was complete in itself and had been added to the Purana later. He
dated it to the sixth or perhaps the fifth century CE. He also recorded
one fixed point: a manuscript of the poem in the royal library of Nepal,
written in an old script and dated 993 CE.[^pargiter-intro]

Pargiter needs the kind of warning this book has given about other old
translators. His English is serviceable and is quoted below because it
is in the public domain. His opinions are another matter. He admired the
poem's hymns, called its battles absurd and debased, and supposed that
its goddess had been borrowed from what he called the rude tribes of
central India.[^pargiter-intro] That is the voice of 1904 and is not
repeated here as fact.

He did record one thing of lasting value about its use. In his day, he
wrote, the poem was the textbook of the goddess's worshippers throughout
northern India and Bengal, especially at the great festival of Durga
Puja.[^pargiter-intro]

## Made from the gods

The second of its stories begins with a defeat. For a hundred years the
gods, led by Indra, fight the demons, led by Mahisha. The gods lose.
Mahisha takes heaven, and they are driven out.[^dm-82]

They go to Vishnu and Shiva and tell them what has happened. At the
news a great energy bursts from the mouths of Vishnu, Shiva and Brahma,
and from the bodies of Indra and all the other gods. The lights flow together into
one blaze like a burning mountain. In Pargiter's words, that energy,
*gathering into one became a female*.[^dm-82]

The poem then says exactly which god's energy formed which part of her,
and which weapon each one gave.

| The god | What his energy formed | What he gave her |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Shiva | her face | a trident |
| Vishnu | her arms | a discus |
| Yama, god of death | her hair | a rod |
| Indra | her waist | a thunderbolt, and a bell |
| Varuna | her legs | a conch |
| Agni | her three eyes | a spear |
| Vayu, the wind | her ears | a bow and a full quiver |
| Brahma | her feet | a water-pot |
| The Sun | her toes | his rays, on her skin |

The list in the poem is longer than this table.[^dm-82] Even at this
length it shows something about the last chapter. Every great god of the
Rigveda is here. Indra brings the thunderbolt with which he killed
Vritra. Agni and Varuna and the wind are present. But they are present
as donors. The powers that once ran the world one by one are gathered
into a single figure, and she is not one of them.

The battle fills two cantos. At the end Mahisha is in the form of a
buffalo. She leaps on him and pins his neck with her foot.
He begins to come out of the buffalo's mouth in his own form, and when
he is half out she strikes off his head with her great
sword.[^dm-83] That is the moment the sculptor in Bihar carved.

## Alone

If the poem ended there, the Goddess would be a weapon the gods built.
It does not end there.

In its third story two demon brothers defeat the gods again. The Goddess
fights, and this time other goddesses fight beside her, each one the
power of a male god in female form: the power of Brahma, of Vishnu, of
Indra with thunderbolt in hand.[^dm-88] When one brother is
dead the other taunts her. She is proud, he says, but she is fighting
with the strength of others.

Her answer is the centre of the poem:

*Alone verily am I in the world here; what other goddess is there
besides me?*[^dm-90]

And the other goddesses, who, she says, have their power from her, enter
her body and disappear. She stands alone and kills him.[^dm-90]

Put the two stories side by side. In the second the gods make her out of
themselves. In the third she takes back into herself everything that
looked separate. The poem does not choose. The word for what she is, in
the tradition that worships her as supreme, is *shakti*:
power.[^dehejia-hinduism] The goddesses who join her in battle are called
the energies of the gods.[^dm-88] On this reading the claim of the poem
is that energy is not something the gods own. It is her.

She makes one more statement before the poem closes. Whenever trouble
arises from the demons, she says, she will come again and destroy
them.[^dm-91]

## Kali

One moment in the third story produced the most startling of her forms.

Demon generals come to seize her. Her face, the poem says, grows dark as
ink with anger. From her frowning forehead another goddess springs out:
Kali, of terrible countenance, carrying a sword and a noose and a staff
topped with a skull, and wearing a garland of skulls. She destroys the
army.[^dm-87]

An image like this is easy to misread, and has been. The Met's note on
the print says so directly: Kali's blood-stained form came to embody
everything that Europeans in India most feared, and misunderstood, in
Hindu society.[^kali-met] Pargiter's remarks about barbarous practices
are an example from the same years.

The same note explains how her worshippers see her. She is the supreme
embodiment of female power, a form of Parvati, the loving wife of Shiva,
and she is especially worshipped in Bengal. She has power over time and
is called the destroyer of time. In the print she is young and
beautiful. The two greatest male gods appear small in the clouds, as
witnesses to what she can do.[^kali-met]

Look at who is under her feet. It is Shiva, her husband. The print
reverses the usual order of a god with his consort beside him.
Here power stands, and the god lies still beneath it.

## Before the poem

Where did she come from? The honest answer is that nobody can trace it
fully.

The Rigveda has few goddesses of any weight. The dawn, Ushas, has about
twenty hymns, and Arthur Macdonell calls her almost the only female
deity to whom whole hymns are addressed.[^macdonell-ushas]

But one late hymn stands out. Its speaker is Speech herself, and she
talks in a way no other Vedic voice does. *I am the Queen*, she says in
Griffith's translation. She holds up Varuna and Mitra, Indra and Agni.
She makes whom she loves mighty. She bends the bow for Rudra. She has
spread through earth and heaven, and breathes like the wind while she
holds all existence together.[^rv-vac] A reader who comes to this hymn
after the *Devi Mahatmya* will find the resemblance hard to miss. It
would be too much to call it the same goddess. It is the same kind of
claim, made many centuries earlier.

An Upanishad adds a small scene. The gods have won a victory and think
it their own. A mysterious being appears before them. Agni is sent to
find out what it is, and cannot burn a straw that it sets before him.
The wind cannot lift it. When Indra approaches, the being
vanishes, and in its place he finds a woman, *highly adorned*, named
Uma, daughter of the Himalaya. It is she who tells him what the gods had
met: the absolute, by whose power alone they had won.[^kena] Uma is
also a name of Shiva's wife.[^uma-name]

Vidya Dehejia points to a source outside the Veda altogether. One of
the strands that formed Hinduism, she writes, was the belief among other
peoples of the subcontinent in the power of a mother
goddess.[^dehejia-hinduism] That belief left no early texts, which is why
it cannot be dated. What can be dated is stone. One of the earliest
goddesses who can be recognised in the art of southern Asia is the one
in the next section.

## Many names

The Goddess is one, and she is also a number of distinct figures whom
people worship separately. Three of them matter most for this chapter.

**Lakshmi** is fortune. The Vishnu page told how she rose from the ocean
on a lotus and was bathed by the elephants of the sky before she went to
Vishnu's side. That bathing is what this carving shows, and the Met
notes that it is among the very earliest goddess images that can be
identified at all.[^lakshmi-met] She is Vishnu's consort, and she is
also worshipped for herself by anyone who hopes to prosper.

**Parvati** is the daughter of the mountain and the wife of Shiva, and the
mother of Ganesha. Dehejia insists that she is a major deity in her own
right, and gives the link that ties this page together: it is Parvati
who, as Durga, kills the demons the other gods cannot
control.[^dehejia-recognizing] The graceful figure in this bronze and
the eight-armed warrior at the top of the page are the same goddess.

**Kali** we have met. The Met's note calls her, too, a manifestation of
Parvati.[^kali-met]

So the gentlest and the most terrible of these forms are one person, and
her worshippers know it. Dehejia's essay names the goddess at the centre
of Hinduism simply as Shakti, power.[^dehejia-hinduism] The names are
the many ways that power is met: as luck, as a mother, as a wife, as a
rescuer, as the end of all things.

There is one more way she is shown, mentioned on the overview page. In
some images Shiva's body is male on the right side and female on the
left.[^ardhanarishvara] After reading the *Devi Mahatmya* it is hard to
see the female half as an attachment.

## The goddess comes home

The Goddess does come back every year, in clay.

Each September or October the city of Kolkata gives ten days to the
festival of Durga. UNESCO placed it on its list of the intangible
heritage of humanity in 2021, and its description is worth following
step by step. For months beforehand, small workshops model images of
Durga and her family in unfired clay taken from the Ganga. Worship
begins on a day when the eyes are painted on, which is what brings the
goddess to life in the clay. On the tenth day the images are carried
back to the river the clay came from and immersed.[^unesco-durga]

UNESCO says the festival also means a homecoming, a seasonal return to
one's roots. And it reports something about the crowds. During those
days, divisions of class, religion and ethnicity collapse as people
walk through the city to look at the images
together.[^unesco-durga]

Go back to the stone from Bihar. Its Durga carries eight weapons, and
none of them is her own. The text behind it says that the gods are
real, that their gifts are real, and that she is nevertheless alone in
the world. Eleven centuries later the same figure is made of river clay
every autumn and given back to the river.
