A shaft of dark stone thirty-five centimetres high, rounded at the top,
stands on a square base. It was carved in Kashmir in the seventh century.
From one side of the shaft a face looks out: a calm young man with a
third eye in his forehead, heavy rings in his ears, and his hair drawn up
in loops with a crescent moon set in it.[^linga-met]

This object is called a *linga*. It is the form of Shiva that stands in
the inner chapel of his temples, as it does at Elephanta.[^elephanta]
The Met's title for a plain example gives the idea in three words: it is
his aniconic symbol, a sign that is not a portrait.[^linga-plain] Here the sculptor has done something that looks
like a contradiction. He has carved a sign that is not a picture, and
then let the god's face come through it.

That double nature is a good place to start. Almost everything about
Shiva comes in pairs that should not fit: the terror and the healer, the
hermit and the husband, the destroyer who dances. This page takes them in
the order the evidence gives them.

## The god they asked to stay away

In the Rigveda his name is Rudra, and the poets are afraid of him.

He has only three or four hymns, by Arthur Macdonell's count. They do
not praise great deeds, as Indra's hymns do. They ask him not to do
harm.[^macdonell-rudra] One of them, in Griffith's translation, calls him
tawny and fierce, an archer who slays *like a dread beast of the forest*.
The poet begs that his missile will turn aside, that his great anger will
pass by, that he will spare the singer's children and
horses.[^rv-rudra]

The same hymn turns to him as a doctor. *I hear thee famed as best of all
physicians*, the poet says, and asks for his medicines and for a life of
a hundred winters.[^rv-rudra] Fear and need are in the same verses. The
one who is dreaded is the one who heals.

Even his name was handled with care. *Shiva* began as a word applied to
Rudra, meaning auspicious or kindly. Macdonell calls it a euphemism that
became his regular name.[^macdonell-rudra] Stephanie Jamison and Michael
Witzel are more direct: it was a replacement for a name too dangerous to
say, and the texts sometimes avoid even that and speak only of *that
god*.[^jw-rudra]

## One Rudra only

A later text speaks of the same god in a completely different voice.

The Shvetashvatara Upanishad belongs to the last layer of the Veda, the
layer of speculation about what lies behind the ritual. In Max Müller's
translation it says: *there is one Rudra only, they do not allow a
second*. He rules all the worlds by his powers. He stands behind every
person. Having created all the worlds, he is their protector, and he
rolls them up again at the end of time.[^svet]

The old fear has not gone. The next verses speak to him as a dweller in
the mountains, ask him to look on the worshipper with the form of his
that is *auspicious, not terrible*, and beg him not to loose the arrow
in his hand: *Do not hurt man or beast!*[^svet] But the archer on the
edge of the settled world is now also the one God, present, the text
says, in the faces and the hearts of all beings.

Nothing in the surviving texts explains how the one became the other.
Jamison and Witzel call the rise of both great gods
controversial.[^jw-rudra] What the texts do preserve is a story about
his admission.

## The uninvited guest

A patriarch named Daksha holds a great sacrifice. It is a rite of the old
kind, with every god invited and a share of the offering set aside for
each. All of them come, with Indra at their head. One god has been left
out.

H. H. Wilson translated a version of the story in 1840. In it Shiva's
wife sees the gods go by and asks him why he is not going too. He
answers plainly. It is an old arrangement among the gods, he says,
*that in all sacrifices no portion should be assigned to
me*.[^daksha]

At the sacrifice itself a sage objects, and tells Daksha that a rite
which ignores the greatest god cannot be completed. Daksha replies that
he knows the eleven Rudras of the old hymns and no other Mahadeva, and
makes his offering to Vishnu instead.[^daksha]

It is the goddess who cannot bear the insult. Out of her displeasure a
being of terrible power comes into existence, Virabhadra, and Shiva
gives him one instruction: *Spoil the sacrifice of Daksha.* The altars are overturned
and the posts uprooted. The sacrifice itself tries to flee in the shape
of a deer, and is beheaded. The gods are bound. Virabhadra tells them
that the anger of Rudra is better than the blessings of other
gods.[^daksha]

Then it ends, and not in revenge. Daksha submits and sits in
meditation. Shiva appears from the ruined altar, smiling, and says that
he is pleased. He grants that the wasted offerings shall not have been
prepared in vain, and Daksha praises him by his eight thousand
names.[^daksha]

It is hard to imagine a clearer account of this chapter's subject. The
Vedic sacrifice is the old religion. The god left without a share is the
one the Rigveda asked to keep his distance. The story does not have him
replace the sacrifice. It has him break in, and be acknowledged, and
restore it.

Wilson read the legend as the memory of a struggle between the followers
of Shiva and of Vishnu.[^daksha] That is one scholar's guess from 1840
and should be taken as such. He was on firmer ground in noticing how
much sculptors loved the scene. It is carved at Elephanta.

## The sign and the face

Return to the linga. The Met's note on the stone from Kashmir calls
Shiva emerging in human form from the shaft a celebrated representation
that rose to prominence in north India during the fifth century. It also
states plainly what the shaft is. The incised lines on it, the record
says, mark it as phallic.[^linga-met]

That needs saying without embarrassment and without making too much of
it. The form is what it is. In worship it is the god's sign, and the
same word is used for a plain shaft of stone with no carving at
all.[^linga-plain]

At Elephanta, on an island near Mumbai, the two are set in one
building. The caves were cut between about the middle of the fifth
century and the sixth. A chapel for the linga stands inside the main
cave, with large reliefs of the god's deeds around it, and the entrance
is dominated by the seven-metre bust with three faces that the overview
page described.[^elephanta]

## Reading his body

When Shiva is shown as a whole figure, Vidya Dehejia lists what to look
for. He carries a trident. A serpent is often thrown around him like a
scarf. His hair is matted and piled high, with a skull and the crescent
moon in it. A third eye in his forehead stands for his all-seeing
nature. His mount is a bull.[^dehejia]

Nothing in this list is royal. Vishnu wears a crown and jewels. Shiva
wears what a wandering ascetic wears, and Dehejia describes him in those
terms: the great practitioner of yoga, who sat in meditation for
ages.[^dehejia]

The three faces at Elephanta are a way of showing the pairs in one image.
UNESCO's description gives each a name and a character: a fierce face on
one side, a calm one in the middle, and on the other side one that it
identifies with the feminine, with Uma.[^elephanta] Dehejia gives the
general rule for such heads: the central face shows the god's essential
character, and the flanking ones his fierce and his blissful
aspects.[^dehejia-hinduism]

The Met calls the image a brilliant invention, because it puts all of
Shiva's roles into one figure: creator, preserver and destroyer at
once.[^nataraja-met] Each part can be read.

| Part of the image | What the museum says it means |
| --- | --- |
| The drum in the upper right hand | the first sound of creation |
| The flame in the upper left hand | the fire that will destroy the universe |
| The lower right hand, palm out | the gesture that takes away fear |
| The small figure under his foot | illusion, which leads people astray |
| The hand pointing to the raised foot | refuge for the troubled soul |
| The ring of flames | the edge of the cosmos |

Notice that this single figure does by himself what the row of three
gods was supposed to share out. Creation is in one hand and destruction
in the other, and the same god protects with a third. It is the claim
of the Upanishad again, made in bronze.

Notice also what kind of destroyer this is. The fire is in his hand,
but his face is calm, and the hand nearest the worshipper says *do not
be afraid*.

## A hermit with a household

The ascetic has a family, and the tradition enjoys the contradiction.

Dehejia tells the turning point in a sentence. Shiva spent ages in
meditation until he opened his eyes, saw the goddess Parvati, and fell
in love with her. The texts call her the daughter of the mountain
king.[^daksha] They have two sons: Ganesha, who has the head of an elephant and removes obstacles,
and Skanda, a young warrior who rides a peacock.[^dehejia]

The Met holds other images of the couple seated together from Nepal.
They show a different god from the archer of the hymn: at home, at ease,
with his wife beside him. In the Daksha story it is she who
acts when he is slighted.

Sometimes the two are not shown side by side but as one body, male on
the right and female on the left.[^ardhanarishvara] The page for Devi
takes up what that says about her.

## The woman who sang to him

Gods in this chapter are not only described. They are loved, and some of
the people who loved them are remembered by name.

  <figcaption>

    thirteenth century CE, copper alloy, 23.2 centimetres high, from Tamil
    Nadu. She holds a pair of cymbals and is shown singing. The bronze was
    made some seven hundred years after she is said to have lived, and is
    a portrait of a saint as later devotion pictured her, not a likeness.
    The Met, 1982.220.11. Purchase, Edward J. Gallagher Jr. Bequest, 1982.
    Image: The Metropolitan Museum of Art, public domain.
    <a href="https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/39413">Object record and full image</a>.
  </figcaption>
</figure>

Karaikkal Ammaiyar was a poet of the sixth century in the Tamil south.
The Met's record tells her story as her tradition does. She asked Shiva
to free her from everything that tied her to the world, and above all
from her famous beauty. He granted it and turned her into a withered old
woman, and she spent the rest of her life, delighted, composing hymns to
him. Her hymns celebrate his fondness for cremation grounds and his
wild dance.[^ammaiyar-met]

She is not the only saint remembered in bronze: the same museum holds a
figure of the child saint Sambandar.[^dehejia] And the Vishnu page ended
on the same note from the other side: a god who can be reached by a leaf
and an honest heart. This is what *bhakti* looked like in
practice. A woman sings in her own language, with a pair of cymbals, to
a god she speaks to directly.

Go back to the stone from Kashmir. The shaft is a sign with no features,
and it could stand for a god as far from human as the one the Rigveda
feared. The face that comes out of it is smiling. Shiva's worshippers
have kept both, and have never thought they had to choose.
