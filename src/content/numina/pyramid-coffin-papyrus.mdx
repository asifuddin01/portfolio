Inside a hollow wooden figure of Osiris lay a roll of papyrus. It belonged
to Nauny, a singer in the service of Amun-Re, buried at Thebes around
1050 BCE. Unrolled, it stretches more than five metres. Among its painted
scenes, Nauny stands beside a balance on which her heart is weighed.[^nauny]

The roll was made for someone with a name.

That is easy to forget when we open a modern book of Egyptian mythology.
We meet a cast of gods and expect their stories to explain a whole
civilisation. But many of the texts that preserve those gods had a more
immediate task. They were meant to help a particular dead person survive.
They addressed a body, called upon a god, or supplied words for a dangerous
encounter. Their first concern was not to make the mythology easy for us
to follow.

So we begin with the objects that carry the writing. A wall, a coffin and
a roll ask us to read in different ways. They also remind us how much a
printed translation leaves out.

## Three names, overlapping collections

The Pyramid Texts, Coffin Texts and Book of the Dead are names for
collections. No ancient writer sat down and composed all three. Nor did
Egypt move neatly from one to the next, throwing away the old texts as
soon as new ones appeared.[^pyramid][^coffin]

Here is a starting map. These dates describe important surviving evidence,
not a precise birthday for every belief.

**Pyramid Texts.** Royal pyramids at Saqqara, beginning with Unas in the
late Fifth Dynasty, in the twenty-fourth century BCE. Some compositions
continued in later tombs and on other surfaces.

**Coffin Texts.** Especially Middle Kingdom coffins and burial chambers,
in the early second millennium BCE. The collection includes inherited
material as well as other compositions.

**Book of the Dead.** Early evidence in the seventeenth dynasty, followed
by numerous papyrus copies from the New Kingdom onward. Its spells also
appear on linen, walls and funerary objects.

These dates follow UCL's introductions to the collections. The boundaries
are useful for study, but they are not sealed divisions in Egyptian
religious life.[^pyramid][^coffin][^bd-surfaces]

## Words inside a pyramid

The earliest surviving extensive collection called the Pyramid Texts is
inside the pyramid of Unas at Saqqara. He was the last king of the Fifth
Dynasty. Later kings and some queens also had texts inscribed in their
pyramids. This is a particular group of monuments, not a description of
every Egyptian pyramid.[^unas][^pyramid]

An inscription in a burial chamber has a different audience from a story
read aloud for pleasure. The words address the dead and the powers on
whom the dead depend. Their placement makes the chamber part of their
meaning. Taking them off the walls and putting them in a continuous
printed sequence is already an act of interpretation.

A fragment from Pepy's pyramid in the Petrie Museum gives a small example.
The king's name appears four times. The surviving formulae concern his
ascent to heaven and his supply of food and drink. These needs belong
together: reaching a divine destination does not remove the need to be
sustained there.[^pepy]

Such passages make a practical demand upon the gods. The person for whom
they work must be recognised, admitted and fed. A reader looking only for
an account of how the universe began will miss much of what these texts
are doing.

The date of Unas's inscription also needs care. It tells us when this
surviving writing was made. It cannot, on its own, tell us when every
formula was first spoken. The first surviving copy and the first use of
a text are different things.

## A coffin that looks out

Move forward to the Middle Kingdom. Texts now surround many non-royal
owners on the inner surfaces of their coffins. Egyptologists group a
large body of this material under the name *Coffin Texts*. Some passages
draw upon the Pyramid Texts. Others broaden the surviving record. Later
funerary compositions will draw on this material in turn.[^coffin]

The body lies within the writing. Reading a translation removes that
physical relationship. It gives us sentences in an order, but not the
experience of a wooden enclosure whose surfaces have been prepared for
one dead person.

Look at the eyes on Khnumnakht's coffin. They sit above a painted doorway.
The museum explains that this panel faced east, towards sunrise, with the
mummy's face behind it. The exterior inscriptions call upon deities
including Osiris and Anubis. Decoration, writing and the position of the
body work together.[^khnumnakht]

Some coffins from Deir el-Bersha also preserve material known as the
*Book of Two Ways*. Parts of that composition later appear in Book of the
Dead spell 136. We can follow particular passages across collections,
rather than merely saying that a later book resembles an earlier one.[^two-ways]

## Who could hope to live again?

There is a familiar account of this change. First, it says, only the king
could enjoy a full afterlife. Then royal power weakened and other people
gained the same hope. This has often been called the democratization of
the afterlife.

The evidence does not support that simple progression. Mark Smith's study
points to Old Kingdom evidence for non-royal people receiving ritual
recitations and expecting to become effective spirits among the gods.
In the tomb of Kagemni, for example, a priest is shown reading from a
roll for the dead man's benefit.[^afterlife]

The appearance of more writing on coffins cannot prove that the people
inside them had previously been excluded from life after death. It may
tell us about changes in how ritual was recorded and displayed.

Nor does *non-royal* mean *available to everyone*. An inscribed coffin
required resources. The people most visible in this evidence are not a
fair sample of everyone who lived beside the Nile.[^afterlife]

We need to leave room for people whose hopes have not survived in costly
objects. A missing inscription is not a record of what its owner did
not believe.

## A book made from a plant

Papyrus introduces another kind of surface. Its sheets were made from
the pale inner tissue of the papyrus stalk, arranged in two layers at
right angles. Sheets could be joined into a roll. The crossing fibres
are still part of what we see when we look closely at a manuscript.[^making]

The general structure is clear, but the complete ancient manufacturing
process was not documented for us. Modern experiments and examination
of surviving sheets help fill that gap. We should not mistake a modern
demonstration for a witnessed ancient procedure.

Scribes wrote with black and red ink, while illustrations could use a
wider range of colours. The material could hold an image and a passage
beside one another, then roll up into a small space. Age, burial conditions
and later conservation have all affected the appearance of the sheets
we now read.[^making]

*Book of the Dead* is the conventional modern name for a body of funerary
formulae associated with going out by day. A surviving manuscript usually
contains a selection. It is not an incomplete copy simply because it
lacks spells found in another person's roll.[^bd-background]

Early manuscripts vary greatly. A more regular sequence became established
in the Late Period. The familiar chapter numbers belong to modern
scholarship, not to a numbering system shared by all ancient scribes.
They help us compare manuscripts, but they should not make us imagine
one fixed book behind every copy.[^bd-numbering]

Nauny's heart is balanced against a small figure of Ma'at. Osiris presides;
Anubis attends the scales. The image presents a favourable judgment.
It belongs to the work of securing her future, rather than reporting an
event that an artist watched.[^nauny]

We cannot simply assume that each owner chose every spell personally.
UCL's discussion notes the absence of explicit accounts of commissioning
these manuscripts. The finished roll survives more readily than the
conversation that arranged it.[^bd-background]

## What a spell asks someone to do

The word *spell* can make these texts sound like mysterious words with
no practical setting. Consider a particular case instead.

Book of the Dead spell 100, in UCL's translation from the papyrus of Nu,
links the deceased with the company of the sun god. Its instructions
specify an image on fresh papyrus, a preparation involving green pigment,
and placement at the dead person's breast. Speech, an image, a material
and a location belong to the same operation.[^spell100]

In an edition, the person's name may be replaced by *N*. The ancient
manuscript could name the individual in full. That small editorial
shortcut lets us compare versions, but it also makes the text feel less
personal than the object was.[^spell100]

Another formula, spell 105, concerns the nourishment of the person's
*ka*. UCL's version includes an address to the ka and references to
purification with natron and incense. A religious term can remain
unfamiliar while the concern is plain: the dead person must continue to
receive what sustains them.[^spell105]

These are useful questions to bring to any passage: who speaks, whose
name appears, what has to be done, and what outcome is sought? They often
explain more than a list of gods alone.

## A text that keeps more than one answer

Egyptian religious writing sometimes includes its own interpretation.
Book of the Dead spell 17 offers an unusually clear example. Its earlier
form is known as Coffin Text 335. Statements identifying the deceased
with divine beings are followed by explanations, and some passages
preserve alternative explanations.[^spell17]

One passage connects yesterday with Osiris and tomorrow with Ra. Elsewhere,
a divine identification can receive more than one reading. The text
does not always discard one answer so that another can stand alone.

Here is a composition in which more than one interpretation was worth
preserving. Calling that carelessness would miss what the scribes kept.

There is another caution. The UCL text is a modern presentation based
on Naville's edition of several sources. It does not reproduce one
perfect ancient roll containing everything we see on the webpage.
When we read an edition, we need to know whether we are reading one
manuscript or material assembled from several.[^spell17]

## The old words stay in use

A burial chamber makes the overlap between collections visible.
Sobekmose served as a treasury official under Amenhotep III. His chamber
at el-Rizeiqat dates to the fourteenth century BCE. Its inscriptions
include selections from the Book of the Dead alongside a composition
classified as Pyramid Text utterance 32.[^sobekmose]

The old classification remains useful to us, but it did not prevent an
Egyptian owner from using old and newer material in the same burial.
Neither did the name *Book of the Dead* confine its contents to papyrus.
Here, funerary words were cut into stone.[^sobekmose]

Our three materials therefore give us a route through the evidence,
not a law that the evidence must obey.

## Reading what survives

There are further steps between a burial and a book on our shelf.
Fragments may have to be brought back into sequence. A damaged sign
must be distinguished from a sign a scribe actually wrote. An object
without a secure findspot cannot tell us as much about its original
setting as one recorded carefully during excavation.

The papyrus of Hepres in the Petrie Museum shows this work in practice.
Its fragments, of uncertain provenance, were the subject of a conservation
project in 2000 to 2002 that sought to recover their arrangement. The
manuscript belongs to the eighteenth dynasty, around 1400 BCE. Its ancient
date and its modern reconstruction are two parts of its history.[^hepres]

Even an ancient statement about a text's age needs examination. The
Shabaka Stone claims to preserve damaged older writing. The British
Museum's record treats that account cautiously. What the inscription
says about its source is evidence to assess, not a date we can accept
without question. Its Memphite creation account gives Ptah a central
role in bringing things into existence through speech.[^shabaka]

That distinction will matter in the next chapter, when we meet Egypt's
accounts of creation. We will need to ask which text preserves each
account and what that text was for. A statement in a funerary spell and
a statement honouring a city's god do not necessarily answer the same
question.

For now, look again at the eyes painted on Khnumnakht's coffin. They face
outwards. The box closes around a body, but someone has taken care to
paint a way of looking beyond it.
