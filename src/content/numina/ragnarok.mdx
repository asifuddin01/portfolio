Most mythologies keep the end of the world at a distance — a threat, a warning,
something conditional on behaviour. Norse myth does not. It gives the ending in
detail, in advance, with the participants named and the outcomes assigned, and
then has everybody carry on.

## What is foretold

The *Völuspá* has a seeress deliver it to Odin. Three winters come without a
summer between them. Brothers kill each other. The wolf Fenrir, bound by a
ribbon that held because it was made of impossible things, gets loose. The
Midgard serpent leaves the sea. Loki, who has been chained under dripping
venom, arrives with the ship of the dead.

Then the assignments. Odin is swallowed by Fenrir, and Víðarr avenges him.
Thor kills the serpent and walks nine paces before the poison takes him. Freyr
falls to Surtr, having given away his sword for a woman. Heimdallr and Loki
kill each other, which is tidy. The sun is eaten, the stars go out, and the
earth sinks into the sea.

And afterwards — this part is easy to miss — the earth comes up again, green.
Baldr returns from the dead. Some of the young gods survive, find the old chess
pieces in the grass, and start again.

## Why the foreknowledge matters

Odin spends the entire mythology acquiring information. He gives an eye for a
drink from Mímir's well. He hangs himself on the world tree for nine nights,
wounded with a spear, to get the runes. He raises a dead seeress to ask what
is coming. He collects the best of the slain into Valhalla, and the reason
given is explicit: he needs an army for the last battle.

None of it changes anything. He learns that Fenrir will kill him and gathers
the army anyway; the army loses anyway. The knowledge buys no escape, and the
mythology does not pretend otherwise.

That is the shape worth noticing. This is not a system where virtue is
rewarded with survival, or where the right sacrifice averts the disaster. It is
a system in which the disaster is scheduled, everyone competent knows it, and
the question of what to do about it has exactly one answer: show up.

## A caution about the source

Nearly all the detail comes from two places, and one of them is a book by a
thirteenth-century Icelandic politician. Snorri Sturluson was writing some two
hundred years after Iceland's conversion, for an audience of Christian poets
who needed to understand the pagan metaphors their craft was built on.

He is careful and he clearly had older material, but he is also arranging it,
and he had read enough Christian eschatology for the resemblance to be worth a
raised eyebrow. The world consumed by fire, the return of an innocent
sacrificed god, the green earth afterwards — how much of that is Norse and how
much is Snorri is a question with no settled answer.
