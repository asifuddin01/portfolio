Between the legs of a mummy at Deir el-Bahri, the Met's excavators found a
papyrus rolled into a tight scroll. The mummy was that of Henettawy,
daughter of Isetemkheb, a wealthy woman buried in a family tomb around 990
to 970 BCE. Unrolled, her papyrus is 139 centimetres long and is drawn in
black and red ink.[^henettawy]

The left end is blank. The rest holds one scene in three bands. In the
middle band a boat carries a row of standing figures and a small shrine.
Ahead of it a black hawk sits on the back of a serpent, and a second boat
carries a long snake. In the band above, a beetle pushes an oval. In the
band below, bodies float in water drawn as zigzag lines.

The museum identifies the scene as the tenth hour of the night, from a
composition called the Amduat. It suggests that the scribe meant to add
one or more further hours in the empty space, and never did.[^henettawy]

Why would a woman be buried with one hour of the night laid on her body?
The answer needs two journeys. One is the sun's, and it is made every
night. The other is made once, by each dead person, and it ends at a pair
of scales. This last chapter of the Egyptian book follows both, and then
asks what became of those who failed.

## The country under the day

The Egyptian name of the place is the Duat. The Met glosses it as the
netherworld. Budge, whose translation of 1905 is the main guide in this
chapter, wrote the name *Tuat* and described it as the country the sun
god crosses during the hours of the night, which is also the realm of the
dead.[^henettawy][^budge-book]

We have already been told who rules it. When Horus became king, the world
reached its present arrangement: a king on earth, the sun travelling in
his boat through the day and night skies, and Osiris ruling the dead
below.[^earlier] The Duat is where the second and third of these meet.

It should not be called hell, and Budge himself said so. He objected to
the titles *Book of Hades* and *Book of Hell* that earlier scholars had
given to one of the compositions about it, and to the idea that the place
lay beneath our feet in a lower hemisphere.[^budge-book] The sun passes
through it and the blessed dead live in it, as well as the condemned.

Egypt also kept another picture of the night, in which the sky goddess
Nut gives birth to the sun each morning. The two pictures were not
treated as rivals. Budge's twelfth hour calls her the womb of the
morning, and on the sarcophagus shown below the box carries the journey
by boat while the lid carries Nut.[^budge-twelve][^wereshnefer]

## A book made for kings

The composition on Henettawy's papyrus is known by two Egyptian words,
*am duat*, what is in the Duat.[^budge-book] It is a book of pictures with
captions. According to the Met, it appeared first in the royal tombs of
the New Kingdom, and its annotated scenes represent the twelve hours of
the night.[^henettawy]

How early it began is still argued. Limestone slabs carved with hours of
the Amduat were found in two tombs in the Valley of the Kings, numbered
KV 38 and KV 20, and scholars have disagreed about which reign they
belong to. A current project at Göttingen, which conserved the slabs,
argues that all of them come from the tomb of Thutmose I and are the
oldest royal version, earlier than the copy in the tomb of
Thutmose III.[^earliest]

Budge, writing in 1905, knew the oldest copies from the tombs of
Thutmose III, Amenhotep II and Amenhotep III. The fullest, he says, is on
the walls of the tomb of Seti I, which has eleven of the twelve
hours.[^budge-book] UCL describes that tomb as the longest in the valley,
decorated throughout with scenes taken mainly from the books of the
underworld.[^ucl-sety]

Then the book moved. Budge traces the sequence: first the walls of tombs,
then stone sarcophagi and wooden coffins, then rolls of papyrus. On
papyrus, he observes, one usually finds only the last four hours, from the
ninth to the twelfth.[^budge-book] Henettawy's tenth hour fits that
habit.

Wereshnefer was a priest, not a king, and he lived more than a thousand
years after the texts on his sarcophagus were composed for royalty. The
Met's record explains the plan. The box stands for the netherworld. Hours
one to seven run around the outside, starting at the head, and hours nine
to twelve are inside; the eighth was left out. In the middle band of each
hour the sun god stands in a shrine on his night boat, with the beings of
that region above and below him. The lid is the day sky.[^wereshnefer]

A word on Budge is needed before we follow him. His translation is in the
public domain, which is why it can be quoted. But his readings of Egyptian
names are out of date, and his explanations are his own: he thought the
Amduat was written by priests of Amun to assert their god's supremacy over
the dead.[^budge-book] He is used below for what the scenes contain, and
the museum records are used to check him.

## Twelve hours

Each hour is a region with a name, a gate and a goddess of its
own.[^budge-seven] The sun passes through them standing in a boat, but he
does not arrive as the sun of the day. Budge calls the traveller Afu-Ra,
the flesh of Ra, and speaks of him as *the dead Sun-god*, journeying in a
mummified form.[^budge-twelve] What goes down in the west is a body.

By the fifth hour the boat is being towed through the land of Sokar, a
region walled with sand and lying in thick darkness, where heads show
above a lake of boiling water.[^budge-five]

The sixth hour is the turning point. In Budge's account the dead god lies
on the back of a five-headed serpent and draws towards himself the beetle
of Khepri, the sign of coming into being that we met in the chapter on
creation.[^budge-six] The Met puts the event in other words: in the
deepest hour Ra joins with Osiris, king of the dead, so that he can be
reborn in the morning.[^henettawy]

Jiří Janák explains what that joining meant. New Kingdom texts present it
as a union of two parts of one being: Ra as the *ba*, the mobile and
manifest part, and Osiris as the corpse.[^janak] The first chapter quoted
a line from the Book of the Dead that set yesterday beside Osiris and
tomorrow beside Ra. The sixth hour is where yesterday and tomorrow touch.

Danger follows at once. In the seventh hour there is not enough water to
float the boat, and the way is blocked by Apep, the serpent, stretched on
a sandbank that the text makes four hundred and fifty cubits long. Isis
speaks her words of power. The serpent is fettered at head and tail and
pinned to the ground with knives, and the boat passes.[^budge-seven]

We have met this enemy before. The papyrus of Nesmin, where the chapter
on creation began, held a whole book of spells for overthrowing him so
that the sun could rise. Rita Lucarelli notes that Apep stands apart from
every other dangerous being of the netherworld. As the cosmic enemy of
the sun god, he belongs to neither the gods nor the demons.[^lucarelli]

Then comes Henettawy's hour. In the tenth, Budge says, the boat is neither
towed nor rowed. A hawk rides on a serpent before it, and gods armed with
arrows, javelins and bows go ahead as an escort. A beetle pushes an oval
of sand, which Budge reads as the seed of new life entering the dead
god's body. And in a row of lakes, beings are plunging, swimming and
floating, while Horus leans on a staff and promises them that their limbs
will not perish and their flesh will not decay.[^budge-ten]

Budge admits he cannot say who the beings in the water are.[^budge-ten]
The Met says only that in this hour the inhabitants of the Duat begin to
prepare for sunrise.[^henettawy] Either way, these appear to be the
figures lying in the zigzag water at the bottom of Henettawy's roll.

The twelfth hour is called, in Budge's words, the *uttermost limit of
thick darkness*. Twelve servants take the tow-rope and draw the boat
through the body of an enormous serpent, in at the tail and out at the
mouth. Inside it the god becomes Khepri. Twelve goddesses tow him up into
the sky, and Shu, the air, receives him. The mummified form in which he
travelled is left behind, lying against the wall that divides the Duat
from this world.[^budge-twelve]

The chapter on creation ended by saying that the first time had to keep
happening. The Amduat describes how it was thought to happen each night.

## Gates, and the names of their keepers

The Amduat was not the only map. A second composition, first attested in
the tomb of King Horemheb, covers the same night journey and is known as
the Book of Gates. Budge preferred that name because a series of gates
is what most distinguishes it.[^budge-book][^stadler]

Martin Stadler points to one thing the Book of Gates contains: the
judgment hall of Osiris. In this book the judging of
the dead is tied to the sun's journey. The sun god himself is vindicated
on the way, and that provides a model for the dead person.[^stadler]

Gates need keepers. Lucarelli describes a whole class of beings whose work
is to guard the gates and regions of the realm of the dead. They are
aggressive because they are protecting their own ground, and they can be
well disposed to anyone who knows their names and how to face them. That
is why the texts describe them so precisely, often with an animal's head
and a knife in the hand: the dead person has to recognise
them.[^lucarelli]

The last chapter showed the same rule at the door of the judgment hall.
The dead person begins by telling the god that he knows his name, and the
names of the forty-two who sit with him. In the Duat, to know a name is
to have the means of passing.

This is the practical reason for carrying the book. Budge quotes the
promise attached to the sixth hour: whoever knows this path, and the
names and forms of those on it, will share in the offerings.[^budge-six]

## The heart on the scale

The second journey ends in a hall.

The Met's record identifies the figures. Two figures of Ma'at
lead the dead person into the Hall of the Two Truths. Forty-two judges
sit in two rows above, each with a feather on his head and a knife in his
hand. In the centre Anubis, the jackal-headed god of embalming, weighs the
heart against a small squatting figure of Ma'at. Thoth, with his scribe's
equipment, reports the result to Osiris, who sits in a kiosk at the
left.[^judgment-met]

Stadler sets out how this picture came about. Threats of a judgment
before the Great God appear in tombs as early as the fourth dynasty. The
scale itself is already present in the Coffin Texts. From the Middle
Kingdom the dead were thought to pass a series of trials modelled on the
vindication of Osiris, which the third chapter described. But the scene
as we see it here, with the declaration of innocence that belongs to it,
is not attested before the New Kingdom.[^stadler]

Why a heart? The Met's note on another object answers. For the Egyptians
the heart was the centre not only of life but of thinking, memory and
moral values.[^hatnefer] It was the one witness that had been present for
everything.

That was the difficulty. The heart could testify.

A heart scarab was placed on the mummy to deal with this. The base of
Hatnefer's is engraved with a version of chapter 30 of the Book of the
Dead. In the museum's translation she addresses her own heart. It is not
to rise up against her as a witness, or contend with her in the court, or
oppose her before the keeper of the balance. It is not to invent lies
about her in the presence of the god.[^hatnefer]

The museum states the purpose plainly. The Egyptians feared a bad outcome,
and special amulets were used to make sure of a good one.[^hatnefer]

So the same burial could contain two things that sit awkwardly together.
One is a long list of wrongs the dead person swears not to have done, the
list examined in the last chapter. The other is an amulet instructing the
only witness not to speak against her. Stadler describes chapter 125
itself as a text intended to ensure that its owner passes, and the
judgment as a kind of initiation.[^stadler]

It would be too simple to say the ethics were insincere, and too simple to
say the magic was an afterthought. Both are in the tomb. As in every
chapter of this book, the sources keep two answers.

What a good result sounded like is recorded on the papyrus of Nauny, the
singer of Amun with whom this book began. Anubis reports that her heart
is an accurate witness. Osiris orders that her eyes and her mouth be
given back to her.[^nauny]

## What eats the failures

Look again at the centre of the judgment papyrus. Beside the balance, on
a stand, a beast is waiting with its jaws open.

The Met calls her Ammut, the Devourer, and describes her in this drawing
as a hippopotamus with a crocodile's head. If the scales do not balance,
she eats the heart, and the person dies for ever.[^judgment-met] Lucarelli
gives her usual form, the best known of all Egypt's composite monsters: a
crocodile's head, a lion's body, and the hindquarters of a hippopotamus.
She translates the name as the devourer of the dead.[^lucarelli] Stadler adds what the
eating does. It annihilates.[^stadler]

In this scene, then, the penalty is not a sentence to be served. It is an
end. The person who fails stops existing, which is the opposite of
everything a burial was meant to secure.

The Amduat has its own version, and it is harsher to look at. The name of
the eleventh hour, in Budge's rendering, is the *Mouth of the Circle which
judgeth bodies*. To one side of the sun's path are pits of fire. The
enemies of Ra are in the first. In the others, separately, are the bodies,
the souls, the shadows and the heads of the condemned. Goddesses hack them
apart and supply the fire that burns them, and Horus stands by to direct
the work.[^budge-eleven] In the sixth hour a serpent named *Eater of the
Spirits* has the task of devouring the shadows of the dead.[^budge-six]

Budge thought these pictures might be the distant source of later beliefs
in a fiery hell. He also thought the Egyptians had taken the idea from the
red clouds of dawn.[^budge-eleven] Both suggestions are his own, and are
more than a century old. What the text itself says is narrower. Those in
the pits are burned until they are consumed. Here too the end is
destruction.

Who are the condemned? Budge's account calls them the enemies of Ra and
the damned, and says no more. None of the papyri in this chapter puts its
owner among them. Each was made for
a named person, and each shows that person passing.

## A night of one's own

So we come back to the question of the first page. Why did a book made
for the tombs of kings end up on the bodies of people who were not
kings?

Henettawy was not alone. Another woman from the same tomb, Tiye, a singer
in the cult of Amun-Re, had an Amduat papyrus with very little writing on
it. The museum describes it as a set of abbreviated scenes. Tiye stands
before Osiris, worships the sun god in the form of a ram, and presents
offerings to the inhabitants of the Duat.[^tiye]

Nauny had one too. Her Book of the Dead, the roll described at the start
of this book, lay inside a hollow figure of Osiris. A second and shorter
papyrus had been folded eight times and laid across the knees of her
mummy. It is labelled at one edge as the *Book of What is in the Duat*,
though the museum notes that it looks unlike most copies. It shows forty
wrapped figures. Some are forms of the sun god. Others are labelled as
the Osiris Nauny, *true of voice*, the phrase for someone who has passed
the weighing of the heart.[^nauny]

Janák supplies the idea that connects these burials to the sun. An
ordinary dead person also had a *ba*, which left the corpse by day and
returned to it every night. That bond, he writes, was modelled on the
daily course of the sun, and its counterpart in the heavens was the union
of the sun god's ba with his corpse in the underworld.[^janak]

Each dead person, in other words, was to do nightly what Ra does in the
sixth hour. The Met says as much of Wereshnefer's sarcophagus. Box and lid
together make an image of the journey from death to life that the priest
hoped to complete each day in the company of the sun.[^wereshnefer]

The third chapter said that the dead were given an Osiris of their own.
This one adds that they were given a night of their own as well.

Henettawy's papyrus has one hour on it and an empty space. It is tempting
to think the hour was chosen, since it is the one in which those lying in
the water are promised that their flesh will not decay. The museum says
only that the scribe may have meant to add more.[^henettawy] We do not
know which it was.

Five chapters have not produced a single Egyptian story. They have
produced things that had to be done again: a text recited, a beginning
repeated, a murder repaired, a right upheld, a night crossed. The Amduat
ends with the sun leaving the Duat and turning to bid farewell to its
lord. Budge translates the first words: *Life to thee, O thou who art
over the darkness*.[^budge-twelve]
