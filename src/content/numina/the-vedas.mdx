In the manuscript library of the Bhandarkar Oriental Research Institute at
Pune there are thirty manuscripts of the Rigveda. Twenty-nine are written
on paper, in the Devanagari script. One, from Kashmir, is on birch bark,
in the older Sharada script. In 2007 UNESCO entered the whole group in its
Memory of the World Register.[^bori]

The oldest of the thirty that carries a date is numbered 5/1882-83. It was
written in 1464 CE.[^bori] The hymns it contains are roughly three
thousand years older than that.[^jw-dates]

Nothing has gone missing from the record. This gap is the first thing to
understand about the Vedas. They were composed without writing, and for
about two thousand years they were kept without it. They are still
recited from memory now.

So this book cannot start, as the Egyptian one did, with an object in a
case. It starts with a text that lived in trained voices. This chapter
asks how that was done, how old the text is, and what its oldest hymns
are about. Four powers will keep returning: fire, storm, oath, and a
pressed drink.

One note on tense. The tradition described here was never interrupted,
and where it is alive this chapter speaks of it in the present.

## Heard, not written

The Indian tradition has its own name for texts of this kind. They are
*shruti*, hearing: revealed to the first seers, the rishis, and passed on
by ear. Texts with human authors belong to a second class, *smriti*,
remembrance.[^jw-texts]

Stephanie Jamison and Michael Witzel, whose survey is the main guide in
this chapter, state the historical side of the same fact. Both the
composition and the transmission of the Vedic texts were completely oral
for the whole Vedic period and for a considerable time after it, until at
least about 1000 CE. The texts survive because particular priestly
schools, called *shakhas* or branches, made it their work to preserve
them.[^jw-intro]

How do you keep a long text unchanged without writing it down? The
schools built checks into the recitation itself. Arthur Macdonell
described them in 1900. First the continuous text is broken into separate
words, the *pada* text. Then comes the step text, in which the words are
recited in overlapping pairs: the first with the second, the second with
the third. In the woven text each pair is said forwards, backwards and
forwards again. The most intricate pattern adds a third word: the pair
forwards and backwards, then all three forwards, backwards and forwards.
Macdonell presents each pattern as one more safeguard against any change
to the text.[^macdonell-patha]

UNESCO lists the result as living heritage. Its entry on Vedic chanting
describes practitioners who are taught from childhood, and a recitation
that depends on tonal accents, an exact pronunciation of each sound, and
set combinations of words. It adds a warning. Of more than a thousand
branches of Vedic recitation, thirteen survive, and four schools, in
Maharashtra, Kerala, Karnataka and Odisha, are under imminent
threat.[^unesco-chant]

One small custom shows how the reciters think of their text. Before a
hymn is recited, the name of its poet is spoken. Jamison and Witzel
explain that this keeps his memory alive, and that the daily recitation
is understood as food for the ancient seers.[^jw-exchange]

## Four collections

There are four Vedas. UNESCO's entry gives the plainest summary. The Rig
Veda is an anthology of sacred hymns. The Sama Veda sets hymns from it and
from other sources to music. The Yajur Veda holds the prayers and formulas
that priests use in sacrifice. The Atharva Veda holds incantations and
spells.[^unesco-chant]

Each Veda in turn has layers, which scholars list in rough order of age.
The collection of verses comes first. After it come prose works that
explain the ritual, then more secret speculation on it, and finally the
Upanishads, which carry that speculation furthest. All of these count as
shruti.[^jw-texts]

The oldest of the four is the Rigveda, and the rest of this chapter stays
with it. Macdonell counts 1,028 hymns, arranged in ten books. Six of the
ten, the second to the seventh, are each the work of one family of poets,
and were probably handed down inside those families before anyone
combined them. The ninth book is different in kind: every hymn in it is
addressed to a single power, Soma.[^macdonell-books]

Jamison and Witzel add that the hymns were composed by a number of bards
and bardic families over several hundred years at the least, and that the
text has reached us through essentially one school, that of a scholar
named Shakalya. It was he who made the word-by-word analysis on which the
recitation patterns rest.[^jw-rv]

The manuscripts at Pune belong to the last stage of this history.
Sixteen of the thirty contain the commentary of Sayana, written in the
fourteenth century, and five contain the word-by-word text. When Max
Müller printed the Rigveda with that commentary in the nineteenth
century, one of the manuscripts he used was from this
collection.[^bori][^jw-rv]

## How old?

There are two answers, and they are not answers to the same question.

For the tradition, the hymns have no date of composition, because they
were not composed. They were heard.[^jw-texts]

For historians, the hymns were made by poets, and the problem is that
nothing fixes when. Jamison and Witzel say it is difficult to set even
imprecise limits. They suggest 1500 to 500 BCE as convenient outer dates
for the whole Vedic period. For the Rigveda itself they reason from what
the text knows. It does not know the cities of the Indus civilisation,
which had gone by about 1900 BCE, and mentions only ruins. Iron, which
reached north India around 1100 BCE, is first mentioned in a later Veda.
So the Rigveda falls somewhere in the long stretch between.[^jw-dates]

One document from outside India helps. An agreement between the Hittites
and the kingdom of Mitanni, made about 1380 BCE far to the west, names
Varuna, Mitra, Indra and the Nasatyas, all of them gods of the
Rigveda.[^jw-dates]

Other sources round the figure differently. UNESCO says the Vedas were
composed over 3,500 years ago; the Met, on one of its labels, says about
1500 BCE.[^unesco-chant][^surya] None of these is a measurement. They are
estimates from language and from silence.

The hymns also have relatives. Jamison and Witzel point to the oldest
texts of Zoroastrian Iran, written in a closely related language. There
too a fire stands at the centre of worship, the most valued offering is an
invigorating drink, and one of the gods has the same name: Mitra in the
Veda, Mithra in Iran.[^jw-iran]

## A religion without pictures

Here is the difficulty for a book that likes to begin with objects.
Jamison and Witzel put it bluntly. Our knowledge of Vedic religion is
entirely verbal. Nothing is known of how it looked, if it had images at
all beyond the performance of the rites.[^jw-intro]

The three plates in this chapter are therefore late. The earliest was made
more than a thousand years after the hymns it is set beside. Each shows
how a later age gave a body to a god whom the poets had only addressed.

The words themselves are not easy evidence either. A hymn is spoken to a
god who already knows his own deeds, by a poet whose audience knows them
too. So nothing is explained. Jamison and Witzel call the hymns
deliberately allusive and obscure, and quote a scholar's test: take a
famous ode to joy, replace the word *joy*, and ask a stranger to guess
what the poem is about.[^jw-myth]

Nor do the gods form a system. Some are plainly forces of nature, their
names ordinary nouns. Some are abstractions. Some belong to ethics, and
some to the ritual itself. What is striking about the Vedic gods, the
survey says, is the lack of any overarching organisation.[^jw-myth]

With that warning, here are four of them.

## Fire

The Rigveda opens with a hymn to Agni. In Griffith's old translation its
first words are *I laud Agni, the chosen Priest*.[^rv-agni]

Agni is the Sanskrit word for fire, and the god is the fire: above all the
fire of the ritual. Jamison and Witzel make a point about him that is easy
to miss. Agni is one of the few gods who are actually present and visible
on the offering ground.[^jw-gods] The worshipper does not imagine him. He
is burning in front of the priest.

His work follows from that. Whatever is placed in the fire is carried by
it to the other gods, and the same opening hymn asks him to bring the gods
here in return.[^rv-agni][^jw-exchange] He is the messenger in both
directions. That is why so much of the collection is addressed to him:
about two hundred hymns, by Macdonell's count, more than to any god but
one.[^macdonell-gods]

## Storm

The god with the most hymns is Indra: at least two hundred and fifty,
nearly a quarter of the collection.[^macdonell-gods]

Jamison and Witzel call him the most vividly realised of the Vedic gods.
He is a warrior, and unlike the gods of the last section he has stories.
The one told most often is his fight with Vritra.[^jw-gods][^jw-indra]

A hymn in the first book tells it. Indra drinks soma. He takes up the
thunderbolt that the craftsman god has made for him. He strikes Vritra,
whom Griffith calls *the Dragon* and who lies on the mountain holding back
the waters. The enemy falls like a tree to the axe, and the rivers run
over his body to the sea.[^rv-indra]

What is this story about? Scholars once read it as weather: the storm
breaks, the clouds open, the rain comes. Jamison and Witzel report that
more recent studies treat it as an act of creation, in which the world's
waters are set free for the first time.[^jw-indra] Both readings are
still in print, and the hymn itself does not settle the matter.

Indra is not simply admirable. The survey describes him as the hero and
also the villain of many episodes, in which he shows overwhelming strength
and, less creditably, a talent for trickery.[^jw-indra]

The Met's note on this figure lists the parts Indra came to play in
Buddhist tradition: a witness to the birth of the Buddha, an attendant and
protector, the ruler of a heaven.[^indra-met] The slayer of Vritra had
become part of another religion's court. It is a first sign of something
this book will meet often. Gods here are rarely discarded. They are given
new work.

## Oath

A third group of gods has almost no stories at all.

Varuna and his constant partner Mitra belong to a family called the
Adityas. Jamison and Witzel describe Varuna as a stern but just king, and
they describe the whole group as figures: gods with roles and qualities,
and very little narrative.[^jw-gods][^jw-adityas]

What they stand for has been argued over for more than a century, and the
argument is instructive. Scholars of Macdonell's generation assumed that
every god must be a piece of nature, and made Mitra the sun and Varuna the
sky, or the moon. In 1907 Antoine Meillet proposed something else. Mitra,
he said, is the god of the contract: not a natural force but a social one.
Later scholars followed him in different directions. One saw Varuna as the
god of oaths, another as true speech. A study of 1981 redefined the three
chief Adityas as the gods of alliances, of commandments and of
customs: three kinds of bond that hold people together.[^jw-adityas]

The hymns show what such a god was like to live under. In one, a poet
named Vasishtha believes that Varuna is angry with him and does not know
why. He asks the wise, and they only confirm it. He asks to be freed from
his own offences and from those of his fathers, as a calf is loosed from
its cord. And he offers an excuse that reads very humanly in Griffith's
version. It was not his own will that betrayed him, but *thoughtlessness*,
*wine, dice, or anger*.[^rv-varuna]

Behind these gods stands a word that will not translate. It is written
*rita* in plain letters. Jamison and Witzel call it very difficult and
controversial. It is commonly rendered as cosmic order, and it has also
been argued to mean simply truth. Their own account is that it is truth
as something active, a force that makes things work: Varuna lets the
rivers flow by it. They compare it to a later and better-known word that
is just as hard to translate, *dharma*.[^jw-rita]

A reader coming from the Egyptian book may hear an echo of Ma'at, which
was also a truth that had to be done. The comparison is this book's own
and is offered with caution. No source cited here makes it.

## The pressed drink

The fourth power is the strangest, because it is at once a god, a plant
and a liquid.

Soma, in the survey's words, is the deified soma drink, and also the plant
it is made from.[^jw-gods] What plant that was is still debated.[^jw-iran]
Like Agni, Soma was present at the rite, in a form that could be seen and
handled.

The whole ninth book of the Rigveda accompanies its preparation. Macdonell
summarises the process the hymns describe. The stalks are pounded with
stones. The juice runs through a strainer of sheep's wool into wooden
vats. It is mixed with water and with milk, and offered to the
gods.[^macdonell-soma] The poets turn each step into images. The ten
fingers that press the stalks become ten sisters. The juice rushing into
the vat becomes a bull among the herd.

The gods need it. Without drinking soma, Indra could not have killed
Vritra.[^jw-gods] And people drink it too. A hymn in the eighth book
speaks in the voice of someone who has: *We have drunk Soma and become
immortal*.[^rv-soma]

There is no plate for this section. The hymns describe a plant and a
drink, not a figure.

All four powers meet in a single act. A fire is lit, the drink is pressed
and poured, hymns are sung, and the gods are asked for something. Jamison
and Witzel name the principle with a formula from the ritual texts: *give
me, I give you*. The hymns are not disinterested praise. Most of them
contain plain requests for the goods of this world, and the offering is
one move in an exchange that is meant to go on for ever.[^jw-exchange]

That exchange also explains why there are so many hymns. The gods, the
poets say more than once, are best pleased by the newest one.[^jw-exchange]

## A hymn that is not sure

Among the last hymns added to the collection is one that asks where
everything came from.[^jw-texts]

It begins before the world. There was then neither the existent nor the
non-existent, no air and no sky; neither death nor anything deathless.
There was darkness hidden in darkness, and one thing that breathed by its
own nature. Desire arose in it, and that was the first seed.[^rv-creation]

Then the poet stops and questions his own account. Who really knows? he
asks. The gods cannot say, since *the Gods are later than this world's
production*. Perhaps the one who looks down on it from the highest heaven
knows. The hymn's final words, in Griffith's translation, are *or perhaps
he knows not*.[^rv-creation]

It is worth pausing on what this is. A hymn inside a scripture guarded as
carefully as this one ends on a doubt, and the doubt was memorised and
handed on with the same care as everything else. Generations of reciters
have kept that last clause exactly, syllable by syllable.

## What changed, and what did not stop

It would be wrong to read the Rigveda as a description of the Hinduism of
temples and images. Jamison and Witzel warn that even the phrase *Vedic
Hinduism* is a contradiction in terms. Vedic religion, they write, is at
least as different from later Hindu religion as the religion of ancient
Israel is from later Christianity. It is its predecessor, with many
surprising continuities.[^jw-intro]

The clearest difference is in who matters. The two great gods of later
Hinduism, Vishnu and Shiva, are present in the Rigveda but not prominent.
Shiva appears under an older name, Rudra.[^jw-gods] Indra, with his two
hundred and fifty hymns, will not keep his place. The next chapter
follows that change.

But the old religion did not end when the centre moved, and this is the
point at which the past tense fails. The survey, written in 1992, reports
that the simpler fire rites were still performed reasonably often in
various parts of India and Nepal, and cites a list of some 550 people who
had maintained the solemn rites over the previous hundred years. The soma
ritual was regularly performed only in certain districts of Andhra, Tamil
Nadu and Kerala. In 1975 one of the rarer and more complicated rites, the
building of a great fire altar, was performed in Kerala and filmed. And in
temples
of south India, reciters went through their school's whole Veda aloud, one
lesson a day.[^jw-recent]

Those reports are now more than thirty years old. UNESCO's entry, quoted
earlier, is the more recent witness, and it is worried: thirteen branches
out of more than a thousand.[^unesco-chant]

Return to the manuscript at Pune, the one dated 1464. In most traditions
the oldest manuscript is the nearest we can come to the original. Here it
is a latecomer. The older copy of the Rigveda is not on paper or birch
bark. It is recited, and it has to be learned again in every generation.
