Before they give Marduk a kingdom, the gods sit down to eat.

They have heard the news about Tiamat. They know that the older gods have
failed to face her. Now a younger god has offered to fight, provided they
give him the right to command them. The gathering described in *Enuma
Elish* has an urgent purpose. Yet there is time for food, beer, and a change
of mood. Frightened gods become a company ready to name a king.[^banquet]

In [Before the world](/numina/mesopotamian/before-the-world), we followed
Marduk onto the battlefield. Stay at the table for a moment. His strength
does not settle everything. Other gods must recognise his claim. Someone
must speak, someone must agree, and the agreement must hold after the
danger has passed.

This chapter follows that problem through several texts. It is not a
single story assembled from their parts. A hymn to Enlil, a poem about
Enki, and a Babylonian account of Marduk each have their own favourite.
Read together, they let us hear different answers to the same question:
who has the right to decide what happens next?

## No fixed seating plan

A list of Mesopotamian gods can look reassuring. Each name gets a task:
sky, wind, water, war. Learn the list and the world seems to fall into
place.

Then the reading starts. Two gods can claim the same power. A goddess
can cross several fields of life. A city can praise its own deity in
terms that seem to leave no room for a superior. The arrangement changes
with the text, the place, and the period. We are reading works produced
across many centuries, not chapters from one ancient handbook.

Even the names require care. An is the Sumerian name usually paired with
the Akkadian Anu. Enki is commonly identified with Ea. Enlil also appears
as Ellil. These pairings help us follow the literature, but they do not
make every account of a god identical.[^names]

Keep four names to hand for this chapter:

| Name | A principal city | What to watch for here |
| --- | --- | --- |
| An / Anu | Uruk | Rank, kingship, and the authority to grant power |
| Enlil / Ellil | Nippur | Binding commands, the ordering of life, and royal authority |
| Enki / Ea | Eridu | Fresh water, skilled knowledge, and the assignment of work |
| Marduk | Babylon | A claim to supreme rule that gathers older powers around it |

These are starting points, not job limits. Nor are the four a permanent
committee with a fixed order of succession.[^cities]

The collective names are no tidier. *Anunna*, with Akkadian forms
*Anunnaku* and *Anunnaki*, can refer to major gods or to the gods of a
particular city. Some later uses connect the name with the underworld.
It does not always describe the same membership. A text's own setting
matters more than a modern chart that assigns every god a permanent
seat.[^anunna]

Calling divinity an office is useful here. It directs our attention to
responsibility and rank. But a god is not only an official. These beings
have tempers, desires, family loyalties, and powers that no human office
could contain. The stories become interesting when those things interfere
with the orderly division of duties.

## Anu and the right to rule

Anu is the sky god, but that description leaves out much of his importance.
He can grant other gods their standing. He belongs to the older generation
whose recognition makes a claim to power legitimate. His authority remains
valuable even in texts where someone else takes the leading role.[^anu]

Watch how Inanna approaches him in the Sumerian poem *Inana and Ebih*.
The mountain Ebih has failed to show her respect. She wants to destroy it.
Before she attacks, she comes to An, makes an offering, and addresses him.
He takes the place of honour in heaven.

Inanna begins by recalling what he has already given her. Her fearsome
status and her weapons rest on his grants. She uses those earlier gifts
to support a new demand. If she possesses such power, how can the mountain
be allowed to ignore it?

An is not persuaded that the attack will succeed. He describes the
mountain's frightening strength and tells her she cannot overcome it.
Inanna attacks anyway. She destroys its forests and breaks its resistance.
The poem celebrates her victory.[^ebih]

This is a difficult scene for anyone hoping to draw a simple chain of
command. An has enough authority that Inanna seeks his hearing. He does
not have the last practical word on what she does. The poem can honour
his position and still let its heroine act against his warning.

It helps to notice what kind of work we are reading. A poem praising
Inanna has reasons to show her exceeding expectations. That is different
from a hymn devoted to Anu's own greatness. The change of emphasis is
part of the evidence, not a mistake we need to repair.

## Enlil gives the command

At Nippur, the great name is Enlil. His temple is the Ekur, the Mountain
House. His power to grant kingship makes his favour matter far beyond
the city itself. Scholars still debate parts of his name and original
character. Reducing him to a god of wind is a poor guide to the range
of his authority.[^enlil]

The Sumerian hymn *Enlil in the E-kur* begins with his commands. They
cannot be overturned. Other gods come before him and obey. His city
is described as a place where wrong behaviour cannot endure.

Then the hymn widens its view. Without Enlil, cities would not be built.
No king would be raised to power. Fish would not reproduce in the
reedbeds. Crops would fail to grow. Human government and the renewal
of life depend on the same divine will.[^ekur]

We often separate these things. A king's appointment belongs to politics;
a field of barley belongs to farming. The hymn puts them together.
It praises the power that allows an inhabited world to continue. Enlil's
command has consequences in a palace and in the mud beside a river.

That is why a decision about a king can be described as part of the
ordering of the world. It is not merely a private favour to one ruler.
The poem presents authority as something that makes other kinds of
life possible.

There is a revealing detail near the end. Ninlil, Enlil's consort, sits
with him and gives counsel. The hymn describes them deciding destinies
together. A short list headed only by Enlil would miss her presence
beside the throne.[^ninlil]

None of this makes the hymn a neutral account of Nippur. A city praised
as free of wrongdoing is an ideal city. It tells us what the poet wants
Enlil's home to mean, not that its human streets were without theft or
cruelty. Praise is evidence for an ambition. It is not a report proving
that the ambition was met.

## Enki puts the world to work

Enki lives in the Abzu, the fresh waters beneath the earth. As Ea, he is
closely linked with incantations and the knowledge used in healing and
protection. His skill is practical. He knows how to make something work,
how to remove a danger, and sometimes how to escape another god's
plan.[^enki]

In *Enki and the world order*, the world needs more than a roof of sky
and a stretch of land. It needs water in the right places, grain in the
fields, and people able to build houses. The poem follows Enki as he
sets activities in order and places gods in charge of them.

Enbilulu receives responsibility for waterways. Enkimdu is assigned
ditches and dykes. Grain comes under Ashnan. Kulla is associated with
brickmaking; Mushdama with building. These tasks are close enough to
touch, but they are not the same. Making bricks does not by itself
produce a house that stands.[^assignments]

The poem gives us a way to think about a field. Its crop depends on
several acts of care, each with a divine name attached. Water must
arrive. The boundaries must be known. Someone must plough. The grain
must grow. Abundance is shared work before it becomes something a
person can eat.

Enki's authority is also shared, though he has plenty to say about his
own greatness. Early in the poem he explains that Enlil placed powers
in his hands. He speaks of deciding destinies alongside Enlil and
overseeing justice beside An. His wide activity does not require the
other great gods to disappear.[^commission]

This is a different account from Marduk cutting up Tiamat. Its interest
is in how a working world is arranged. We lose that difference if we
make all creation stories answer only the question of where matter
came from. Here, a building plot without a builder would still be
unfinished creation.

The details also keep the poem from becoming an abstract account of
power. There are animals, boats, tools, and provisions for the gods.
The divine order reaches down to the work of making and maintaining
things. That is where people must live with it.

## What a divine decision does

The phrase "decreeing destinies" can sound like a prediction. A god
looks ahead, sees what will happen, and announces it.

Often these texts mean something more active. A deity grants a city
prosperity, gives another god a duty, or raises someone to kingship.
The speech helps establish the condition it names. Enki's appointments
are decisions about what the gods will do. Enlil's favour gives a ruler
the standing to rule.[^decisions]

Our nearest everyday comparison is a judge delivering a verdict or an
authority appointing someone to a post. The words do not just describe
a situation. Spoken by the person entitled to say them, they change it.
This is a comparison, not a claim that divine power was merely human
law with larger consequences.

Now Marduk's demand becomes easier to understand. He wants more than
permission to fight Tiamat. He wants his future decisions to bind the
gods who are listening. The point of the assembly is partly to establish
whose words will count.

Its members are not equal citizens choosing a representative. We should
not imagine ballot papers or a recorded majority. *Enuma Elish* narrates
agreement, acclamation, and the granting of kingship. To call this a
"vote" can be convenient shorthand, but it adds a procedure the poem
does not describe.[^authority]

The resulting problem is quite sharp. If the gods have the standing to
make Marduk their ruler, they already possess authority together. They
use that authority to give him a command that none of them may undo.
The poem lets a king's supremacy grow out of a collective act.

## Marduk takes the chair

Return to the banquet. Marduk is placed before the gods to receive
kingship. They compare his command with Anu's. Anu's standing is the
measure by which the new king's power can be understood.

They test Marduk's speech. In W. G. Lambert's translation, he makes a
constellation vanish and appear again. Then they acclaim him as king
and give him the signs and weapons of rule. His word works before
his weapon is put to its great test.[^test]

After the battle and the building of Babylon, the gods gather again.
The epic makes room for further acts of recognition. Anu honours
Marduk's bow. The gods swear an oath. They confirm his kingship and
expect him to care for their sanctuaries and offerings.[^confirmation]

That last expectation matters. Supreme rule comes with the duty to
keep the divine community provided for. The older gods have not simply
ceased to exist. They have places, needs, and a claim on the new order.

The final names given to Marduk widen his role. The poem does not leave
him as a specialist in killing one enemy. It makes his victory support
a much larger claim over the activities of the gods. Near the end,
Ea gives him his own name and responsibilities.[^names-of-marduk]

In historical terms, Marduk's rise belongs to the growing importance
of Babylon. His earliest role is uncertain. He was not always the
obvious head of the pantheon, and his later greatness should not be
read backwards into every earlier text.[^marduk]

Nor does a poem praising his supremacy tell us that devotion to other
gods ended. Its argument depends on their recognition. Their names
still carry weight; their acknowledgement helps make his claim powerful.

The assembly is therefore more than scenery around a hero. Remove it,
and we have a strong god who wins a fight. Keep it, and we can see how
the poem turns a victory into a right to govern.

## A house for a god

In these stories, rank needs a place to live.

The Ekur belongs to Enlil at Nippur. Enki's home is tied to Eridu and
the Abzu. Marduk's great sanctuary is Esagil at Babylon. A god's city
gives divine authority an address. It is a place where human beings
can bring offerings and where a ruler can make a public claim to
serve the deity.[^houses]

Temples were also places of care. Cult images were dressed and supplied
with food and drink. They were treated as bearers of divine presence,
not as decorative pictures. The work of worship involved materials and
people, not only belief.[^care]

This gives the creation epic's ending a practical force. When Marduk
is charged with maintaining the other gods' sanctuaries, the poem is
talking about something its audience could recognise. Someone must
provide what a divine household requires. The feast in heaven has
consequences for work on earth.

It would be too simple to dismiss all this as a disguise for politics.
The stories do help justify authority. They also express hopes about
food, security, successful work, and a world that will continue tomorrow.
The same passage can support a ruler's claim and speak to a farmer's
fear that the water will fail.

The poems do not separate those concerns as neatly as we might wish.
Reading them well requires us to keep both in view.

## The person waiting outside

So far, most of the speakers have been gods. Human beings generally
meet the order they establish from the other side: waiting for a
favourable decision, making an offering, or asking for help.

The seal shown earlier gives that distance a shape. A worshiper does
not simply take the seated figure's place. A goddess leads him forward.
The scene is arranged around an approach and a difference in rank.
The museum identifies the recipient as a deified king, which makes
this an especially clear meeting of royal and sacred authority.[^seal]

Another object offers a quieter view. The small figure below was found
at Tell Asmar, ancient Eshnunna. His hands are joined. His eyes remain
open. He represents a worshiper, not one of the great gods whose names
we have been following.

The Met describes this statue as a continuing presence in prayer on
behalf of its owner. We do not know that person's private thoughts.
The posture cannot tell us precisely what was asked. It does show the
care given to remaining before the divine presence.[^worshiper]

A chapter about power needs that small figure. The assembly decides
destinies, but most people do not sit in it. They must find ways to
be heard by those who do.

## Someone has been left out

There is one more voice to hear before we leave Enki's ordered world.

Near the end of *Enki and the world order*, Inanna complains. Other
goddesses have received their duties. The birth goddess has the
instruments of her work. Nisaba has the measuring tools and the task
of writing. Inanna wants to know where her own responsibilities are.
She brings the complaint directly to Enki.[^inanna-claim]

He does not answer by saying she should be content without a place.
He insists that she already has powers, and begins to describe them.
His response ranges through dress and weaving to the violence of
battle. The ending is damaged, so we cannot turn it into a perfectly
preserved settlement between the two.[^inanna-answer]

Even so, her complaint changes the sound of the poem. We have watched
Enki distribute tasks with great confidence. Now someone challenges
the distribution. The orderly list has not satisfied everyone whose
life it is meant to arrange.

Notice, too, the terms of the protest. Inanna asks for a function. She
wants her power acknowledged through something she has the right to
do. The office is not empty ceremony. It is a recognised share in
the running of the world.

We will meet her at greater length in the next chapter. For now, leave
her here, before Enki, refusing to let the list close without her name.
