This is the picture many people carry of the Hindu gods: a creator, a
preserver and a destroyer. It is a real picture, and this chapter starts
from it. But it misleads in three ways. One of the three, Brahma, has
almost no temples of his own.[^britannica-trimurti] The other two are
each far more to their worshippers than a single task. And the deity who
would complete most Hindus' own short list is not in the row at all. She
is the Goddess.[^dehejia-hinduism]

So the title of this chapter names a different three: Vishnu, Shiva, and
Devi, which is simply the word for goddess. The last chapter described a
religion of fire and hymns in which none of them stood at the centre.
This one asks how the centre moved, and what happened to the gods who
had been there first.

The chapter has four pages. This page is the map. Each of the three has
a page of their own: [Vishnu](/numina/hindu/vishnu),
[Shiva](/numina/hindu/shiva) and [Devi](/numina/hindu/devi).

## Where they stood in the Veda

Start by counting. The Rigveda has 1,028 hymns, and Arthur Macdonell
tallied how many are addressed to each god. Indra has at least 250. Agni
has about 200, and Soma more than 100.[^macdonell-counts]

Then look for the gods of this chapter. Vishnu, Macdonell says, is by
this measure a deity of the fourth rank, invoked less often than three
minor solar gods, one of whom has about eight hymns. Rudra, the earlier
form of Shiva, has three or four. As for goddesses, the only one who
receives whole hymns with any frequency is Ushas, the dawn, with about
twenty.[^macdonell-counts]

They are not absent, and what the hymns say of them matters for
everything later. Vishnu is the god who takes three strides across the
whole universe. Rudra is feared: an archer whose arrows the poets dread,
and who is also begged for his medicines. And there is one remarkable hymn
in which Speech herself, a goddess, declares that she upholds all the
other gods.[^rv-three] Each of these is taken up on its own page.

Stephanie Jamison and Michael Witzel, the guides of the last chapter,
state the position carefully. The two great gods of later Hinduism are
not at all prominent in the Veda, though they do appear there. How they
rose is, in their words, rather controversial.[^jw-gods]

## When the centre moved

No text announces the change. It has to be read from dates.

Vidya Dehejia gives the broad one. Hinduism in a form comparable to its
present-day expression, she writes, emerged at about the start of the
common era, with an emphasis on the supremacy of the god Vishnu, the god
Shiva, and the goddess Shakti, whose name means power. She describes two
strands that came together. One runs back to the Vedas. The other drew
on beliefs among other peoples of the subcontinent, especially faith in
the power of a mother goddess.[^dehejia-hinduism]

Objects narrow it down. In the second century BCE a Greek ambassador
set up a stone pillar in central India as a standard of Garuda, the bird
of Vishnu, and called himself by a title usually taken to mean a devotee
of that god.[^heliodorus] Lakshmi, the goddess of fortune, appears in
sculpture bathed by two elephants from the first century BCE, which
makes her one of the earliest goddesses who can be recognised in
southern Asia.[^lakshmi-met] By the fifth century CE
shafts of stone carved with the face of Shiva were spreading across
north India,[^linga-met] and a three-faced Shiva seven metres high was
being cut into the rock of an island near Mumbai.[^elephanta] In about
the same period a poem was composed in which the Goddess defeats a demon
whom the other gods could not.[^pargiter-date]

## From the fire to the image

The change was not only in which gods mattered. It was in what worship
was.

The religion of the last chapter needed no building and, as far as
anyone can tell, no image. Its gods were called to a fire.[^jw-intro] The
religion of this chapter is full of images, and it houses them. Dehejia
describes the Hindu temple as dedicated to the glory of a deity whose
image stands in the inner sanctum, with other aspects of the same deity
carved in niches on the outer walls: the incarnations of Vishnu on a
temple of Vishnu, the feats of Shiva on a temple of Shiva, the battles
of the Goddess on hers.[^dehejia-hinduism]

Images need to be told apart, and a whole vocabulary grew up to do it.
Each deity has things held in the hands, and an animal to ride. Vishnu
carries a discus and a conch and rides the great bird Garuda. Shiva
carries a trident and rides a bull. The Goddess rides a
lion.[^dehejia-recognizing] The pages that follow read these signs one
by one.

The texts changed their idea of an offering too. In the Bhagavad Gita,
the god Krishna, whom his worshippers know as Vishnu, says that he is
*the enjoyer as well as the lord of all sacrifices*. Then he lowers the
price of one. Anyone who offers him, with devotion, a *leaf, flower,
fruit, water*, will find it accepted.[^gita] No priest is required, and
no costly ritual. The word for that devotion is *bhakti*.

## Three in a row

Now return to the row of three gods. The idea behind it has a name,
*trimurti*, three forms, and a classic statement. It comes from the
Vishnu Purana, in H. H. Wilson's translation of 1840:

*Thus the one only god ... takes the designation of Brahmá, Vishńu, and
Śiva, accordingly as he creates, preserves, or destroys.*[^vp-trimurti]

Read the sentence again and notice who the one only god is. This is the
Purana of Vishnu. The verses just before it say that it is Vishnu who
becomes Brahma in order to create, and Vishnu who takes the form of
Rudra to swallow the universe at the end.[^vp-trimurti] The three are not
partners here. They are three roles of one god, and that god is the
book's own.

The other side did the same. At Elephanta, the island near Mumbai, the
entrance to the main cave is dominated by a bust with three faces. It
looks like a trimurti. UNESCO's description calls it Sadashiva and
explains that the three faces are three aspects of Shiva: creator,
preserver and destroyer are all him.[^elephanta] The stone from Kashmir
at the head of this page goes furthest of all. It gives three faces to
each of the three.[^triad]

So the formula of three gods was never neutral ground. The Encyclopaedia
Britannica sums up the scholarly view: the doctrine was an attempt to
reconcile different approaches to the divine, it was known by the fourth
or fifth century CE, and it passes over the fact that Vishnu is not
merely a preserver and Shiva not merely a destroyer. It also notes how
unequal the three are. Vishnu and Shiva are worshipped everywhere. Very
few temples are dedicated to Brahma.[^britannica-trimurti]

Sculptors found another way to say that two great gods are one.

Hari is a name of Vishnu and Hara a name of Shiva. In a Harihara image
the body is split down the middle, Shiva on the right and Vishnu on the
left. In Indian examples, the Met notes, the two halves are given
different casts of face. In this Khmer one the face is single, and the
division shows only in the headdress and in half of an
eye.[^harihara-met]

The same was done with Shiva and the Goddess, in a figure that is male
on one side and female on the other.[^ardhanarishvara] These images do
not explain a doctrine. They show two deities sharing one body, which is
an old way of saying that the two are not rivals.

Dehejia offers a modern Hindu image for the same thought. One may view
the Infinite, she writes, as a diamond of innumerable facets. One facet
or another draws each believer, and to worship that facet is not to deny
the rest.[^dehejia-hinduism]

## Each one is the whole

There is a harder version of the same idea, and the texts state it
without embarrassment. Each tradition says that its own deity is not one
of the three but all of everything.

We have heard the Vishnu Purana. Here are the other two, both in old
public-domain translations.

An Upanishad devoted to Shiva under his older name: *there is one Rudra
only, they do not allow a second*.[^svet]

The Goddess, answering a demon who jeers that she fights with the help
of others: *Alone verily am I in the world here; what other goddess is
there besides me?*[^dm-alone]

These three sentences cannot all be true in the way a census is true.
They were not meant that way. Those who follow Vishnu are called
Vaishnavas, those who follow Shiva are Shaivas, and those who follow the
Goddess as supreme power, *shakti*, are Shaktas. Each tradition has room
for the others' gods, as forms or servants or kin of its own. The
introduction to this book put it in one phrase: the same god is many
gods.

## What became of the old gods

The subtitle of this chapter promises an answer to one more question.
Indra had a quarter of the Rigveda. Where did he go?

He was not thrown out. None of them were. The most visible answer is
that they were given posts.

The gods of the Veda became the guardians of the directions, the
*lokapalas*. In the Hindu list Indra governs the east, Yama the south,
Varuna the west, and Kubera the north.[^lokapala] The slayer of Vritra
still holds his thunderbolt, and rides an elephant, but his task is to
stand at one side of the world and keep watch. Buddhists had guardians
of the directions too, and a Buddhist set cast in Sri Lanka kept Indra
in the east.[^regent-met]

Others were absorbed into the sky. Soma, the pressed drink of the last
chapter, became in later literature a regular name of the moon, which
the gods are said to drink until it wanes.[^macdonell-soma] Agni did not
need to be moved, because he was still the fire. An iconography manual,
Dehejia remarks, lets a viewer pick him out on a temple wall by his
mount, the ram.[^dehejia-recognizing]

And they were kept as witnesses to the greatness of the new centre.
Three stories do this, and each is told on a later page. In an
Upanishad, Agni and the wind god boast of their strength and then fail
to burn or lift a single straw, and it is a goddess, Uma, who explains
to Indra what power they had met.[^kena] In the story of Daksha's
sacrifice, the gods attend a great Vedic rite to which Shiva has not
been invited, and see it wrecked. In the poem of the Goddess, the gods
are beaten and driven from heaven, and their only remedy is to pour
their own energies together and hand over their weapons: Indra's
thunderbolt, Agni's spear, Varuna's conch.[^dm-weapons]

In every one of these the old gods survive. They are present, named and
respected. They are simply no longer enough.

One thing was kept more carefully than any god, and that was the Veda
itself. The Vishnu Purana identifies Vishnu with the four Vedas by
name.[^vp-vedas] The claim is typical. The new traditions did not
present themselves as a replacement for the old scripture. They
presented themselves as what it had meant all along.

## Reading the next three pages

Three things are worth holding on to.

First, the evidence changes kind. The last chapter had words and no
pictures. From here on there are objects that can be dated, and the
plates begin to carry real weight.

Second, the sources are partisan, and say so. A Purana of Vishnu will
make Shiva a form of Vishnu. A hymn to the Goddess will make both of
them her instruments. This book reports each in its own voice and does
not rule between them.

Third, the translations quoted are old. Wilson worked in 1840 and the
others between 1879 and 1904. They are used because they are in the
public domain, and some carry the prejudices of their time, which the
notes point out.

Look once more at the stone from Kashmir. Three gods stand in a row, and
each has been given the faces of the others' powers. The sculptor could
not show them as simply three. And the one who is missing from the row
has a page to herself.
