At Besnagar in central India, where two rivers meet, a sandstone pillar
six and a half metres tall still stands in the open. It was set up in the
second century BCE. When archaeologists examined it in 1877 its lower part
was hidden under thick layers of red pigment, a sign that it had long
been an object of local devotion. Under the pigment were two
inscriptions.[^heliodorus]

The first names the man who raised the pillar. He was Heliodorus, an
ambassador sent by a Greek king of the north-west to the court of an
Indian one. It calls the pillar a Garuda standard, after the bird that
carries Vishnu. And it calls Heliodorus a *Bhagavata*, a word usually
taken to mean a devotee of Vishnu.[^heliodorus]

That reading is not quite certain. The encyclopaedia that describes the
pillar notes that followers of the Buddha used a similar title, and some
scholars have argued the word could mean that.[^heliodorus] But the bird
on the pillar and the remains of a Vishnu shrine nearby point the usual
way. If they are right, one of the earliest dated witnesses to the
worship of Vishnu is a foreigner.

People in the village call the pillar Khambh Baba, the pillar saint, and
it is still occasionally worshipped.[^heliodorus]

## Three steps

Vishnu is in the Rigveda, but only just. Arthur Macdonell ranks him among
the deities of the fourth rank, with fewer hymns than three other gods
of the sun.[^macdonell-vishnu]

What the hymns do say about him is always the same thing. In Griffith's
translation, he is the one who measured out the regions of the earth,
*thrice setting down his footstep, widely striding*. All living creatures
have their home inside those three paces. He alone holds up the
threefold world. And his third and highest step is a place people long
to reach, because a spring of honey is there.[^rv-154]

What are the three steps? Macdonell, writing in 1900, was sure. They are
the course of the sun through the three divisions of the universe, and
Vishnu was at first the sun in motion.[^macdonell-vishnu] The modern
survey used in the last chapter warns against exactly this habit of his
generation, which felt obliged to end every discussion of a god by
declaring that he must be the sun or the sky.[^jw-nature] The hymn
itself says only that he strode, and that the strides were for the good
of those who live within them.

One thing in Macdonell's account does matter for what came next. Later
Vedic texts tell a story in which Vishnu takes the form of a dwarf to win
the earth back from the demons, and covers it in three strides. Macdonell
sees in this the root of the later teaching that Vishnu descends to
earth in one form after another for the good of the
world.[^macdonell-vishnu] Those descents have a chapter of their own
further on.

## The one who lies on the water

By the time of the Puranas the strider has become the ground of
everything.

The Vishnu Purana describes what happens at the end of each immense age
of the world. The universe is swallowed up and becomes a single ocean.
On that ocean, in Wilson's translation, the Supreme *reposes upon his
mighty serpent couch amidst the deep*. After a time he wakes, and the
world is made again.[^vp-sleep]

This is the other great picture of Vishnu, and it is the opposite of the
first. In the Veda he is all motion, three steps and done. In the Purana
he is utterly still, asleep on a serpent, while everything that will
ever exist waits inside him.

## Reading his hands

How do you know that a figure is Vishnu? Vidya Dehejia gives the short
rule. He holds a war discus and a conch-shell trumpet. He wears a tall
crown and rich jewellery. And unlike most Indian gods, who stand with a
sway, he stands perfectly straight.[^dehejia]

The Met describes this small bronze as Vishnu displaying the symbols of
his authority as a divine monarch.[^pallava-met] That is the plain
meaning of the objects in his hands. They are a king's weapons and a
king's trumpet.

The Vishnu Purana gives them a second meaning. A pupil asks how Vishnu
can be said to carry the whole world, and the teacher answers that every
ornament and weapon on the god's body is a part of the universe in
another form.[^vp-emblems]

| What he bears | What the Vishnu Purana says it is |
| --- | --- |
| The jewel he wears | the pure soul of the world |
| The mace | intellect |
| The conch and the bow | the sense of self, in its two divisions |
| The discus | the mind, whose thoughts fly faster than the wind |
| The arrows | the faculties of action and of perception |
| The sword | wisdom, sometimes hidden in the scabbard of ignorance |

The table follows Wilson's wording closely, and Wilson's terms for the
parts of the mind are those of 1840.[^vp-emblems] The point survives the
translation. A worshipper looking at the image is meant to see not a
warrior with his kit, but everything that exists, arranged on one body.

The conch has a life outside his hand as well.[^conch-met]

## Lakshmi

Vishnu is seldom alone. Dehejia notes that he is often accompanied by his
consort Lakshmi, the goddess of fortune.[^dehejia]

The Vishnu Purana tells how she came to him. The gods and their rivals
churn the ocean of milk for the drink of immortality, and wonderful
things rise out of it one after another. Then, in Wilson's words, *seated
on a full-blown lotus*, the goddess rises from the waves. The elephants
of the sky pour water over her from golden vases. Bathed and adorned,
and with all the gods watching, she goes to Vishnu and rests upon his
breast.[^vp-lakshmi]

The scene with the elephants is one of the oldest in Indian art. Lakshmi
bathed by two elephants appears in sculpture from the first century BCE,
long before any surviving copy of this text.[^lakshmi-met] She is treated
more fully on the page for Devi, because she is also a goddess in her own
right.

The plaque also shows the last of his usual companions. Every major
deity has an animal that carries them, and Vishnu's is Garuda, a great
bird like an eagle.[^dehejia] It is Garuda's name that Heliodorus gave
to his pillar.

## The form that contains everything

The most ambitious images of Vishnu try to show the claim of the Purana
directly: that all forms are his.

*Vishvarupa* means having all forms. In this small carving from Kashmir
the sculptor gives Vishnu a lion's head on one side and a boar's on the
other, which are two of the shapes he takes in his descents, and fills
the halo behind him with more beings than can be counted at this
size.[^vishvarupa-met]

The text says the same in words. Vishnu, the Purana declares, exists in
manifold forms, as gods, men and animals; his shape is all visible
things, and he is also without shape or form.[^vp-emblems] The
contradiction is deliberate. An image with ten arms and three faces is
one way of drawing something that has every form and none.

## A god for a king, and for anyone

Two kinds of people appear around Vishnu in the evidence on this page.

One is the ruler. The bronze from Tamil Nadu shows him as a divine
monarch, and the museum ties it to the reigns of particular
kings.[^pallava-met] A god whose business is to hold the world steady is
a natural patron for someone whose business is to hold a kingdom steady.

The other is anyone at all. The pillar at Besnagar was raised by a
foreign envoy. And in the Bhagavad Gita, the best known of the texts in
which Vishnu speaks, he sets the cost of approaching him as low as it can
go. In Telang's translation: *Whoever with devotion offers me leaf,
flower, fruit, water*, he accepts it.[^gita]

Compare that with the last chapter. The Vedic rites described there
grew ever more elaborate, expensive and specialised.[^jw-ritual] This
asks for a leaf and an honest heart. The older rites did not disappear,
but the weight of the religion had moved to something every person
could do.

## What is kept for later

This page has left out the stories for which Vishnu is best loved, and
that is on purpose.

Dehejia lists what belongs to him: a theory of ten incarnations, of
which nine are past and the tenth is still to come, and among them the
two most popular, Rama, the prince of the *Ramayana*, and Krishna, the
cowherd and the teacher of the Gita.[^dehejia] The two epics are the
subject of the next chapter, and the idea of descent has the chapter
after that.

What this page has tried to show is the figure who stands behind them. He
appears first as a few lines in the Rigveda about a god who took three
steps, and then as a sleeper on the water with the universe inside him.
The pillar at Besnagar has stood for some twenty-one centuries, and it
is still, now and then, worshipped.
