Apsu cannot sleep.

The younger gods are making too much noise. They move about, disturb the
waters, and give their elders no peace. Apsu proposes a solution. He will
destroy them. The world has not yet taken its familiar shape, but the wish
to silence the next generation is already here.

Tiamat objects. These are their own children. Their behaviour may be hard
to bear, but that is no reason to kill them.[^first-quarrel]

Remember her objection. By the middle of the poem, Tiamat will be the enemy
whom Marduk must defeat. If we begin by calling her an evil dragon, we miss
the mother who first tries to keep the family alive.

The poem is *Enuma Elish*, an Akkadian title taken from its opening words,
usually rendered as "When on high". It unfolds across seven tablets. This
chapter retells its ancient story in modern prose, then asks what the story
does with power, work, and the city of Babylon. It is a reading of one
Babylonian poem, not a single account of what everyone in Mesopotamia
believed.[^text]

## Two waters, before the shore

Go back a little, before the quarrel. The opening gives us a world without
a named heaven or earth. There is no patch of marsh to stand in, no reedbed
at the edge of a river. There are waters, mixed together.

They have names. Apsu is the fresh water, Tiamat the salt sea. They are also
the beings from whom the gods descend. In this poem, water can be a parent.
A sea can listen, become angry, and answer another voice.[^waters]

This is not creation out of nothing. Something already exists, and life
begins within it. The poem does not explain who made the first waters. Its
interest lies in what comes from them, and who will rule what follows.

Lahmu and Lahamu appear first. Anshar and Kishar follow. Then comes Anu,
and after him Ea, the god whose skill will save the younger generation.
These names slow the opening down. The gods arrive through generations,
rather than stepping onto the page as an assembled court. Marduk is not
there at the start.[^opening]

The title of our chapter needs that small correction. There is already a
world of relationships before there is a world of settled places. Parents
have children. Children grow stronger. Someone who once had no rival now
has to share the waters.

The trouble starts there.

## The first death

Apsu takes advice from Mummu, his counsellor. Tiamat urges restraint, but
Mummu supports the plan to destroy the younger gods. Apsu welcomes it.
Peace, as he imagines it, means an end to their lives.

The plan reaches its intended victims. They are frightened into silence.
Ea understands the danger and acts before Apsu can carry out the threat.
He casts a spell over the waters and puts Apsu into a deep sleep. Then he
strips him of his power, binds him, and kills him. Mummu is captured.[^ea]

The first killing in the poem does not belong to Tiamat.

That does not make Ea's act unprovoked. Apsu had planned to kill him and
the others. It does mean that the later war grows out of something more
troubling than a good god discovering an evil monster. This family has
already chosen violence as a way to survive.

Ea establishes his dwelling upon the defeated Apsu. There he lives with
his wife, Damkina. The name Apsu now serves as the name of a place as well
as that of the slain father. Ea's home is founded on his victory.

This movement from being to place will return on a much larger scale.
For now, the waters that threatened the younger gods have become a home
for one of them. The poem allows a death to be both an ending and a
foundation. It will ask us to accept that twice.

## A child who plays with storms

Marduk is born to Ea and Damkina in the Apsu. The poem makes him impossible
to overlook. He has four eyes and four ears. Fire comes from his mouth.
His grandfather Anu delights in him and gives him the four winds.

The gift is not harmless. Marduk's winds stir the waters and trouble
Tiamat. The gods who share her unrest complain. Once again, they cannot
sleep.[^birth]

The noise that led Apsu towards murder has not stopped. Ea has gained a
quiet dwelling, but his remarkable son brings a new disturbance to the
household outside it. The poem is preparing its champion while also
showing us why others might dread him.

It is easy to imagine Marduk as a young man with a weapon, simply larger
than the gods around him. His birth scene is stranger. Extra eyes and
ears give him more than ordinary sight and hearing. His body announces
the claim that he will exceed the powers of his elders.

The stone above offers a useful caution. A dragon in Babylonian art is
not automatically Tiamat. This one belongs to the imagery of Marduk and
his son Nabu. It stands among signs of divine authority on a monument
of a kind used to record and protect grants of land.[^dragon]

Tiamat herself resists a fixed picture. The poem gives her a body with
human features, yet also speaks of a tail and treats her as water.
Sophus Helle's survey notes that no Mesopotamian image has been securely
identified as Tiamat. Modern paintings can help us imagine the scene.
They cannot settle what an ancient audience saw in its mind.[^tiamat-image]

## Tiamat takes up arms

The complaining gods remind Tiamat of Apsu's death. They ask why she did
not act while he was being killed. Now, they say, the disturbance has
become unbearable. They press her to fight.

She agrees. The mother who once resisted the destruction of her children
now gathers a force against some of them. Other gods stand on her side.
The battle will divide the family, not set all the gods against a thing
that has no connection to them.

Tiamat makes terrifying creatures for her army. The poem names serpents,
scorpion-men, and other beings whose bodies cross the boundaries between
familiar animals. They bear weapons and inspire terror. Her power to
bring forth life has become a way of making an army.[^army]

She places Qingu, also written Kingu, at its head. He is her chosen
consort and commander. She gives him the Tablet of Destinies and fixes
it to his chest. This object carries authority over the divine order.
Qingu is meant to command more than a group of soldiers.

That detail is easy to lose in a quick retelling. Tiamat's side has a
leader, an army, and a claim to rule. Calling it simply "chaos" hides
the rival order it is trying to establish.

The poem wants us to support Marduk. We can recognise that intention
without flattening everyone who opposes him. Tiamat has become dangerous,
but she has a history. The story told us that history before it armed her.

## A crown before the battle

Ea learns what is coming. He brings the news to Anshar. Attempts to face
Tiamat fail; even the older gods cannot resolve the danger. Marduk offers
to act, but he sets a condition. If he saves them, his command must stand
above theirs. The assembly must grant him the authority to decide
destinies.[^kingship]

He is asking for a crown before he earns the victory.

The gods meet. They eat, drink beer, and agree to his demand. The pause
for a feast can feel odd when an enemy army is waiting, but the gathering
does something the fight alone cannot do. The gods make his rule binding
among themselves. Strength needs their recognition.

They test his word. In the reading followed by W. G. Lambert, a
constellation disappears at Marduk's command and returns when he speaks
again. Older translations describe a garment here. The difference is a
reminder of the work discussed in [The tablets](/numina/mesopotamian/tablets):
the English sentence depends on how the signs are read.[^test]

Having proved that his speech can change what exists, Marduk prepares his
weapons. He takes a bow and arrow, carries a mace, and makes a net. The
winds surround him. His chariot is a storm.

At last he faces Tiamat. He accuses her of turning against her children
and of raising Qingu to a rank that is not his to hold. The speech belongs
to a claimant confronting another claimant. Each side has declared where
authority should lie.

Tiamat answers in fury. Marduk casts his net. When she opens her mouth,
he drives a wind into it, so that she cannot close her lips. The wind
fills her body. He shoots an arrow through her and kills her.

Her allies lose their courage. Marduk captures them and takes the Tablet
of Destinies from Qingu.[^battle]

He has won. The earth still has to be made.

## The body of the world

After the battle, Marduk looks over Tiamat's body and decides what to do
with it. The poem compares his division of her to splitting a fish for
drying. One half becomes the sky. He stretches it into place and sets a
guard over the waters, with orders not to let them escape.[^sky]

The image is practical and terrible. Making a habitable world requires
cutting, stretching, and holding things apart. The sea that gave birth
to the gods has become the material their new king works upon.

In the next tablet, the work continues. Marduk assigns places to the
stars and orders the year into months. The moon is given a course by
which people can reckon days. Creation includes a calendar: the world
must have a dependable pattern, not merely a shape.

Then Tiamat's body becomes a landscape. The Tigris and Euphrates flow
from her eyes. Mountains are heaped upon her breasts. Her waters,
spittle, and bodily parts enter the account of clouds, mist, springs,
and the earth itself. Some of these lines are damaged, and modern
editions mark restored words. The main movement is clear even where
the details are not.[^landscape]

Marduk does not make Tiamat vanish. He makes the world from her.

That gives her defeat an unusual force. She can no longer act as an
independent power, but the gods cannot build their home without her
substance. The enemy remains everywhere as matter. The victory depends
on what has been killed.

The poem also keeps boundaries under watch. Water belongs behind a
barrier. Stars have assigned positions. The moon must follow its course.
Seen this way, Marduk's creation is a continuing rule over things that
could otherwise get out of place. His title is not a reward separate
from his work. Kingship is how the poem explains the world's arrangement.

We should be careful about turning this into a claim about early human
history. A male god's killing of a female ancestor does not, by itself,
prove that the poem records the overthrow of a real society once ruled
by women. That would require other evidence. What the text does show is
how its author makes conquest into the basis of a settled world.

## People are made to work

Human beings arrive late. They do not witness the struggle or help to
settle it. By the time they appear, the gods have already chosen a king.

Marduk proposes their creation so that they can carry the gods' work.
Ea advises that a god must be given up. The assembly is asked who caused
the war. It names Qingu. He is bound and killed, and Ea creates humanity
from his blood, carrying out the plan under Marduk's authority.[^people]

Two deaths now support the human world. Tiamat's body gives it its
material. Qingu's blood makes its workers possible.

The account is not a celebration of human freedom. People receive a
purpose chosen before they exist. Their work will allow the gods to rest.
Yet the material from which they are made is divine. The same passage
that assigns them a lower place also gives them a connection to the
beings they must serve.

Qingu is blamed for the revolt, but the poem does not say that every
human being therefore inherits his personal guilt. That is a further
claim, not a line we should quietly add. Here his blood provides the
means of creation, and the obligation placed upon people is service.

Look back at Apsu. He wanted rest and tried to gain it by destroying the
younger gods. The settlement at the end gives the surviving gods rest
by bringing another kind of being into existence to do their work.
Read together, the two scenes make rest a question of who can require
someone else to bear a burden.

The story has found a lasting answer to the gods' problem. We are the
ones who will live inside that answer.

## A city at the centre

The freed gods offer to build Marduk a sanctuary. He directs them to
build Babylon. They make bricks for a year. In the next year they raise
Esagil, his great temple, and establish shrines. When the work is done,
the gods gather for a feast.[^city]

This is where the creation story has been heading. A reader who stops
at Tiamat's death has stopped before the poem reaches its chosen home.

Babylon is not introduced as one city among many. It becomes the place
where the divine community can meet and rest. The ordering of the
heavens leads towards the ordering of a city. Its temple is given a
place within the structure of the cosmos.

The historical Babylon stood on the Euphrates, in what is now Iraq.
It was not the first Mesopotamian city. Its actual beginnings and its
later rise belong to human history. The poem gives it a different
beginning, one in which its place is established by the gods.[^history]

The cylinder above brings us back to the city built by people. Its
inscription belongs to Nebuchadnezzar II and describes work on Babylon's
defences. The object is small enough to hold. The claims it records
concern an enormous city.[^cylinder]

The poem's date is less secure than the date of this royal inscription.
A common scholarly proposal places its composition in the later second
millennium BCE, around the time of Nebuchadnezzar I, or shortly after.
That is a proposed historical setting, not a date supplied by a named
author. Nebuchadnezzar I and Nebuchadnezzar II are different kings,
separated by centuries.[^dating]

Calling the work political does not mean its religion was a disguise.
Its claim about Babylon is a religious claim. The city's god has saved
the divine family and arranged the world. Worship at his temple belongs,
within the poem's own reasoning, to the order that keeps that world
working. A city can be loved, governed, and understood through the same
story.

## Fifty names, and a voice in the temple

The closing movement gives Marduk fifty names. To a reader waiting for
another fight, this can seem like a long ending after the action has
finished. But naming is part of the action.

The names gather powers and tasks around him. They praise his care for
fields and grain, his wisdom, and his ability to preserve divine life.
Older gods recognise his authority. Ea even allows his own name and
the work attached to it to belong to his son.[^names]

The poem began before heaven and earth had names. It ends by asking its
audience to remember the names of their ruler. Between those points,
speech has granted kingship, commanded a test of creation, and assigned
places in the world. The final list teaches people how to address the
god who now holds those powers together.

The text calls for the names to be taught and passed on. Its intended
life extends beyond a single performance. A father is to instruct his
son; learned people are to discuss their meaning. Knowing the story is
made part of serving its god.

At Babylon, *Enuma Elish* was recited during the spring New Year festival,
before Marduk's statue in Esagil. The ceremonial city had its own routes
and images. Glazed lions such as the one above lined the Processional
Way. They belonged to Ishtar's imagery and helped protect the route
used for divine processions.[^festival]

We do not need to invent a listener's feelings to see what that setting
adds. A poem which gives Marduk kingship was spoken in his sanctuary.
The city praised in the story was the city around the performance.
The telling joined a very distant beginning to a particular place in
the present.

## Other ways to begin

*Enuma Elish* is powerful enough to crowd other accounts out of view.
We should not let it.

The Sumerian *Song of the Hoe* gives a different beginning. Enlil
separates heaven and earth, and the hoe takes a place in the making
of the human world. People emerge from the earth and are assigned
work for the gods. The poem follows the tool into building and
cultivation. There is no need for Marduk's battle to explain these
events.[^hoe]

In *Enki and Ninmah*, gods complain about the labour of digging canals.
Namma brings their distress to Enki. He gives instructions for making
humans from clay with the help of birth goddesses. Work again needs
to be passed on, but this is not the account of Qingu's death.[^ninmah]

These compositions share concerns without telling one continuous story.
If we combine every detail into a single version, we create a new myth
of our own and hide the choices made by the ancient writers. The
differences tell us which god, task, or place each poem wants us to
notice.

For *Enuma Elish*, the place is Babylon and the ruler is Marduk. Its
settled world has a violent past. The poem praises the settlement,
but it does not erase the body from which it was made.

Tiamat has lost her voice. The rivers still flow from her eyes.
