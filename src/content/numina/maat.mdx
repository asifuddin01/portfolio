In 1913 a flake of limestone came out of the Valley of the Kings. It is
about fifteen centimetres across, and someone has drawn on it in ink. A
king in a tall crown stretches out one hand. On his open palm sits a very
small figure with a feather on her head.[^ostracon]

The Met dates the drawing to the reign of Ramesses IX, about 1137 to 1118
BCE, and attributes it to a chief draftsman named Amenhotep. The museum's
title says what the king is doing: he is offering Maat. On the other side
of the flake the same king offers a pot.[^ostracon]

The little figure is a goddess, and her name is also a common noun. The
last chapter ended on that noun. The hymn to Osiris gave the throne to the
one whose it was by right, and we asked what the Egyptians meant by right.

This chapter follows the word through four settings: the temple, where a
king holds it out to a god; the office, where an official claims to have
done it; a story, in which a poor man demands it; and the hall where the
dead are examined on it.

## A word with no single translation

No English word covers Ma'at. The scholars cited in this chapter each
choose differently. Emily Teeter calls her the goddess of truth, and the
principles she stands for a world order established at the beginning of
time. Nikolaos Lazaridis gives justice and cosmic order for the goddess,
and justice, truth and balance for the noun. UCL's translators write
simply *What is Right*.[^teeter-intro][^lazaridis-maat][^ucl-king]

Sandra Lippert adds a warning for anyone who settles on *justice*. Only a
comparatively small part of Ma'at concerns what we would call legal
justice. The word covers much more.[^lippert-law]

We have met it twice already. In the papyrus of Nesmin, the creator says
he laid the foundations of things by Ma'at. And the Coffin Texts equate
the creator's first daughter with Ma'at, as they equate her brother with
life.[^earlier] Right, on this view, is as old as the world and was part
of how it was made.

As a goddess, Ma'at is the daughter of the sun god and an essential aspect
of him. Several gods bear the title *Lord of Ma'at*, among them Ptah as
creator and Thoth.[^lazaridis-maat]

The Met's record for this figure describes her as the goddess of truth and
justice and the embodiment of an ideal world in equilibrium. She wears an
ostrich feather, which signifies her name. Small figures like this one
often sat in front of an ibis, the bird of Thoth, god of writing and
wisdom.[^figure-met]

The word has opposites. The chief one is *isfet*, which Lazaridis glosses
as sin or wrongdoing, and UCL as *What is Wrong*. Close to it is *gereg*,
a lie.[^lazaridis-maat][^ucl-king]

Notice what kind of opposites these are. Each names something a person
does. That will matter, because in text after text Ma'at too is something
done.

## What a king is for

There is no ancient Egyptian treatise on kingship. But UCL points to one
religious composition, first attested in the New Kingdom, that states the
king's functions in Egyptian terms. Ra has placed the king on earth, it
says, for a list of purposes. He is to judge between people and to content
the gods. He is to bring about what is right and to destroy what is wrong.
He is to make offerings to the gods and to the blessed dead.[^ucl-king]

The pair at the centre of the list is Ma'at and isfet. The king does not
simply live under an order that exists. He makes it happen, and he removes
its opposite.

UCL attaches a caution to this text. It calls the definition ethical and
ritual, and for that reason partial. Beneath the phrase about right and
wrong lie the economic and military sides of rule. An inscription of
Amenemhat II, which UCL summarises line by line, records events at court
in sequence. Its entries include offerings to temples. They also include
mining expeditions, foreign delegations arriving with goods, and an army
sent to destroy two foreign places that came back with 1,554
captives.[^ucl-king]

Teeter records the same idea in pictures. On temple gateways the king
strikes down Egypt's enemies, and in such scenes the defeated enemy can be
shown holding a feather of Ma'at as a sign of submission.[^teeter-intro]
Destroying what is wrong could mean that.

Kings put the word into their names. Teeter traces this back to Snefru,
who was *lord of Ma'at* in two of his names. Hatshepsut's
throne name was Maatkare, and Seti I's was Menmaatre. In the Ramesside
period nearly every king took a throne name compounded with
Ma'at.[^teeter-intro][^ucl-names]

The word marked what a king repaired as well as what he was. The
Restoration Stela of Tutankhamun, Teeter notes, says that the king
restored Ma'at in a land that had been in confusion under his
predecessor.[^teeter-intro]

## Offering what is right

Now return to the gesture on the ostracon. Teeter's book is the fullest
study of it, and its findings are more exact than one might expect.

The idea of presenting Ma'at appears in texts as early as the Middle
Kingdom. As a picture it appears first under Thutmose III, in his Festival
Hall at Karnak, in a part of the building dated after his twenty-fourth
year. It is carved most often on the walls of Ramesside temples, and only
rarely in the royal tombs of that age.[^teeter-date]

With few exceptions, the one who offers is the reigning king. Teeter reads
that restriction as the point. The ritual shows the king's willingness to
uphold the order set at the beginning of time, and it works as a sign of
his legitimacy. She observes that he is most often shown in the blue
crown, which carried the same connotation.[^teeter-meaning] The king on
the ostracon wears that crown.[^ostracon]

Why would a god want this offering? The texts call Ma'at the food of the
gods and say the gods live on her. Teeter describes the presentation as a
supreme offering, in which all other offerings are contained. Scenes can
even show the king holding jars while the caption says he is giving
Ma'at.[^teeter-meaning]

The statue in the plate is a case of exactly that. No picture of the
ritual survives from Hatshepsut's reign. But Teeter cites a statue of her
from Deir el-Bahri that holds jars and names her as the one who offers
Ma'at to Amun, and concludes that the rite was performed in her
time.[^teeter-date] The Met reads its statue the same way: by this
offering Hatshepsut affirms that Ma'at is the guiding principle of her
reign.[^hatshepsut]

Under Seti I a new version appears. The king holds out his own name,
written so that it can be read as Ma'at, and the caption calls the name
Ma'at. Teeter takes this as a claim that the king, as king, is imbued with
what he offers.[^teeter-date][^teeter-meaning]

Two details complicate the tidy picture, and both are worth keeping.

The first concerns the reign of Akhenaten. Teeter notes that the
traditional view expected little of this ritual in his time. She found
the reverse. The scene is more common in his early years than in any
other part of the eighteenth dynasty: thirty examples, all but one from
his buildings at Karnak.[^teeter-date]

The second concerns our ostracon. Ramesses IX is one of the very few
Ramesside kings with no Ma'at anywhere in his names.[^teeter-intro] On the
limestone flake he holds her out all the same. A king did not need the
word in his name to be shown performing the act.

## Doing right in an office

A king held out Ma'at to a god. Those below him were told to do Ma'at
for the king, because it is what the king loves.[^teeter-intro]

Lazaridis sets out what kind of evidence we have. No standard moral code
survives from Egypt. Moral principles appear instead in tomb biographies,
in the teachings that scholars call wisdom literature, in funerary books,
and in stories. He calls the result practical ethics: observations and
warnings drawn from working life, with no theory of why the good is
good.[^lazaridis-sources]

The best-known teaching is put in the mouth of Ptahhotep, vizier to King
Isesi. UCL dates its composition to the twelfth dynasty; the two earliest
surviving copies are of the late Middle Kingdom. The old vizier asks
leave to retire and to train his son as his successor.[^ptahhotep]

One of his maxims is addressed to anyone who will lead others. It tells
him to seek every chance to be effective, until his conduct is without
fault. Then it gives the reason. What is right is great and lasting. It
has not been overturned since the time of Osiris. Those who pass over the
laws are punished, and although the small-minded may seize riches, crime
has never brought its gains safely to land.[^ptahhotep]

*Since the time of Osiris.* The teaching dates the rule of right from the
events of the last chapter. Ma'at is what survived the murder and was
upheld in the verdict.

Another maxim calls greed a disease that sets parents and kin against
each other, and says that the man who endures is the one whose guideline
is right. A third says that making right secure is the
life of one's children.[^ptahhotep]

Tomb biographies make the same claims in the first person.

Mentuwoser was a steward under Senwosret I. His stela is dated to that
king's seventeenth year and was a gift from him. In the museum's
translation, Mentuwoser says he was a father to the orphan and a helper of
widows. No one went to sleep hungry in his domain. He kept no one from the
ferry. He cut down no man weaker than himself. He was a proper judge, who
showed no favour to the party able to pay.[^mentuwoser]

The text ends by turning to its audience. Those who hear it, Mentuwoser
says, will have to say that it is true, and their children will tell
their own children: *It is true; there is no lie in it*. The museum's
transliteration shows the two words he chose. *True* is *maa*, the root
of Ma'at. *Lie* is *gereg*.[^mentuwoser]

How far should such a text be believed? Lazaridis notes that biographies
describe their owners in glowing terms, and that all our sources come from
the same small, literate class. They show what that class thought a good
man was. They cannot show how many lived up to it.[^lazaridis-sources]

## No judges, no code

If Ma'at was right conduct, where was it enforced? Not where we would
look.

UCL's survey of Egyptian law begins with two absences. There were no
judges and no courts in our sense: officials heard cases as part of their
ordinary work, and no one is known whose only job was to judge. And no
legal code survives. Society ran on royal decrees and on precedents from
earlier cases.[^ucl-law]

The nearest thing to a manual is the text called the *Duties of the
Vizier*, copied in the tombs of four viziers at Thebes. It opens with the
vizier at a hearing: seated on a particular stool, a staff at his arm,
thirty rolls laid out before him, his scribes in their places. Petitioners
are heard one after another, in order of rank.[^vizier]

Justice appears here as a procedure in an office. The link to the goddess
was worn on the body. The Met notes that high officials are sometimes
shown with a small figure of Ma'at hung around the neck, to signify that
their conduct is just. Lippert reports that such pendants are well
attested on statues from the twenty-sixth dynasty onward, and that
examples in lapis lazuli have been found.[^figure-met][^lippert-courts]

## The peasant who answered back

One Egyptian story is about what happens when an official fails.

A papyrus in the British Museum, written in the twelfth dynasty and found
at Thebes, preserves the opening of the *Tale of the Eloquent Peasant*.
The museum's curators summarise the plot. A peasant named Khunanup is
tricked and robbed by a greedy landowner who wants his donkeys. He takes
his case to the high steward. The steward is so struck by the peasant's
way with words that he makes him plead again and again, so that the
speeches can be written down and shown to the king. Only after much
suffering is Khunanup given justice.[^peasant]

It is an uncomfortable story. The man in the right is kept waiting for the
entertainment of the men who could help him. Lazaridis singles it out as
a work in which ethical principles, and the institutions tied to them,
such as the judicial system, are questioned. Their authority, he
concludes, was not beyond challenge.[^lazaridis-sources]

Egyptian writers could also imagine the principle failing altogether.
Lazaridis describes a whole genre of laments: vivid pictures of a land in
which every moral rule is trampled and every normal way
reversed.[^lazaridis-sources] To value Ma'at was to know what its absence
would look like.

## The dead person's list

The longest Egyptian list of right and wrong was written for the dead.

Chapter 125 of the Book of the Dead is spoken on arrival at a hall named
for Ma'at in the dual: UCL renders it the broad hall of the Two Goddesses
of What is Right. No written copy is older than the New
Kingdom.[^bd125]

The speaker begins by telling the god of the hall that he has brought him
what is right and removed what is wrong.[^bd125] Those are the two duties
that the kingship text gave to the king. Here they are claimed by an
ordinary dead person.

Then come the denials. In the copy made for a man named Nu, the speaker
has not robbed the orphan, caused hunger, or killed. He has not shortened
the measure or added weight to the pan of the scales. He has not taken
milk from the mouths of children, or held back water in its season. Many
of the rest concern the gods' property: their offerings, their cattle,
their birds and fish.[^bd125]

A second list follows, addressed to forty-two assessors, one denial for
each. The speaker has not stolen, lied, slandered, or been deaf to words
of truth. He has not raised his voice.[^bd125b]

Martin Stadler treats these lists, together with the teachings and the
biographies, as a main source for Egyptian ethical standards. A life lived
by them, he writes, was a life lived according to Ma'at. He adds that the
set of denials stayed remarkably stable for more than fifteen hundred
years.[^stadler]

Where did the form come from? UCL and Stadler both mention a proposal that
it derives from oaths of purity sworn by priests entering temple service.
Stadler is careful: the surviving oaths are of Roman date, and the
borrowing may have gone the other way. UCL notes that affirmations of
righteousness appear on earlier monuments, one of them a twelfth-dynasty
stela from Abydos.[^bd125b][^stadler] Mentuwoser's stela, also from
Abydos, belongs to that older habit.

And how seriously should the list be taken as a guide to daily life?
Lazaridis gives both sides. It was standardised and widely used, which
suggests an established code. But it was used only in a funerary setting,
and may have had a ritual purpose there.[^lazaridis-sources] The first
chapter of this book made a similar point. A funerary text is made to work
for its owner.

## Did Ma'at decline?

One disagreement among scholars should be reported, because it shows how
much depends on which sources one reads.

Jan Assmann has argued that the meaning of Ma'at changed in the later New
Kingdom. On his account, as Teeter summarises it, people came to rely
directly on the will of a god. A good life no longer guaranteed a good
outcome, and Ma'at faded as an ethical standard. He traces the change to
the reign of Akhenaten and its aftermath.[^teeter-debate]

Teeter disagrees. Assmann's evidence is mostly hymns, letters and
teachings. The temple walls, she argues, tell a different story. Scenes of
the king offering Ma'at become more numerous in exactly this period, not
fewer. The king's posture before the god does not grow humbler. And the
new offering of the royal name as Ma'at suggests confidence. She finds
nothing in the reliefs to suggest that Ma'at had ceased to
matter.[^teeter-debate]

Lazaridis stands between them. Piety does seem to become a more important
measure of a person from the New Kingdom on, and later teachings mention
the gods far more often. But Ma'at remained a vital part of religion,
kingship and the sense of right throughout Egyptian history. He warns that
the apparent rise in piety may partly reflect what happens to have been
excavated.[^lazaridis-history]

The question is open. The hymns and the temple walls were made for
different purposes, and each side of the argument rests on one of them.

Look again at the flake of limestone. The Met lists what such sketches
were for: practising a figure, drafting a composition, copying a scene,
and sometimes serving as an image for devotion.[^ostracon] Whichever this
one was, a draftsman drew the king's hand and the goddess sitting on it,
in the form used on the walls of royal tombs and temples.

That is the sense of the word this chapter has followed. In the temple,
the office, the story and the hall of the dead, Ma'at is something to be
done or offered, and each text names the person responsible for doing it.

One instrument has been left for the next chapter. In the hall of the two
Ma'ats stands a balance. A feather lies in one pan, and a heart in the
other.[^stadler] To reach it, the dead must first travel where the sun
goes at night.

[^lippert-courts]: Sandra Lippert, "Law Courts", *UCLA Encyclopedia of Egyptology* (2012), 12, citing Grdseloff (1940) and Möller (1920).

[^peasant]: The British Museum, [Papyrus Butler, EA10274](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/Y_EA10274), description and curator's comments. The papyrus holds forty lines from the introductory part of the tale. The record cites the translation in R. B. Parkinson, *The Tale of Sinuhe and Other Ancient Egyptian Poems* (Oxford World's Classics, 1999), which was not consulted for this chapter; nothing is quoted from the tale.

[^bd125]: UCL, [Book of the Dead, chapter 125A](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/museums-static/digitalegypt/literature/religious/bd125a.html), from the papyrus of Nu, British Museum EA 10477. Paraphrased.

[^bd125b]: UCL, [Book of the Dead, chapter 125B](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/museums-static/digitalegypt/literature/religious/bd125b.html), from the papyrus of Maiherperi, with the introduction on temple oaths and earlier affirmations of righteousness. UCL notes that manuscripts vary in the order and even the number of declarations.

[^stadler]: Martin A. Stadler, "Judgment after Death (Negative Confession)", *UCLA Encyclopedia of Egyptology* (2008), 1 to 3. For the lists as a source for ethics, their stability, the priestly oaths and the uncertainty about which came first, and the balance on which the heart is weighed against a feather.

[^teeter-debate]: Teeter, *The Presentation of Maat*, 3 and 83 to 87. Assmann's position is given here only as Teeter reports it; his own studies, chiefly *Ma'at: Gerechtigkeit und Unsterblichkeit im alten Ägypten* (1990), were not consulted.

[^lazaridis-history]: Lazaridis, "Ethics", 4, section "Historical Development".
