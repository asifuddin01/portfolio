Socrates wrote down nothing. Every word attributed to him comes through someone else, mostly Plato, who had his own agenda and spent decades using his dead teacher as a mouthpiece. Still, the core posture is unmistakable. In fifth-century Athens, while Sophists charged high fees to teach wealthy young men how to win arguments in court, Socrates walked around the market barefoot asking people what they actually meant by words like justice and courage.

He called himself a gadfly stinging a sluggish horse. The horse eventually swatted him.

His method, the elenchus, was simple and irritating. He would ask an expert for a definition, listen politely, point out an obvious contradiction, and keep asking until the person realized they had no idea what they were talking about. This was not a debate trick to score points. It was an attempt to clear away false certainty. His famous claim that he knew nothing was not modesty; it was an epistemological wedge. Most people go through life convinced of ideas they absorbed by osmosis from their culture. Socrates thought that an unexamined life was literally not worth living because you end up living out someone else's ungrounded assumptions.

He argued that virtue is knowledge. If you truly understand what is good, you will do it; nobody does wrong on purpose, only out of ignorance. To modern ears, that sounds naive. We watch people do destructive things while knowing full well they are destructive. But for Socrates, knowing was not academic. It was seeing reality clearly enough that evil stopped looking desirable.

Then Athens got tired of him.

In 399 BC, three citizens named Meletus, Anytus, and Lycon brought formal charges: corrupting the youth and impiety. The religious accusation claimed he ignored the city's gods and invented new ones, pointing to the daimonion, an inner voice he said gave him warning signs. The political charge was the real one. Athens had just lost the Peloponnesian War to Sparta, suffered through the brutal rule of the Thirty Tyrants, and restored its democracy on shaky legs. Several of the worst tyrants, including Critias and the traitorous Alcibiades, had been close students of Socrates. The city was traumatized and insecure. They wanted someone to blame, and an eccentric old man who taught aristocratic boys to question civic authority was an easy target.

At his trial, detailed in Plato's _Apology_, Socrates refused to grovel. When found guilty by a narrow jury vote and asked to propose an alternative punishment to execution, he suggested free meals for life in the Prytaneum, the banquet hall reserved for Olympic heroes. That insolence sealed it. The jury voted by a wider margin to execute him.

His friends bribed the guards and mapped out an escape route to Thessaly. Socrates turned them down. In the _Crito_, he explains his reasoning: he had lived in Athens for seventy years and accepted the benefits of its laws. To run away the moment those laws ruled against him would destroy the principle of the social contract. So, surrounded by his weeping disciples, he took the cup of brewed hemlock and drank it.

Jacques-Louis David painted the scene in 1787, two years before the storming of the Bastille.

The canvas, displayed at the Metropolitan Museum of Art, is a study in Neoclassical restraint. The setting is a cold stone dungeon stripped of decorative clutter, organized around geometric lines and stark lighting.

Socrates sits dead center, upright on his cot like a general. He is seventy, but David gave him the muscular shoulders and chest of an athlete. With his left hand, he points straight up toward the transcendent realm of ideal forms. With his right hand, he reaches blind and steady toward the cup of hemlock held by an executioner who turns his face away in grief. Socrates is not even looking at the cup. He is mid-sentence, lecturing his followers until the last second.

Around him, everyone else has broken down.

Crito sits on a low stool beside the bed, his hand clamped onto Socrates' thigh, staring up at his master with desperate focus. Further back on a stone bench, disciples bury their faces in their cloaks or lean against the wall in despair.

The most telling figure in the room is Plato, slumped at the foot of the bed.

Plato sits facing away from the cot, eyes downcast, silent, with a scroll and pen resting on the floor. Historically, Plato was not even there; the dialogue _Phaedo_ notes that he was ill that day. David placed him in the cell anyway, not as an eyewitness, but as the author constructing the memory. He is painted as a frail, gray-haired man, though Plato was only about twenty-eight when his teacher died. David chose to show him at the age he was when he wrote the dialogues, dreaming the scene into permanence.

Near the back of the chamber, climbing the stone stairs toward the light, Socrates' wife Xanthippe is escorted out after a final wave goodbye.

David had contemporary France on his mind. In 1787, the French monarchy was rotting and Paris was full of seditious talk. By painting Socrates as a thinker who chose death over bowing to a corrupt court, David turned an ancient execution into a rallying cry for civic defiance.
