If Plato was a mystic with a geometry habit, Aristotle was a field biologist who wanted to catalog the world.

He came from Stagira in northern Greece, the son of a physician to the king of Macedon. He arrived at Plato's Academy at seventeen and spent twenty years studying and arguing before breaking away from his teacher's metaphysics. When Raphael painted the two of them centuries later in _The School of Athens_, he caught the entire split in their hand gestures: Plato points upward toward the sky of ideal forms, while Aristotle holds his palm flat toward the earth.

Aristotle's fundamental move was pulling the Forms down to ground level.

Plato thought a physical horse was a flawed shadow of a celestial blueprint existing outside time and space. Aristotle found that absurd. For him, forms do not float around in an immaterial realm. They exist inside physical things. A statue is bronze shaped into a specific figure; you cannot separate the bronze (matter) from its shape (form). This pairing of matter and form is called hylomorphism. When a horse dies, its form dissolves. There is no disembodied realm waiting for it.

Because reality is here on earth, Aristotle studied how things actually behave.

He was obsessed with purpose (_telos_). Everything in nature is moving from potential to actuality. An acorn is not just a hard little nut; it is a potential oak tree striving to fulfill its growth.

To explain why anything exists or changes, he created the doctrine of the Four Causes. Take a marble statue of Athena:

The material cause is the physical marble block.

The formal cause is the shape of Athena.

The efficient cause is the sculptor swinging the chisel.

The final cause is the purpose of the statue, honoring the goddess in a temple.

Modern science threw out the final cause centuries ago. But for Aristotle, nature is teleological from top to bottom. If you do not know the purpose of a thing, you do not understand it.

He brought that same practical framework to human behavior.

In the _Nicomachean Ethics_, Aristotle defined the goal of human life as _eudaimonia_, usually translated as happiness, though flourishing or functioning well is closer. Eudaimonia is an active life lived according to reason, not a fleeting emotional high.

Virtue is a habit built through practice, like playing the harp. You become brave by doing brave deeds until courage becomes second nature. Aristotle mapped virtue as a Golden Mean between two extremes. Courage sits between cowardice (too little confidence) and recklessness (too much). Generosity sits between stinginess and wasteful extravagance. Finding the mean is not a mathematical formula. It requires practical wisdom (_phronesis_) applied to messy, specific situations.

Humans cannot achieve this flourishing in isolation. Aristotle called humans political animals by nature (_zoon politikon_). The state exists to cultivate virtue, going far beyond basic security or trade agreements.

Beyond ethics and biology, Aristotle invented formal logic. His primary tool was the syllogism, a structured deduction where two true premises force a conclusion:

All humans are mortal.

Socrates is human.

Therefore, Socrates is mortal.

For two thousand years, Western logic barely moved past his collection of logical texts, known as the _Organon_.

Aristotelianism had a strange second life after Rome fell.

Europe lost most of his original Greek texts during the early Middle Ages. They survived in the Islamic world, where scholars like Ibn Rushd (Averroes) wrote extensive commentaries and kept his philosophy alive. In the twelfth and thirteenth centuries, Latin translations flooded back into European universities. Thomas Aquinas took Aristotle's framework and welded it to Catholic theology, arguing that reason and revelation are complementary paths to truth. To the medieval church, Aristotle was no longer an ancient Greek pagan. He was simply "The Philosopher."
